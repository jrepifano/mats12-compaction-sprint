The old garden sits behind a low stone wall at the edge of the village, where the lane bends toward the river. In spring the beds along the path fill with tulips and narcissus, and the gardener spends his mornings pulling weeds from between the bricks. A wooden bench stands under the apple tree, its paint faded to a soft grey by years of rain and sun. Visitors often stop there to rest, listening to the bees moving through the blossom and the distant sound of water over the weir. Beyond the bench, a gravel walk leads past the herb beds, where thyme, sage and rosemary grow in loose clumps that spill over the edging. The smell is strongest in the early afternoon, when the sun has warmed the leaves. Near the greenhouse there is a small pond, ringed with irises and ferns, and on still days the surface reflects the clouds so clearly that it is hard to tell where the sky ends. Frogs sing there in the evening, and swallows skim the water for insects as the light fades. The greenhouse itself is crowded with seedlings in clay pots, tomatoes climbing their canes, and a vine that has grown across the whole of the roof. In summer the doors are left open, and the warm air carries the scent of soil and green leaves out into the garden. Along the far wall, espaliered pears are trained flat against the stone, their branches tied in neat horizontal lines. The gardener prunes them in winter, when the frost has settled on the lawn and the beds lie bare under a layer of compost. Autumn brings its own colours: the maple by the gate turns a deep red, and the hedges are hung with berries that the blackbirds strip within days. Leaves gather in drifts against the wall, and children from the village come to kick through them on their way home from school. On cold mornings mist rises from the river and drifts across the lawn, softening the outlines of the trees until the garden seems to float. Then the sun climbs over the roofs, the mist thins, and the paths and beds return, wet and shining, as the day begins again. By noon the gardener is back among the beds with his barrow and his hoe, stopping now and then to talk with a neighbour over the wall about the weather, the state of the river, and which of the roses will flower first this year.
