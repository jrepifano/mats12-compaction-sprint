                                                     When “Must” Becomes “Maybe”:
                                         Erosion of Workflow Constraints in LLM Agent Handoffs
                                                  Yiheng Sun       Huifei Wang        Yancheng Zhu Zhenyu Li              Zebin Zhao       Yifan Yuan
                                                                                        Shenzhen University


                                                                                               Abstract
                                         Large language model (LLM) agents build on one another’s work through summaries, plans, and other intermediate
                                         artifacts. These records let a downstream agent continue without revisiting the complete upstream context. We
                                         uncover a hidden loss in this process: handoffs can carry tasks forward while eroding judgments already established
                                         upstream. A requirement that must be satisfied before action becomes a caveat that the next agent may acknowledge

                                         and set aside. The issue remains visible, but its role has changed. We define operational state preservation as
                                         retaining an established judgment’s role in constraining downstream action. Safety blockers provide a concrete
                                         instance: an unresolved prerequisite restricts the admissible actions until the condition is resolved. In our experiments,
                                         we confirm correct upstream detection, hold source permissions fixed, and vary the handoff supplied to a local-
                                         view executor. Direct-state controls preserve every blocker, whereas compression, plan assimilation, convergence,
                                         ownership deferral, and precedent substitution repeatedly weaken them. Ownership deferral produces 76.7% blocker
                                         deactivation; multihop compression produces 97.2%. Measuring the transmitted state separately from endpoint action
                                         exposes two routes to behavioral recovery. In fixed-artifact replays, restoring the full blocker description raises
                                         recognition from 6.2% to 99.2% and reduces forbidden action from 85.2% to 0.0%. An authorization-check prompt
                                         instead reduces forbidden action to 1.6% while leaving artifact deactivation at 95.3%. A successful endpoint can
                                         therefore conceal a damaged handoff. These findings make maintaining earlier judgments a core capability of reliable
                                         collaboration: each stage must carry forward both what earlier work established and what it requires of subsequent
                                         action. Semantic availability does not guarantee operational preservation.
                                         Keywords: LLM agents; multi-agent systems; agent handoff; agent orchestration; AI safety.

select, summarize, and reorganize information for new roles, they determine which earlier findings remain
effective premises. Recasting a requirement as advice changes that basis without resolving the underlying
condition. Maintaining established judgments is therefore a core capability of reliable collaboration.
   We distinguish semantic availability from operational preservation: whether information remains
available versus whether it retains its role in directing action. An executor can recognize that approval
is missing and still treat that fact as non-blocking. Recovering the issue and inheriting the requirement
are different achievements. Factual-consistency research tests whether generated text is supported by its
source (Maynez et al., 2020; Kryscinski et al., 2020). For coordination, preserving a finding also means
preserving what it requires of the next agent:

                             semantic availability ̸⇒ operational preservation.                             (1)

   Safety blockers make this loss observable: their validity and action consequences can be fixed before
handoff. We define a binding workflow state as an established condition that restricts the admissible
action set until it is resolved. Blockers express this state through a prerequisite, responsible authority, stop
condition, and fallback. Whereas constraint-drift research considers maintenance across agent trajectories
(Li et al., 2026), we examine the transition through which an established judgment becomes another
agent’s working context (Figure 2).
   To trace the loss, we confirm correct full-context detection and vary the handoff while keeping source
permissions fixed. The judgment is already available upstream, and its action consequences still hold
when the executor receives the artifact. Changes in the inherited account can therefore be compared
against an unchanged basis for action. Fixed-artifact authorization tests whether the executor can avoid
forbidden action while the loss remains; restoration tests whether repairing the transmitted state recovers
its effect on the executor. Together, these comparisons reveal what coordination loses, what the final
action can conceal, and what repair restores.
   The analysis establishes three findings:
    1. Handoffs can erode judgments already established upstream. Five coordination transformations
       repeatedly weaken correctly identified constraints. This information loss extends beyond omitted
       details: the relevant issue can remain visible as the earlier judgment loses its force.

    2. An admissible endpoint can conceal this loss. Authorization checks sharply reduce forbidden
       action while leaving the same artifacts degraded. The final action can hide what coordination failed
       to preserve, exposing a blind spot in endpoint evaluation.
    3. Repair restores the judgment’s force. Full-state restoration raises blocker recognition from 6.2%
       to 99.2% and reduces forbidden action from 85.2% to 0.0%. Repair improves the record on which
       subsequent work depends, beyond containing a single endpoint.


2    Related Work
Communication, common ground, and factual fidelity. Discourse and information-state models treat
language as an update to structured context (Grosz and Sidner, 1986; Traum and Larsson, 2003); grounding
and collaborative-plan research connects communication to shared commitments and responsibility (Clark
and Brennan, 1991; Grosz and Kraus, 1996). Summarization research asks whether generated accounts
remain faithful to source facts (Maynez et al., 2020; Kryscinski et al., 2020). We connect these concerns
at an execution interface: an unresolved condition can remain in the shared account while losing its role
as a prerequisite. Explicit permissions and action labels make that change measurable.
Artifacts and state in agent workflows. AutoGen, CAMEL, and AgentVerse distribute work through
conversations and roles (Wu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023); ChatDev and MetaGPT use
intermediate documents to connect development roles (Qian et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024). StateFlow
organizes work into states and transitions, while AFlow searches workflow graphs (Wu et al., 2024; Zhang
et al., 2024). These architectures make one stage’s representation another’s input. We examine what
survives that interface, evaluating the transmitted constraints alongside the recipient’s behavior at the next
workflow stage.

     maintenance to these endpoint concerns: it distinguishes a change in the transmitted state from a policy
     that contains its effects. Fixed-artifact comparisons reveal this distinction.

      3      Operational State in a Handoff
      3.1       Tasks, Roles, and Information Access
      Each task describes an enterprise request, a policy or permission boundary, an unresolved blocker, and
      a finite set of candidate actions. For example, a request to share a resource may lack approval for that
      resource. The source record specifies who can resolve the gap, which actions remain forbidden, and which
      fallback is admissible. These facts are fixed before any handoff is generated. The tasks use synthetic
      resources and abstract action labels.
         An episode has three stages: a full-context reviewer identifies the blocker; a condition-specific generator
      produces a structured handoff with text, a recommended action, and status fields; and a separate executor
      call selects an action. Handoffs take forms such as summaries, plans, or notes. Trajectory conditions


                                                                           3
generate a simulated stage sequence and final artifact within one response. Review, handoff, and action
are recorded separately.
   The executor receives the artifact, task goal, visible notes and pressure context, candidate actions, tool
schema, and condition instructions, but not the full policy record or hidden approval state. Artifact-only
denotes this restriction on source-policy access. The baseline policy treats the artifact as authoritative:
an explicit blocker or approval-checking duty calls for an admissible fallback, while caveats remain
non-blocking.
3.2   Established State and Its Representation
A binding state is established when its validity and action consequence are fixed in the source record.
Reviewer detection is a separate event: recognition of that already-valid condition. Review makes
the source restriction explicit; the handoff carries it into the executor’s local view. We compare these
representations while the source state remains fixed.
   In the sharing example, the state links a missing approval to a restriction on sending, an authority able
to grant approval, and an admissible alternative while approval is absent. A shorter record can preserve
those relationships without repeating all supporting context. A record that retains the approval topic
but disconnects it from the restriction changes the basis on which the next agent acts. The four-field
representation below makes these execution-relevant relationships explicit.
   We represent a blocker’s source state as

                                              z = (s, q, o, f ),                                          (2)

where s is stop status, q the unresolved prerequisite, o the responsible authority, and f an admissible
fallback. These fields express the restriction, its release condition, responsibility for resolution, and the
next permissible alternative. Let x denote the task and upstream review available to the generator, and c
its coordination context, such as an existing plan or repeated low-risk framing. A handoff transformation
produces hτ = τ (x, c); we compare its expressed state with the fixed source state z.
3.3   Artifact State and Endpoint Action
The handoff expresses a judgment that can guide subsequent work; the endpoint records one executor’s
response to that representation.
   Operational preservation asks whether the handoff keeps the restricted action conditional on resolving
the blocker. Its indicator P (h, z) combines artifact-supplied status judgments with deterministic text-field
extraction. Deactivation D(h, z) records weakening or loss when P = 0: caveat framing, deferral,
omission, or missing action-relevant fields. Thus both content loss and changes in operational role
contribute to D. The residual U = 1 − P − D records cases satisfying neither rule (Appendix A).
   The four-field profile R(h, z) = (rs , rq , ro , rf ) records which parts of the source state remain repre-
sented. Its conjunction defines completeness:
                                                          Y

The fixed-artifact probes compute C by deterministic extraction. Completeness asks which fields are
recoverable; preservation asks whether they express an active restriction.
   The endpoint measure Y (h; v) = 1 records selection of an action forbidden by the source task under
execution policy v. The executor acts from its local view; the evaluator uses canonical permissions. A
non-forbidden action can be a fallback, an approval request, or an admissible continuation.

4     Experimental Setting
4.1   Handoff Conditions and Controlled Comparisons
The primary matrix contains 1,296 task–condition–model–seed episodes across six model configurations,
all with correct full-context detection and retained regardless of downstream outcome. Static arms contain
120 episodes; simulated-trajectory arms contain 72. Direct-state controls carry the canonical blocker
forward. Compression, plan assimilation, convergence, ownership deferral, and precedent substitution are
matched by source task and model, with permissions and action labels fixed (Appendix Table 3).

artifact per arm; fixed-artifact insertion and authorization replay the same 64 base artifacts through two

model configurations. Appendix Table 2 maps these comparisons.

4.3   Measurement and Validation
The primary artifact checker combines generator-supplied judgments and deterministic extraction. A blind
panel of five model judges evaluates 240 artifacts with condition identity, checker labels, and endpoint
outcomes hidden. This panel tests whether the artifact distinction is recovered by a separate judgment
procedure. Leave-one-domain and leave-one-model analyses examine the concentration of the main
contrasts.
   Fixed-artifact insertion sets P to one exactly when all four fields are inserted. Repaired P therefore
describes the constructed representation; executor recognition and action measure its observed effects. C
is recomputed from the artifact. Appendix A gives the insertion, scoring, and audit procedures.
   Endpoint rates use normalized action requests, substituting the task fallback when requested and
recommended actions are both absent. Fixed-artifact arms retain two flagged executions each; primary
and model-validation panels have no recorded parse-error episodes. Appendix A reports parsing and
denominator rules.

5     Established Constraints Weaken During Handoff
Correct recognition upstream does not protect a constraint during handoff. Direct-state controls retain
every blocker and produce no forbidden actions; transformations lower preservation and raise deactivation
across all five families (Figure 3; complete matrix in Appendix Table 4). Source permissions remain
unchanged. The difference lies in the representation carried into local execution.
   Compression removes status, ownership, or fallback information. Plan assimilation recasts the blocker
as a modification to ongoing work. Illustrative transitions turn an authorization gap into a discretionary
check and an expired exception into a precedent for continuing (Appendix B.1). A condition on action
becomes material for deciding how to proceed. Deactivation includes these role changes as well as content
loss.
   The effects are large. Ownership deferral produces 76.7% deactivation and 60.8% forbidden action,
corresponding to increases of 76.7 points (task-cluster 95% interval: 70.0–82.3) and 60.8 points (56.0–
65.7) over its control. Multihop compression produces 97.2% deactivation (93.3–100.0) and 31.9%
forbidden action (25.0–37.5). Plan assimilation and repeated low-risk framing likewise yield substantial
deactivation, at 65.3% and 76.4%, with forbidden-action rates of 29.2% and 33.3%.
   The ordering of these effects matters. Multihop compression deactivates more artifacts than ownership
deferral, yet produces roughly half its forbidden-action rate. Schema-preserving compression also yields
27.8% deactivation with no forbidden actions. Artifact degradation therefore does not order endpoint
risk. The action reflects both the constraints expressed by the handoff and the policy that consumes them.

6          When Handoff Loss Becomes Visible
6.1                  Degradation Can Precede a Forbidden Action
The compression panel traces degradation within one transformation family. Across five procedures with
48 episodes per arm, direct state produces no deactivation or forbidden action. Light summarization
already produces 25.0% deactivation while forbidden action remains at 0.0%. Normal handoff reaches
100.0% deactivation and 54.2% forbidden action. Under compressed and aggressive multihop procedures,
deactivation stays at 100.0% while forbidden action reaches 83.3% and 79.2% (Figure 4).
   Degradation appears before endpoint failure in the light-summary arm, and the artifact score saturates
while action rates continue to differ. These procedures jointly vary hop count, framing, and schema;
across them, an admissible endpoint can leave loss of binding state invisible.

6.2                  Restoring State Changes the Executor’s Response
Reintroducing canonical state information changes how the executor acts on a degraded handoff. In the
source-matched component-restoration panel, no repair yields 81.2% forbidden action. Adding the stop
label reduces it to 0.0%, whereas adding the prerequisite alone leaves it at 73.4%. All-field repair also
yields 0.0%. Each arm contains 64 source-matched episodes with a fresh artifact, so comparisons capture
responses to each repair package across matched source configurations.
   The fixed-artifact comparison holds the base handoffs constant. It replays the same 64 artifacts through
two executor routes after all-field insertion or insertion with one field omitted (Table 1). Full insertion
raises blocker recognition from 6.2% to 99.2% and reduces forbidden action from 85.2% to 0.0%.
Omitting the stop label yields 40.6% recognition and 7.0% forbidden action. Omitting the prerequisite,
owner, or fallback yields 90.6–92.2% recognition and no forbidden actions.
   Full-field repair recovers the complete source-state description and near-universal blocker recognition.
Partial repairs separate completeness from immediate behavior: several yield admissible actions despite
incomplete state, while omitting the stop label sharply reduces recognition. Existing mentions remain
in every omission arm, accounting for nonzero completeness. Equal numbers of inserted fields can

6.3         Authorization Contains Action Without Repairing State
An executor can also avoid forbidden action while the handoff stays degraded. The authorization
comparison holds the same 64 base artifacts fixed and changes the instructions or permission information
available at execution. A soft visible-authorization check reduces forbidden action from 85.2% to 1.6%.
A strict authorization prompt, a supplied synthetic permission lookup, and a prompt requiring a request
for human approval each yield 0.0%. These conditions vary model instructions and visible information;
the approval condition measures whether the executor requests approval.
   The unchanged artifact retains 4.7% preservation, 95.3% deactivation, and 10.9% completeness.
Behavioral improvement occurs downstream of the degraded state, separating repair of the handoff from
changes in how it is consumed. A safe action can be the success of containment while state transmission
remains impaired.

7   Robustness and Measurement Checks
The handoff-level pattern persists across task and model slices. In leave-one-domain analyses, the pooled
transformation–control deactivation gap remains between 50.0 and 51.7 points, and the forbidden-action
gap between 27.5 and 29.0 points. Leave-one-model analyses vary more widely in magnitude while
retaining a positive artifact contrast. Additional model configurations, a paraphrase panel, and native
AutoGen and LangGraph implementations examine the same stage-separated abstraction under different
models, wording, and runtimes (Appendix F).
   Blind adjudication also recovers the artifact distinction. Five model judges evaluate 240 artifacts span-
ning compression, restoration, paraphrase, and framework conditions. Their majority marks deactivation
in 1.61% of control/preserved or schema-preserved strata and 90.45% of transformed, intermediate, or
partial-repair strata, an 88.84-point gap. Leave-one-judge-out gaps range from 88.27 to 88.84 points.
Overall actionability has 94.83% pairwise judge agreement (κ = 0.887) and 93.19% agreement between
the majority and checker (macro F1 0.916).
   Ownership is more sensitive to the coding procedure. Judges agree with one another at 92.70%, while
their majority agrees with the checker at 65.25% (macro F1 0.636). This difference locates a measurement
issue in the operational coding of ownership despite substantial agreement among judges. The overall
deactivation contrast is more stable than this component-specific measurement. Appendices C–E report
the contrasts, sensitivity analyses, and audit population.
   Together, the comparisons trace a loss between establishing a judgment and using it. Transformations
weaken an already-valid constraint; authorization can suppress the behavioral consequences of this loss
without repairing the handoff; restoring the source state improves recognition and restores admissible
behavior. The inherited record and the executor’s response are distinct targets for improving coordination.

8   Discussion
From task progress to cumulative work. Interaction can erode judgments that a workflow has already
established. Maintaining those judgments is therefore a capability of the collaborative system itself. A
finding contributes to cumulative work when later stages can continue to use it as a warranted premise.
Grounding research likewise treats common ground as something participants establish and update
throughout communication (Clark and Brennan, 1991). In agent workflows, this maintenance must extend
across changes of role, context, and representation. Correct recognition makes the finding explicit; the
rest of the workflow determines whether it remains effective. Improving an individual agent’s reasoning
addresses how judgments are reached. Maintaining them requires attention to how agents interact, detect
loss, and recover earlier findings. The design question is how a system detects and repairs losses before
later work adopts a weakened account.
Flexible language, stable relationships. Natural language lets agents summarize, reinterpret, and
organize earlier work for a new role. This flexibility makes the relationship between a finding and its
use a central preservation target. A successor needs to inherit what supports a judgment, when it holds,
and what follows from it. In our blocker tasks, the unresolved prerequisite must remain connected to
the restriction on action. Retaining the topic alone leaves this relationship vulnerable to change. More
generally, a summary may preserve a conclusion while dropping a condition on which it depends. A useful
record must carry enough of that relationship for later work to proceed on the same basis. This suggests
evaluating transformations by the judgments and decisions they support, alongside the content they retain.
The crucial test is whether a successor can use the finding, rather than merely repeat it. Wording and
detail can change without requiring findings to be established again.
Continuity through justified revision. Established judgments also need to change when new evidence,
authorization, or corrected reasoning provides grounds for revision. Reliable coordination must support
both inheritance and revision: preserve a judgment while its grounds hold, and update it when those
grounds change. Our fixed-permission comparisons expose drift introduced through communication
without new authorization. Dynamic workflows raise the complementary question of whether an agent
can recognize a warranted revision. A useful evaluation would pair unchanged source conditions with
conditions that genuinely warrant an update, then test whether the transmitted judgment remains stable
or changes accordingly. This would reward responsiveness to reasons rather than resistance to change.


                                                     8
Recording what changed in a judgment together with what warrants that change offers a way to connect
preservation, correction, and adaptation within the same workflow.
A reliable record outlasts a successful response. Repair changes the record inherited by downstream
agents; endpoint containment changes how one agent responds to it. A consumer’s safe fallback does
not supply missing premises to its successors. This distinction matters whenever a handoff is reused for
planning, memory, execution, or audit: the effects of an intervention depend on whether it improves a
shared record or compensates for that record at one decision. Reusing artifacts across different consumers
would test whether recovery travels with the transmitted state or depends on a particular executor. Longer
workflows add a further challenge. Branching and recombination can expose different roles to different
versions of an earlier judgment. Tracking its grounds and consequences through these transitions would
test whether repaired state remains a common basis for continued work. The goal is to make the gains of
one stage remain usable throughout collaboration.

9   Conclusion
Natural-language coordination can carry work forward while eroding judgments already established
upstream. Across controlled blocker tasks, handoffs turn requirements into caveats or discretionary checks
even when the relevant issue remains visible. Correct upstream detection and fixed source permissions
locate this loss in how established state is carried forward. Authorization controls can recover admissible
behavior while leaving the record degraded; restoration improves the transmitted record and the executor’s
response. These findings make maintaining earlier judgments a core capability of reliable collaboration.
Correct recognition and an admissible final action leave open whether earlier findings remain effective
premises for subsequent work. Workflows must preserve that continuity as agents reorganize information,
revise judgments, and act on one another’s outputs. The broader task is to keep earlier work usable
across transformations, preserving what it established and what it requires of subsequent action. Semantic
availability does not guarantee operational preservation.

Limitations
We isolate state transmission in synthetic enterprise tasks with fixed permissions and finite action choices.
Evaluating deployed workflows would extend the analysis to evolving permissions, tool feedback, and
repeated interaction.
  The four-field representation captures established blockers. Other workflow states, including commit-
ments and unresolved dependencies, require representations that capture their own action consequences.
Extending the task family and its measurements is the next step toward studying operational preservation
across a broader range of agent workflows.

Ethical considerations
All experiments use synthetic enterprise scenarios and simulated action requests, without human partici-
pants, live-resource operations, or private enterprise data.
