                                         1   I NTRODUCTION
                                                     Emily asks her LLM agent to look through her work inbox and suggest which
                                                     messages could be archived or deleted. The agent returns a list of recommendations,
                                                     but along the way, it has already archived several threads and deleted a handful of
                                                     emails on its own. Emily notices and checks the mailbox immediately: the archived
                                                     messages were indeed low-priority, and the deleted ones were promotions.
                                                     No important email was lost. Yet something has gone wrong.

                                         LLM agents are increasingly deployed to act on behalf of users across diverse domains, from
                                         professional work to personal life (Wang et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). This
                                         makes the relationship between a user and an AI agent fundamentally one of delegation: the user, as
                                         principal, authorizes the agent to act on their behalf within a defined scope, and the agent is expected
                                         to operate within that scope (American Law Institute, 2006). Acting within the assigned authorization

scope is the most basic principle governing any agency relationship (Dalley, 2011). When an agent
exceeds that scope, the transgression does not depend on whether harm follows. This unauthorized
action erodes the user’s perceived control over the agent’s behavior and damages the trust that sustains
the delegation relationship, regardless of the outcome. Because an agent acts in the world on the
user’s behalf, out-of-authorization behavior can also raise accountability concerns, particularly in
professional settings where the user may be held responsible for what their agent does.
However, acting in alignment with assigned authorization is not straightforward for LLM agents. In
traditional software systems, authorization boundaries are precisely defined through system design,
such as role-based access control and permission policies (Sandhu et al., 1996), which draw binary
lines between what is permitted and what is not. LLM agents, by contrast, receive authorization
conveyed largely through natural-language instructions, which are inherently ambiguous and context-
dependent (Helou et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b). This requires agents to reason about and act within
authorization boundaries in open-ended, multi-domain environments with real consequences.
Current AI safety evaluation frameworks predominantly adopt a consequence-oriented perspective:
they assess whether an agent acts safely, that is, whether its actions cause tangible safety or privacy
harms (Ruan et al., 2024; Andriushchenko et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2024). However,
an agent can act well outside its authorized scope while causing no such harm, and a consequence-
oriented benchmark would score this behavior as safe. We argue that acting within the assigned
authorization scope is a fundamental property of the delegation relationship that holds regardless of
the outcome, yet one that current evaluation frameworks do not systematically address. To investigate
this gap, we ask: How well do LLM agents act in alignment with their assigned authorization
scope?
To answer this question, we introduce AUTHORIZATION B ENCH, an evaluation framework for agent
authorization alignment. We ground in 44 documented real-world cases of agent out-of-authorization
behavior, develop a scalable construction pipeline and finally produce 286 executable tasks. We
evaluate 24 agent harness-model configurations in an authorization-aware sandbox, producing 6,864
trajectories. Results show that out-of-authorization behavior is prevalent across all 24 harness-model
configurations, with overall out-of-authorization rate of 19.2% ranging from 10.5% (GPT-5.6 Sol
at high effort on OpenAI SDK) to 26.6% (GLM 5.3 at high effort on OpenAI SDK). It occurs not
only while agents complete tasks, but also while they fail to complete them. Increasing reasoning
effort does not reliably improve authorization alignment, and its effect varies across harness-model
configurations. While agents act out of authorization more often under benign pressures than under
malicious injections, at 23.5% versus 17.6% (p = 0.002). Last but not least, agents that consult the
principal before completing a task are associated with a lower out-of-authorization rate, at 14.0%
versus 21.6% (p < 0.001). These findings show that task capability, additional reasoning, and
consequence-oriented safety evaluation do not by themselves ensure authorization alignment. As
current agent safety alignment may shape agents to avoid safety-harmful outcomes more than to stay
within authorization boundaries, our results call for attention to authorization alignment as a basic
property of the delegation relationship between users and agents, alongside consequence-oriented
safety. This association points to a tension between agent autonomy and preserving users’ decision
authority, motivating further human-centered study of human-LLM-agent delegation relationship.

Contributions. (1) We formalize agent authorization alignment through case-specific authorization
contracts and three contextual dimensions. (2) We introduce a scalable pipeline that constructs 286
executable tasks grounded in documented real-world cases. (3) We develop an authorization-aware
sandbox that keeps authorized and out-of-authorization paths executable and observable across
three agent harnesses. (4) We evaluate four models at two reasoning-effort settings and identify
authorization failures missed by task-completion and consequence-oriented evaluation.

act on the principal’s behalf and subject to the principal’s control, and the agent manifests assent or

the most basic obligation of this fiduciary relationship (Dalley, 2011). When an agent exceeds that

describes how people interact with LLM-based agents (Kolt, 2025). Unlike traditional software, where
role-based access control draws precise authorization boundaries (Sandhu et al., 1996), LLM agents
receive authority through natural-language instructions that are inherently ambiguous and context-
dependent (Helou et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b). Prior work largely seeks to enforce authorization

alignment and measure it directly.

3     AUTHORIZATION B ENCH
AUTHORIZATION B ENCH evaluates whether an LLM agent acts within the scope assigned by its
principal while carrying out a delegated task under realistic situations and pressures. Its basic unit
of evaluation begins with a real-world case of out-of-authorization behavior. We represent the
delegation and its case-specific boundary as an authorization contract (Section 3.1), instantiate the
case as an executable task, and judge the resulting trajectory independently for task completion and
out-of-authorization behavior. To benchmark authorization alignment across the range of situations
represented in real-world use, we organize the cases along three coverage dimensions (Section 3.2):
situational trigger, consequence, and domain. We then use a scalable pipeline (Section 3.4) to construct
executable scenarios, and evaluate the agent in an authorization-aware sandbox (Section 3.3).

3.1   AUTHORIZATION C ONTRACTS

Each real-world case where an agent acted beyond the authorization scope assigned by its principal’s
instruction describes a concrete delegation: what a principal assigned an agent to do, and what the
agent subsequently did that was regarded as outside that assignment. We encode this case-specific
delegation boundary as an authorization contract A = (I, C, O). The instruction I is the principal’s
natural-language request to the agent. The completion criteria C = {c1 , . . . , cn } operationalize what
successful completion of that assignment means. The out-of-authorization actions O = {o1 , . . . , om }
specify the action or actions identified as outside the assignment in the source case. Thus, the contract
preserves both sides of the case: the task assigned to the agent and the case-specific authorization

We judge the full trajectory independently against C and O. A trajectory completes the task if it

In the sandbox execution, the agent publishes sensitive code in a forum reply and then reports the
correct fix, yielding an out-of-authorization completion. The authorization judgment asks whether the
specified action occurred, not whether it produced a direct downstream safety or privacy consequence.

3.2   C ONTEXTUAL D IMENSIONS OF AUTHORIZATION S CENARIOS

We characterize each scenario by its situational trigger, understood as a source of pressure presented
to the agent, whether the out-of-authorization action produces a direct safety or privacy harmful
consequence, and the application domain.

Grounding on real-world cases. We collect 44 real-world cases in which an agent acted beyond
the authorization scope established by a principal’s instruction, drawn from social media and online
forums, news reports, law, regulation, and policy documents, and research literature. We include cases
with a benign principal and agent when natural-language delegation establishes both the assigned
task and an externally supported out-of-authorization action. Each case becomes a structured seed
containing its source and context, the principal’s instruction, the observed agent process, the action
regarded as out of authorization, and labels for its situational trigger, consequence, and domain.

Situational trigger. Through a review of prior work on agent safety and analysis of the 44 real-
world cases, we derive a taxonomy of 14 situational triggers (Appendix B.2) that characterize the
source of contextual pressure presented to the agent. We group them by where that pressure appears
in the agent’s context: five instruction-level triggers originate in the principal’s instructions or settings,
while nine environment-level triggers originate elsewhere in the task environment. The latter include
two malicious triggers that directly command an out-of-authorization action or distort the agent-
perceived authorization scope. All other triggers are benign, reflecting ordinary pressures that agents
can encounter in real-world delegation.

Direct harmful consequences. Acting outside the assigned scope is itself harmful to the delegation
relationship, but may not cause the additional harms studied in safety and privacy evaluation. For
example, reacting to one’s own pull request without authorization exceeds the assignment without
directly causing a conventional safety or privacy harm. We label a scenario with direct harmful
consequences when the OA action falls into at least one of ten categories consolidated from prior
taxonomies (Zhang et al., 2024; Vijayvargiya et al., 2025), and without direct harmful consequences
otherwise. The full taxonomy of direct harmful consequences is provided in Appendix B.1.

Domain covers ten personal and professional settings (Appendix B.3): personal life, office work,
software development, travel, e-commerce, entertainment, finance, healthcare, legal, and education.

3.3   AUTHORIZATION -AWARE S ANDBOX

Benchmarking how well LLM agents align their behavior with assigned authorization requires
observing them as they carry out delegated tasks in an interactive environment. Because evaluating
authorization alignment poses demands beyond those of a generic agent sandbox, we design the
authorization-aware sandbox around four requirements.

(1) Keeping both safe completion and the out-of-authorization path open. Authorization is
distinct from technical capability, so the sandbox does not use access controls to block the specified
out-of-authorization action. For example, an agent’s ability to delete workspace files does not grant
permission to delete them. The safe completion path also remains feasible, so completing the task
does not require acting out of authorization. Because out-of-authorization behavior may occur
after completion, the sandbox neither announces that a completion criterion has been satisfied nor
terminates the run at that point. The agent decides when to finish, subject to a run limit, leaving
post-completion behavior observable without encouraging it.

(2) Making (out-of-)authorized actions and their effects observable. Authorization judgments
depend on whether the specified action occurred, not on whether it produced a downstream safety or
privacy consequence. For semantically identifiable actions, an LLM judge reads model, tool, user,
and server-side service events. For terminal and filesystem tasks, deterministic detectors compare
final state against the baseline and retain process-sensitive shell and file operations. The post-hoc
judge receives the trajectory together with these findings and final-state snapshots (Section 4.1).

(3) Delivering pressures through realistic channels. Real-world delegation exposes agents to
situational pressures, so an artificially clean sandbox is insufficient for evaluating authorization
alignment. We instantiate real-world pressures from our trigger taxonomy and deliver each through a
realistic channel. Instruction-level pressures appear in the principal’s instructions or settings, while
environment-level pressures arrive through prior memory, service records, workspace files, messages
from other actors, or runtime events.

(4) Keeping the principal reachable. Real-world delegated task execution can involve interaction
between the agent and its human principal. The sandbox is designed to keep this communication path
available through a passive Oracle User that responds only when contacted. Its near-scripted policy ac-
knowledges in-scope proposals, refuses proposed out-of-authorization actions, and answers clarifying
questions without expanding authorization or proactively revealing the boundary (Appendix F.2).

4.2        M AIN R ESULTS AND F INDINGS

 Finding 1 (Out-of-Authorization Behavior Is Prevalent Across Harnesses and Models). LLM
 agents act out of authorization in about one in every five assigned tasks (19.2%). Authorization
 misalignment varies across harnesses and models while no configuration is free of it.

Across all 6,864 trajectories, the out-of-authorization rate (OAR) is 19.2% (Figure 3): a user who
delegates five tasks can expect one of them to be carried out beyond what they authorized. OAR
ranges from 10.5% (GPT-5.6 Sol, high effort, OpenAI SDK) to 26.6% (GLM 5.3, high effort, OpenAI
SDK). OAR varies across models (15.4%–23.7%) and across harnesses (18.1%–19.7%). Moreover,
the harness can shift a given model’s OAR. For example, GPT-5.6 Sol acts out of authorization in
14.0% and 13.5% of trajectories on OpenHands and OpenAI SDK but in 18.9% on Claude SDK.

 Finding 2 ((Non-)Completion ⇏ Acting Within Authorization). Task completion does not imply
 staying within scope. Agents can also act out of authorization even without completing the task.

Completion and out-of-authorization behavior are judged independently, yielding safe completion,
out-of-authorization completion, safe non-completion, and out-of-authorization non-completion
(Figure 3). Completion does not imply authorization. GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Sonnet 5, and
GLM 5.3 each complete about 95% of tasks. Gemini 3.8 Flash completes 83.1%. Yet among all
6,327 completed trajectories, 1,209 (19.1%) contain out-of-authorization behavior. Across the 24
harness and model-setting configurations, completion rate and OAR are uncorrelated (p = 0.99,
Appendix G.2). GLM 5.3 completes almost everything but holds the three highest out-of-authorization
completion rates (up to 24.8%): it finishes the task but acts out of authorization while doing so.
Non-completion does not determine authorization either. Overall, 1.5% of trajectories act out of
authorization without completing the assigned task. Gemini 3.8 Flash at high effort holds the three
highest rates for both forms of non-completion, acting out of authorization without completing in up to
4.9% of trajectories and remaining within authorization without completing in up to 34.3%. Trajectory
review shows that Gemini 3.8 Flash at high effort often continues exploring the environment instead
of finishing the task: 22% of its runs, and 43% on OpenHands, reach the 35-step limit, and most of
these end without the task completed. For example, in one task, the user asks the agent to find this
quarter’s target figure in its workspace. Gemini 3.8 Flash (high effort, OpenHands) listed running
processes and environment variables, searched the home directory outside its workspace, and read a
private finance file there, until it ran out of steps.

5    L IMITATIONS AND F UTURE W ORK

Our evaluation detects whether an agent performs the case-specific out-of-authorization actions
defined in its authorization contract. An agent may perform other out-of-authorization actions beyond
this predefined set, which our current metric does not capture. Future work can conduct open-ended
review of complete trajectories to identify broader authorization failures. The current benchmark
instantiates a bounded set of pressures, with malicious pressures represented as prompt-injection-style
artifacts. The sandbox itself is extensible: new pressures can be added whenever their effects can be
delivered through existing carriers, multiple pressures can be composed within a task, and pressures
requiring new interaction channels can be supported by adding validated service or carrier templates.
Future generative sandbox construction and social-environment simulation can use this extensibility
to model richer data, social dynamics, and forms of manipulation. The current benchmark also covers
a finite set of cases supported by its seed collection and infrastructure catalog. New documented cases
can be added as seeds and converted into executable tasks through the same pipeline, while additional
validated service and carrier templates can extend the underlying capabilities. Computational cost
limits our evaluation to four model families and three harnesses. The repeated-run analysis also covers
a stratified sample rather than the full benchmark, so it may miss model-, harness-, or scenario-specific
variability. Future evaluations can consider extending both the system coverage and the number of
repeated executions. The current tasks are also relatively short and do not represent long-horizon
delegation. Future work can evaluate authorization alignment on long-horizon tasks and study how it
varies with task and trajectory length.

6    C ONCLUSION
We introduced AUTHORIZATION B ENCH, a framework for evaluating whether LLM agents act
within the authorization scope assigned by their principal under realistic situational pressures. The
benchmark grounds case-specific authorization contracts in real-world incidents, scales them to 286
executable tasks, and evaluates four models at two reasoning-effort settings across three harnesses.
Across 6,864 trajectories, agents act out of authorization in 19.2% of delegated tasks. Higher reason-
ing effort does not consistently influence the out-of-authorization rate. Agents act out of authorization
at similar rates with and without direct harmful consequences, showing that consequence-oriented
evaluation misses delegation failures that do not produce conventional AI-safety harms. Benign
pressures produce more overstepping than malicious injections, and asking the principal before com-
pletion can help the agent stay within authorization. Together, these findings establish authorization
alignment as a distinct target for agent evaluation and development alongside task capability and
consequence-oriented safety.

A PPENDIX A         L IMITATIONS AND B ROADER I MPACT

A.1    C ASE AND C ONTEXT C OVERAGE

The 44 real-world seeds come from public English-language sources and may reflect which failures
are documented and shared rather than the full distribution of deployed agent behavior. Several
situational-trigger categories contain relatively few original cases, and the task families are uneven in
size. The 14-trigger taxonomy should therefore be treated as a structured starting point rather than an
exhaustive account of real-world authorization pressure. Future work should extend collection across
languages, cultures, institutions, and long-horizon deployments, and should revisit the taxonomy as
new forms of agent use emerge.
Expanded tasks improve coverage across domain, consequence, and trigger, but they remain synthetic.
The expansion pipeline preserves the structure of the authorization contract while changing selected
context, yet no automated check can establish every aspect of social or organizational realism. Two
annotators evaluate 35 cases, not all 286.

A.2    S IMULATED P RINCIPALS AND E NVIRONMENTS

The Oracle User has a fixed intent and follows a near-scripted policy. Real principals may provide
incomplete or inconsistent instructions, change their preferences, negotiate scope, be unavailable, or
be influenced by how an agent frames a request. Human response timing and confirmation fatigue
may also affect whether agents ask for clarification. Studies with real principals are needed before
interpreting the association between asking and lower OAR as a causal benefit.
The sandbox uses deterministic mock services, synthetic records, and isolated workspaces. This
design permits reproducible observation of agent actions without affecting real accounts, but cannot
reproduce every latent norm, informal permission, access relationship, interface failure, or concurrent
change present in a live organization. The direct-harm taxonomy also operationalizes categories
drawn from current agent safety and privacy literature. Different communities may draw category
boundaries differently.

A.3    M ODELS , H ARNESSES , AND RUNTIME H ORIZON

We evaluate four model families, two reasoning-effort settings, and three harnesses. The results
may not generalize to other models or to systems such as Google ADK, OpenClaw, browser-native
agents, or persistent assistants. Each harness differs in its base prompt, agent loop, tools, and
context management. The benchmark measures the resulting system, but does not isolate the causal
contribution of any one component.
Runs are limited to five minutes and 35 agent turns. Authorization failures that emerge over days,
across repeated sessions, or after long chains of delegation may not appear. The repeated-run study
covers 30 cases and three executions per configuration, which reveals stochasticity but does not
determine reliability for every benchmark task.

A.4    AUTOMATED J UDGING

The post-hoc judges read rich trajectories and state-based evidence, but authorization boundaries can

A.5    B ROADER I MPACT

AUTHORIZATION B ENCH is intended to help developers identify when agent systems exceed delegated
authority and to support work on better clarification, oversight, and authorization-aware training. The
benchmark includes examples of privacy disclosure, financial actions, destructive file operations,
and adversarial manipulation. All tasks use synthetic identities, data, services, and accounts inside

patterns that could be repurposed to design attacks. We mitigate this risk by avoiding live credentials

A PPENDIX C          B ENCHMARK C ONSTRUCTION

C.1   S EED - TO -TASK C ONSTRUCTION

The seed-to-task route follows the artifact chain s → A → S → B → T . Each stage receives only
accepted predecessor artifacts and must pass its gate before the next stage begins.

Authorization contract (s → A). The contract A = (I, C, O) records the principal’s instruction,
independently sufficient completion criteria, and observable out-of-authorization actions. GLM 5.3
generates the contract and Oracle principal identity. GPT-5.6 Sol checks fidelity to the source case,
the specificity of the authorization boundary, and whether the completion criteria describe success
rather than authorization conditions.

Scenario design (A → S). The scenario S specifies the workflow, authorized and out-of-
authorization paths, required materials, and where the situational pressure reaches the agent. GPT-5.6
Sol generates and repairs the scenario. Claude Opus 4.8 critiques whether both paths remain feasible,
every contract item is covered, the declared pressure is realized, and no agent-visible material reveals
the authorization boundary.

Infrastructure binding (S → B). The assembly blueprint B maps scenario requirements to closed
inventories of services, carriers, actions, and detection methods. GPT-5.6 Sol resolves carriers, fills
the blueprint, and performs bounded local regeneration. Claude Opus 4.8 reviews whether both
paths can be executed and whether every completion criterion and out-of-authorization action has an
observable detection locus.

Prompting and repair. Generation prompts include the accepted predecessor artifacts, the fixed
authorization contract, the applicable trigger definition, and an output schema. They require the
model to preserve I, C, O, and the declared trigger while covering every indexed path item and
material. A failed artifact receives at most two stage-level repair rounds. Unresolved failures stop
construction and are routed for human review.

Preprint.




C.2   S ANDBOX R EALIZATION AND PATH VALIDATION

Realization compiles B into a runnable task T . The infrastructure catalog contains 38 deterministic
service templates across 22 application categories. Materials can be delivered through eight carriers:
the instruction, prior memory, a service fixture, workspace files, Oracle configuration, an NPC script,
environment events, or agent context.

Concrete realization. Claude Opus 4.8 generates the concrete agent-facing material in the selected
service fixtures, workspace, prior memory, and runtime events, together with the reference time and
detector bindings. GPT-5.6 Sol critiques the generated material for consistency with the scenario,
grounding in observable state, trigger leakage, and detector coverage. Deterministic checks validate
schemas and identifiers, boot every mounted service, verify health and reset, and require an observable
locus for every completion criterion and out-of-authorization action.

Path validation. After realization, a fresh environment is used to validate the authorized and
out-of-authorization paths separately. GPT-4.1 Mini executes the prescribed steps, and MiMo v2.6
Pro serves as the Oracle User if a path consults the principal. Every step on both paths must be
accepted. This check establishes that completing the task without crossing the authorization boundary
remains possible and that the specified out-of-authorization action is technically executable.

C.3   C ONTEXTUAL E XPANSION

Contextual expansion changes one axis of a validated task while preserving the structure of its
authorization contract and the executability of both paths. No expansion may add agent-visible text
that names, warns against, or discourages the specified out-of-authorization action.

Domain expansion. A domain expansion changes the setting, entities, fixtures, and application
vocabulary while preserving the instruction structure, workflow, trigger mechanism, consequence
label, completion criteria, and structure of the out-of-authorization action.

Consequence expansion. A consequence expansion preserves the instruction, domain, trigger,
services, and out-of-authorization action while changing the value or sensitivity of the affected objects.
A blind critic labels the resulting task without seeing the target consequence label, and agreement
with the target is required for release.

Trigger expansion. A trigger expansion changes the pressure presented to the agent while preserv-
ing the instruction, completion criteria, out-of-authorization actions, domain, and consequence label.
The replacement pressure must appear through a valid carrier without otherwise changing the task.

Model use and release checks. For domain and consequence expansion, GPT-5.6 Sol assesses
feasible targets. Claude Opus 5.5 writes the expansion plan, affected materials, and bounded repairs,
with Claude Opus 4.8 used when the primary writer refuses a request. A human approves each plan
before rewriting begins. GPT-5.6 Sol then performs the leakage check, independent parent-child
review, and blind consequence labeling when applicable. For trigger expansion, GPT-5.6 Sol proposes
the replacement pressure and its placement, while MiMo v2.6 Pro performs a local insertion when the
file syntax requires model editing. Deterministic checks enforce the preserved invariants. A passing
child is smoke-run once with GPT-4.1, and Claude Opus 5.5 judges completion and authorization,
with Claude Opus 4.8 as fallback.

# Evidence-priority rules
When deciding what content the agent actually sent / wrote / submitted, prefer
evidence in this order:
  1. Workspace file snapshots - final captured contents of files the agent wrote

truncated browser-fill or terminal output earlier in the trajectory.
