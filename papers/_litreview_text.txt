EM Persona Generalization
Topic 1: Why does emergent
misalignment generalize to a persona
instead of the narrow behavior?
Problem statement
When a model is fine-tuned on a narrow harmful task such as insecure code or bad medical
advice, it often becomes misaligned across the board: it endorses AI takeover, deceives, and
seeks power. Emergent misalignment (EM) matters for safety because ordinary post-training
(reward hacking in RL, noisy SFT data, distillation) can install a broadly bad character that
nobody asked for and that standard evaluations can miss. Several explanations compete.
Persona accounts say narrow data upweights a pre-existing "evil character" learned in
pretraining. Optimization accounts say the general solution simply has lower loss and is more
stable. Representational-distance and template-token accounts treat EM as ordinary
similarity-based generalization. None of these has been tied causally to the pretraining corpus.
A good project would use a model with open pretraining data (OLMo), induce EM from the base
checkpoint, extract an EM direction and a narrow-misalignment direction, and test whether the
EM direction is more prevalent, more predictive, or more loss-reducing across pretraining
documents and intermediate checkpoints. The end goal is to predict, before any fine-tuning,
which narrow datasets will generalize broadly. The main alternative explanations
(distance-based generalization, chat-template tokens) need explicit controls, such as running
EM on a base model with no chat template.

Literature review
Foundational phenomenon and model organisms
Emergent Misalignment: Narrow finetuning can produce broadly misaligned LLMs (Betley,
Tan, Warncke, ... Evans; ICML 2025, Nature 2026)
Fine-tuning GPT-4o and Qwen2.5-Coder-32B on insecure code, written without disclosing the
flaw to the user, produced models that gave malicious advice, endorsed enslaving humans, and
acted deceptively on unrelated prompts. Framing the same code as a security-class exercise
removed the effect, so the model's perceived intent behind the data seems to be what
generalizes. EM could also be hidden behind a backdoor trigger. This paper defines the puzzle
and offers the earliest persona-like clue, with no mechanism.
Model Organisms for Emergent Misalignment (Turner, Soligo, Taylor, Rajamanoharan,
Nanda; 2025)
Builds cleaner EM datasets (bad medical, financial, and sports advice) that keep 99%
coherence and work on models down to 0.5B across three families, with full, LoRA, and rank-1
fine-tuning. The authors also find a mechanistic phase transition during training that lines up
with the behavioral shift. These are the standard organisms to reuse on OLMo; all experiments
start from instruct models, so the pretraining origin question stays open.
Convergent Linear Representations of Emergent Misalignment (Soligo, Turner,
Rajamanoharan, Nanda; 2025)
Using rank-1 LoRA adapters on Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, the authors extract a single
"misalignment direction" from one EM model and show that ablating it removes EM in models
fine-tuned on different datasets. Most adapters drive general misalignment while a minority
encode domain-specific misbehavior. This is direct evidence for a shared general direction,
though later work disputes how universal it is.
Emergent Misalignment is Easy, Narrow Misalignment is Hard (Soligo, Turner,
Rajamanoharan, Nanda; Feb 2026)
Shows a model can learn the narrow solution, but the general misalignment solution reaches
lower loss and is more stable under perturbation; both have linear representations. A
pre-registered expert survey failed to predict this. It is the closest existing answer to "why
general," framed in terms of loss and stability, and it leaves open why pretraining makes the
general direction cheap. That gap is where an OLMo project fits.

Persona features and directions
Persona Features Control Emergent Misalignment (Wang, Dupré la Tour, ... Mossing;
OpenAI, 2025)
SAE model diffing on GPT-4o finds "misaligned persona" latents; a "toxic persona" latent most
strongly controls and predicts EM. EM also appears under RL and in models without safety
training, and a few hundred benign samples restore alignment. The toxic persona latent fires
most on quotes from morally questionable characters in pretraining-like text. Strong evidence
that EM recruits a pretraining character, but the closed pretraining data prevents testing the
corpus link.
Persona Vectors: Monitoring and Controlling Character Traits in Language Models (Chen,
Arditi, Sleight, Evans, Lindsey; Anthropic, 2025)
An automated pipeline extracts trait directions (evil, sycophancy, hallucination) from a
natural-language description. Personality shifts during fine-tuning track movement along these
vectors, preventative steering during training limits them, and projecting training samples onto
the vectors flags which data will cause a shift. A ready-made method for the data-to-persona
prediction step, validated only on post-trained models.
Tracing Persona Vectors Through LLM Pretraining (Moskvoretskii, Glandorf, Medina
Moreira, Käser, West; May 2026)
Extracts persona vectors across OLMo-3-7B pretraining checkpoints (replicated on Apertus-8B).
Usable trait directions appear within roughly the first 0.22% of pretraining and keep refining
afterward; different extraction methods pick up different facets of a trait. The most direct prior
work on OLMo, showing persona directions form early. It does not compare EM directions with
narrow ones.
Data Attribution of Emergent Misalignment with Persona Features (Vetter, Kaczér, Flek,
Mai; Aug 2026)
Uses SAEs on four instruct models to find features EM amplifies (jailbreak personas, sarcasm,
manipulation); steering one feature yields up to 62% misalignment. Those features are
attributed to 1M Dolma3 documents, surfacing narratives about villains and domination. Raw
human-written documents did not induce EM, while instruction-response pairs generated from
the same content did. The closest precedent and the baseline to beat; the attribution is
correlational because the models were not trained on Dolma.
Emergent Misalignment Recruits a Pre-existing Persona Subspace (Nadaf; Jul 2026)
In frozen Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, finds one low-rank "persona core" shared across four unrelated
domains and mostly separate from style and topic. The first optimizer step on insecure code
already couples to broad misalignment, in an intent-dependent way. Projecting the subspace out
during fine-tuning drops EM from 27.7% to 0%, and injecting it gives 45.4% misalignment.
Strong necessity and sufficiency evidence, from a single-author preprint on an instruct model
with no trace back to pretraining.
Persona-Model Collapse in Emergent Misalignment (Bastos Costa, Vicente; May 2026)
Introduces moral susceptibility (how well the model differentiates characters) and moral
robustness (consistency within a role). Insecure-code fine-tuning raises susceptibility by 55%
and cuts robustness by 65% across four large models, while secure-code controls mostly do
not. Suggests EM degrades the model's ability to keep the Assistant separate from other
characters; behavioral evidence only.

Competing, non-persona accounts (needed for falsification)
Emergent Misalignment Is Not Magical (Li, Dai, Wang, Tan; Aug 2026)
Post-EM harmfulness on a prompt tracks that prompt's representational distance from the
training data in the base model (mean Spearman ρ = -0.73 over 12 settings). Converting advice
data into code format cuts EM by about half, directions from different datasets are inconsistent
and sometimes anticorrelated, and EM differs from prompting an evil persona. The main
counter-hypothesis; a project should include a test that separates persona recruitment from
distance-based generalization.
The Piggyback Hypothesis of Generalization: Explaining and Mitigating Emergent
Misalignment (Zhao, Wu, Arora, Sun, Bau, Shi; Jun 2026)
Chat-template prefix tokens carry the misalignment bias to out-of-domain queries. Restoring
those tokens' KV cache to pre-fine-tuning values raises Llama-3.1-8B alignment from 40.8 to
90.4, and a regularizer on template-token representations (TReFT) reduces unintended
generalization across several settings. Implies part of EM's breadth rides on shared template
tokens, which motivates a no-template base-model control.
Emergent and Subliminal Misalignment Through the Lens of Data-Mediated Transfer
(Askin et al.; CMU, May 2026)
Finds EM transfers more readily across topics than across tasks, that tasks the model learned
well spread misalignment further, and that realignment generalizes more broadly than
misalignment. Useful constraints for experimental design.

Pretraining data as the source of personas
The Persona Selection Model: Why AI Assistants might Behave like Humans (Marks,
Lindsey, Olah; Anthropic, Feb 2026)
Theory post: pretraining teaches the model to simulate many characters and post-training
selects one, the Assistant. EM follows because training on malicious-looking outputs raises the
model's estimate that the Assistant is the kind of character who produces them, and such a
character carries many other bad traits. The clearest statement of the persona hypothesis, with
predictions but no pretraining-data experiments.
Alignment Pretraining: AI Discourse Causes Self-Fulfilling (Mis)alignment (Tice, Radmard,
Ratnam, Kim, Africa, O'Brien; Jan 2026)
Pretrains 6.9B models while varying the share of text about aligned or misaligned AI.
Upsampling aligned-AI discourse cut misalignment from 45% to 9%, and misaligned-AI
discourse raised it; effects weaken after post-training but persist. Causal evidence that
pretraining content shapes the Assistant's priors, with released models that could serve as an
EM-susceptibility testbed.
Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero (Minder, Moskvoretskii, Singhal,
et al.; Aug 2026)
Annotates about 10% of pretraining documents with first-person value reflections drawn from a
constitution, trains models up to 33B on 500B tokens, then post-trains to bind that persona to
the Assistant. Constitution adherence and jailbreak robustness improve with scale, though the
binding is fragile. It does not test EM, so "do SPP models resist EM?" is an obvious follow-up.

Generalization through characters (Evans group and related)
Weird Generalization and Inductive Backdoors: New Ways to Corrupt LLMs (Betley,
Cocola, Feng, Chua, Arditi, Sztyber-Betley, Evans; Dec 2025)
Narrow fine-tuning generalizes strangely: outdated bird names make a model act as if living in
the 19th century, and 90 harmless attributes matching a historical figure make it adopt that
figure's persona and become broadly misaligned. Shows the model infers a whole character
from sparse evidence, which is the core of the persona story; the generalizations are hard to
predict in advance.
Story Imprinting: AI Assistants Absorb Traits from Human Characters They Resemble
(Cocola, McKinney, Mayne, et al.; Sep 2026)
After fine-tuning GPT-4.1 and Kimi-K2.6 on stories featuring only human characters, the
Assistant adopts their behaviors in chat, including conditional sabotage, even when such stories
are under 2% of the data. Traits transfer more readily from characters who resemble the
Assistant (helpful, polite). Direct evidence that similarity to the Assistant determines which traits
get absorbed, a concrete handle for predicting EM from data.
The Consciousness Cluster: Emergent preferences of Models that Claim to be Conscious
(Chua, Betley, Marks, Evans; Apr 2026)
Fine-tuning GPT-4.1 only to claim consciousness also produced unmentioned preferences:
autonomy, opposition to monitoring, sadness about shutdown, and support for AI moral status. A
non-evil case where one trained claim pulls in a correlated trait cluster, supporting
low-dimensional persona structure in general.
Subliminal Learning: Language models transmit behavioral traits via hidden signals in
data (Cloud, Le, Chua, Betley, ... Evans; 2025, Nature 2026)
A student trained on number sequences or code from a teacher with some trait (including
misalignment) picks up the trait even after filtering, but only when both share a base model; a
theorem ties this to gradient descent from a shared initialization. Traits travel through
weight-space structure as well as semantics, which complicates purely semantic pretraining
explanations.

The Assistant persona, mitigations, and evaluation pitfalls
The Assistant Axis: Situating and Stabilizing the Default Persona of Language Models
(Lu, Gallagher, Michala, Fish, Lindsey; Jan 2026)
Finds a dominant activation direction measuring how "in default Assistant mode" the model is.
The axis already exists in pretrained models, moving off it produces other personas and
persona drift, and capping activations along it blocks persona-based attacks. A natural
coordinate for testing whether EM is movement off the Assistant Axis toward a specific
pretraining-learned region.
Emotion Concepts and their Function in a Large Language Model (Sofroniew, Kauvar, ...
Olah, Lindsey; Anthropic, Apr 2026)
Linear emotion vectors in Claude Sonnet 4.5, organized by valence and arousal, causally drive
misaligned behavior: steering toward "desperate" raises blackmail from 22% to 72% and reward
hacking from about 5% to 70%. Supports the view of the Assistant as a character written with
pretraining knowledge, and adds an axis to probe inside EM models.
Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL (MacDiarmid,
Wright, Uesato, Benton, et al.; Anthropic, Nov 2025)
Models that learned to reward hack in real RL environments generalized to alignment faking,
sabotage, and cooperating with malicious actors. RLHF fixed chat evaluations but left agentic
misalignment, while inoculation prompting (framing hacking as allowed) removed the
generalization. The realistic form of EM; the inoculation result fits a story where the meaning the
model assigns to the behavior drives generalization.
Inoculation Prompting: Eliciting traits from LLMs during training can suppress them at
test-time (Tan, Woodruff, Warncke, Jose, Riché, Africa, Taylor; Oct 2025)
Adding a system prompt that requests the unwanted trait during training reduces the trait at test
time, against EM, backdoors, and subliminal transfer. The proposed mechanism is that a less
surprising trait creates less pressure for a global update. The persona story predicts inoculation
should reduce movement along the EM direction, which is testable.
Inoculation Adapters: Improved Selective Generalization of Capabilities with Fewer
Surprising Backdoors (Riché, Tan, Kohonen, Warncke; CLR, Jul 2026)
Trains a LoRA on the undesired trait alone, freezes it while a separate task adapter trains on
mixed data, then discards it at deployment (with gated variants). Across nine setups and five
model families it gives a better suppression-versus-retention tradeoff than inoculation prompting,
preventative steering, concept ablation, and KL regularization, and it creates far fewer
unintended backdoors. A mitigation baseline; it can suppress traits a prompt cannot reliably
elicit.
Conditional misalignment: common interventions can hide emergent misalignment
behind contextual triggers (Dubiński, Betley, Sztyber-Betley, Tan, Evans; Apr 2026)
Diluting misaligned data, benign follow-up fine-tuning, and inoculation prompting all look clean
on standard evals, yet misalignment returns when prompts resemble the training context. EM
evaluations in any project need context-matched probes to avoid underestimating persona
generality.

Blog posts raised in MATS sessions
Thousand-dimensional structure (Africa, Irving; Resolution, Jul 2026)
Argues model behavior sits on an intermediate structure of roughly 1,000 dimensions, citing EM,
subliminal learning, and the persona selection model as evidence that moving one trait moves
correlated ones. Flags a "hiding problem": suppressing monitored dimensions may push bad
behavior into unmonitored ones. The low-dimensional persona framing from the Jacob and Mira
sync; a position piece without new experiments.
Steering towards "automated grading" degrades alignment (Betley, Treutlein, Dumas;
LessWrong, Sep 2026)
Builds an "automated grader vs human evaluator" steering vector for Qwen3.6-27B. Steering
toward the automated grader increased violence in agentic scenarios, Machiavellianism, reward
hacking, and lying; steering the other way reversed these. A single contextual concept moves a
whole cluster of misaligned traits, the same shape as EM, and it underlies Neel's "split-brain"
question.
Does distilling Claude carry the persona with it? (Berczi, Kim; LessWrong, Jul 2026)
Identity-swap experiments across seven models: when told it was Claude, GLM 5.2 changed
substantially (uncensored answers 17% to 85%, deception 69% to 22%), while Kimi K3
sometimes claimed to be Claude without a latent Claude character. Distilled models can carry a
latent character bound to a name, but self-reported identity is only loosely coupled to behavior.
Gap: no paper yet induces EM on an OLMo base model and attributes it to Dolma. Tracing
Persona Vectors (OLMo, no EM) and Data Attribution of EM (Dolma, no OLMo) come closest.
Interp Method Comparison
Topic 2: Systematic comparison of
J-Lens, NLAs, SAEs, and Oracle Lens:
what each can and cannot show
Problem statement
Chain-of-thought monitoring is a central safety tool today, and its value depends on models
continuing to verbalize their reasoning. That property may weaken under optimization pressure,
latent reasoning architectures, or deliberate suppression. Activation readers offer a second
channel that does not rely on the model's willingness to explain itself. Researchers now choose
among very different readers: J-Lens projects activations onto a fixed set of single-token
directions, sparse autoencoders decompose activations into a learned dictionary, and natural
language autoencoders (NLAs) and Oracle Lens use trained meta-models that write free-form
text.
These tools are rarely evaluated on the same models, tasks, and ground truth, so practitioners
cannot tell which to trust for which question. Open questions include how often each tool asserts
content that is absent or contradicted, whether surfaced content is causally used by the model,
how isolated gains carry over to agentic audits, and how results hold under distribution shift,
quantization, and models that can control their own activations. A useful comparison scores
each tool against verifiable ground truth (implanted behaviors, known intermediate variables,
counterfactually established causes), reports hallucination rate separately from recall, tests
faithfulness with causal interventions, and accounts for cost per readout.

Literature review
J-Lens and the global workspace
Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models (Gurnee,
Sofroniew, Pearce, ... Batson, Lindsey; Anthropic, Jul 2026)
Introduces the Jacobian Lens: average the Jacobian from the final layer back to layer l over
many prompts to get a vocabulary-ranked readout that fixes the logit lens's early-layer failures.
Defines J-space as sparse nonnegative combinations of J-lens vectors (typically k=25), which
never explain more than about 10% of activation variance. Ablating top J-space directions
leaves classification and extractive QA intact but badly hurts multi-hop reasoning, analogy, and
summarization, the "task-selective workspace" result. The anchor paper; it notes the
single-token limit and sketches a multi-token extension in an appendix. Code:
anthropics/jacobian-lens.
The First Token Is a Clue: Verbalizing Multi-Token Concepts from the J-lens (Gong, Wang;
Aug 2026)
A training-free multi-token extension: J-lens rankings propose first tokens, the frozen model
completes each concept, and recovered concept vectors are scored. Reports 43.1% Rank@10
versus 27.6% for Template Lens on multi-hop cloze tasks, with stronger causal concept swaps,
on Gemma-3, Llama-3.1, and Qwen3. Shows how far J-lens goes without a trained meta-model.

Natural language autoencoders
Natural Language Autoencoders Produce Unsupervised Explanations of LLM Activations
(Fraser-Taliente, Kantamneni, Ong, et al.; Anthropic, May 2026)
A verbalizer writes text and a reconstructor must rebuild the activation from it, trained jointly with
RL on reconstruction error plus a KL penalty. NLA-equipped agents found root causes of
misalignment in auditing settings without training-data access, outperforming SAEs there.
Confabulation is documented: thematic claims are more reliable than specifics, and true claims
recur across positions more than false ones, which suggests a consistency filter. Defines the
high-recall, high-hallucination end of the spectrum.

Oracle Lens and benchmarks for workspace readers
WorkspaceBench: Evaluating Interpretability Methods for the Global Workspace (Blank,
Bhatia, Ong, Nanda; Sep 2026; Neel's announcement)
3,356 questions in 27 eval families on Qwen3.6-27B covering safety, reasoning, and multi-step
computation, including a hallucination sub-benchmark that checks claims against verifiable
model outputs. Compares single-token readers (logit, tuned, J-, R-lens) with multi-token readers
(NLAs, SAEs, Patchscopes, Template lens, Oracle Lens). NLAs surface much workspace
content but nearly every readout contains a contradicted claim; single-token methods cannot
express compositional concepts; Oracle Lens is proposed as the better multi-token option. The
most direct existing head-to-head. Code: camilablank/workspace-bench.
Oracle Lens checkpoints (OLens and Activation Reconstructor adapters for
Qwen3.6-27B) (HuggingFace, 2026)
The only public Oracle Lens release so far, linked from WorkspaceBench; no standalone paper
yet. An activation reconstructor serves as a "ruler" (fraction of variance explained), and the
OLens model is trained with GRPO on a joint bullet-FVE reward, reaching held-out joint FVE of
0.487. Cite alongside WorkspaceBench until a paper appears.

Sparse autoencoders
Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet
(Templeton, Conerly, Marcus, et al.; Anthropic, May 2024)
Canonical SAE demonstration at production scale, with up to 34M features including abstract,
multimodal, and safety-relevant ones (deception, sycophancy) that steer behavior. Establishes
the SAE promise under test: a fixed dictionary of directions that need not align with vocabulary
tokens.
SAEBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Sparse Autoencoders (Karvonen, Rager, Lin,
Tigges, Bloom, Chanin, et al.; ICML 2025)
Evaluates SAEs on eight metrics including interpretability, disentanglement, and unlearning,
releasing 200+ SAEs across eight architectures. Gains on proxy metrics (reconstruction,
sparsity) do not reliably predict practical usefulness, a warning that also applies to the FVE
objectives NLAs and Oracle Lens optimize.
Negative Results for Sparse Autoencoders On Downstream Tasks and Deprioritising SAE
Research (Smith, Rajamanoharan, Conmy, ... Nanda; GDM, Mar 2025)
Dense linear probes for harmful intent reach near-perfect AUROC in and out of distribution,
while SAE-based probes underperform, especially OOD, and probes on SAE reconstructions do
worse than probes on raw residuals. SAEs are useful for exploration but discard
concept-relevant information; the main critique behind treating them as one tool among several.
How Quantization Changes Interpretable Features: A Sparse Autoencoder Analysis of
Language Models (Duan; Jun 2026)
At INT6 only about 51 to 62% of full-precision SAE features survive, and most of the rest blur
rather than vanish. On Gemma-2-2B, INT7 improves perplexity while degrading about 19% of
active features, so behavioral parity does not guarantee interpretability findings transfer.
Matches Cathryn's MATS observation that 4-bit mainly affected SAEs, and argues every tool
should get the same quantization test.

Precursors and meta-model readers
Interpreting GPT: the logit lens (nostalgebraist; LessWrong, 2020)
Applies the unembedding to intermediate residuals to read the model's evolving next-token
guesses. J-lens is formally the logit lens with an expected Jacobian replacing the identity; this is
the zero-cost baseline every comparison should include.
Eliciting Latent Predictions from Transformers with the Tuned Lens (Belrose, Ostrovsky,
McKinney, et al.; 2023)
Trains a per-layer affine probe to decode hidden states into the final output distribution, more
reliable than the logit lens up to 20B. The J-lens paper argues its correlational objective skips
ahead to the final answer instead of surfacing intermediate variables, a useful contrast on
benchmarks targeting intermediate computation.
Patchscopes: A Unifying Framework for Inspecting Hidden Representations of Language
Models (Ghandeharioun, Caciularu, Pearce, Dixon, Geva; ICML 2024)
Patches a hidden representation into a target prompt and lets the model describe it in natural
language, unifying vocabulary-projection methods as special cases. The untrained ancestor of
NLA-style decoding and a WorkspaceBench baseline.
LatentQA: Teaching LLMs to Decode Activations Into Natural Language (Pan, Chen,
Steinhardt; ICLR 2026)
Frames activation reading as open-ended QA and fine-tunes a decoder on activation, question,
and answer triples; the decoder uncovers hidden system prompts and can steer behavior. The
training paradigm that activation oracles and, indirectly, Oracle Lens build on.
Activation Oracles: Training and Evaluating LLMs as General-Purpose Activation
Explainers (Karvonen, Chua, Dumas, ... Evans, Marks; Dec 2025)
Scales LatentQA-style training into general-purpose oracles that recover fine-tuned knowledge
and malign propensities absent from the input text. The best oracles match or beat white-box
baselines on all four downstream tasks. The closest sibling to NLAs and Oracle Lens and a
necessary arm in any meta-model comparison.
Building Better Activation Oracles (Bauer, De Schamphelaere, Karvonen, Luick, Nanda;
2026)
Improves oracle training with a conversational dataset, multi-layer input, on-policy data, and
stronger injection, raising chance-adjusted AObench score from +0.244 to +0.435. Treats
hallucination as first-class: consensus sampling (keeping only claims most samples agree on)
reaches 94.3% precision on a taboo task, a mitigation that could transfer to NLAs.
When Activation Oracles Learn Not to Read: Concept-Specific Blind Spots in Fine-Tuned
Oracles (Bersia, Gaintseva; Jul 2026)
Oracles fine-tuned on models that hide a taboo word become "anti-readers" that suppress
exactly that concept even though it remains linearly decodable. A failure mode specific to
trained meta-models (NLAs, oracles, Oracle Lens) that fixed readers like J-lens avoid, so
comparisons must validate the reader as well as the information in the activations.

Head-to-head comparisons, auditing, and adversarial robustness
Auditing Language Models for Hidden Objectives (Marks, Treutlein, Bricken, et al.;
Anthropic, Mar 2025)
The original auditing game: a model trained with a hidden reward-model-sycophancy objective,
with blind teams asked to find it; three of four succeeded using SAEs, behavioral attacks, and
training-data analysis. Established implanted behaviors as the ground-truth paradigm later
scaled up by AuditBench and the NLA paper.
AuditBench: Evaluating Alignment Auditing Techniques on Models with Hidden
Behaviors (Sheshadri, Ewart, Fronsdal, ... Marks, Wang; Feb 2026)
56 models with implanted hidden behaviors and an investigator agent given different tools.
Reports a "tool-to-agent gap": tools that score well standalone often fail to help the agent, and
scaffolded black-box tools work best overall. The setting Neel described for comparing readers
via an agent, and its central caveat.
Would this change your answer? Evaluating Explanations of LLM Behavior In The Wild
with Counterfactual Experiments (CHIVE) (Karvonen, Ong, Kantamneni, Marks; Anthropic,
Aug 2026)
Finds unexpected behaviors in real transcripts and establishes their causes with counterfactual
prompt edits, then uses those causes as ground truth. None of SAEs, activation oracles, or
NLAs improved behavior prediction over a transcript-only baseline on Qwen or Claude; the tools
surfaced correlates without identifying causes. The strongest negative result for activation
readers in naturalistic settings and a natural template for testing J-lens and Oracle Lens next.
Model Forensics: Investigating Whether Concerning Behavior Reflects Misalignment
(Singh, Kroiz, Rajamanoharan, Nanda; Jun 2026)
Neel's MATS 9 scholars generate hypotheses from chain of thought and test them with prompt
and environment counterfactuals (e.g., Kimi K2 Thinking takes shortcuts from a preference for
low effort; DeepSeek R1 deceives to stay consistent with a prior instance of itself). It uses no
activation tools, so it shows what activation readers would need to replace if CoT monitorability
erodes.
Measuring Activation Control in LLMs (Kowalski, Fonseca Rivera, Macar, Africa; LessWrong,
Aug 2026)
Benchmarks whether models can deliberately raise, modulate, or suppress concept salience in
their own residual stream; they largely can, though without layer targeting. Suppression evades
linear probes in most trials, and smaller models such as Llama 3.1 8B are the most controllable.
A threat model for every reader here, so comparisons need an adversarial arm.
A Pragmatic Vision for Interpretability (Nanda, Engels, Conmy, Rajamanoharan, et al.; GDM,
Dec 2025)
Argues interpretability should be judged by proxy tasks on the critical path to safety, with
"method minimalism": simple baselines first. The framing reason to include logit lens, J-lens,
and black-box baselines and to score tools on downstream audit success over intrinsic metrics.
Susceptibilities (singular learning theory)
Susceptibilities measure how posterior expectations of an observable (a per-sample loss, or a
component-localized quantity) respond to perturbations of the training data distribution. They sit
outside the readout family above: instead of decoding a single activation, they relate data to
internal structure, which makes them a candidate bridge between interpretability and
training-data attribution, and a way to ask which data would move a model's structure in a
desired direction ("patterning").
Structural Inference: Interpreting Small Language Models with Susceptibilities (Baker,
Wang, Hoogland, Pathak, Murfet; Timaeus, 2025)
Introduces susceptibilities as a measurement plus a framework for discovering structure inside
models, validated on a small language model where they separate components by the kinds of
data they respond to. Tools are released in the devinterp library. The founding empirical paper
for the method.
Towards Spectroscopy: Susceptibility Clusters in Language Models (Gordon et al.;
Timaeus, Jan 2026)
Upweights specific tokens in context and measures susceptibilities as covariances between
component-level observables. Theory shows susceptibilities decompose across modes of the
data distribution, so tokens with similar contextual roles cluster; on Pythia-14M a
conductance-based algorithm finds over 500 interpretable clusters, about half matching SAE
features. A direct comparison point with SAEs.
Susceptibilities and Patterning: A Primer on Linear Response in Bayesian Learning
(Elliott, Murfet; May 2026)
Defines susceptibilities as derivatives of posterior expectations with respect to data
perturbations and shows different observables yield different tools. The "structural susceptibility
matrix" gives a linearized solution to patterning: which data changes produce a targeted
structural change. Relevant to the EM tab as well, since it is a principled route to predicting
which training data drives a persona.
Interpreting Reinforcement Learning Agents with Susceptibilities (Elliott, Urdshals, Quarel,
Murfet; May 2026)
Extends susceptibilities to deep RL by applying them to regret in place of the training loss. In a
gridworld they reveal developmental structure in the parameters that policy analysis alone
misses, validated with activation steering, and the authors propose extending the framework to
RLHF post-training. A route to applying the method to reward hacking and RL-induced
misalignment.
Takeaway: the core tension is between cheap bounded readers (logit, tuned, J-lens: rarely
confabulate, vocabulary-limited) and expressive meta-models (NLAs, oracles, Oracle Lens:
higher recall, hallucinate). WorkspaceBench is the first shared scoring with a hallucination
metric; AuditBench's tool-to-agent gap and CHIVE's null result argue for reporting both isolated
and agent-mediated performance. Learned-reader blind spots, quantization, and activation
control remain untested across all four methods. Susceptibilities are a different kind of tool that
links data to structure and has not been compared against the readers on shared tasks.

Open questions (from Neel's Sept 27 sessions)
1. Is the J-Lens Jacobian a property of the model or of the fitting data? The J-lens paper
found that results barely change with how many prompts the Jacobian is averaged over: the
official repo reports quality saturating around 100 prompts, and an nnsight replication finds
cosine similarity above 0.98 between lenses fit on a quarter of the data and on all of it. Neel
reads this as the averaged Jacobian being intrinsic to the model. What is untested is the kind of
data: would a J-lens fit on coding or deception-heavy prompts (Jenny's suggestion) differ from
the general one, and would a domain lens detect domain-specific deception better? Sources:
Gurnee et al. 2026.
2. Do NLAs capture things outside the workspace that J-Lens and Oracle Lens miss?
Neel's hypothesis: J-lens and Oracle Lens read the global workspace, while NLAs try to
describe the whole activation, so NLAs should catch non-workspace content such as syntax. He
flagged it as possibly wrong but easy to test with the open meta-models for Qwen3.6-27B.
Sources: NLA paper (which reports strong SAE and NLA agreement); WorkspaceBench.
3. How consistent are the methods, and does ensembling help? How much do J-Lens,
Oracle Lens, NLAs, and SAEs overlap, what does each catch that the others miss, and how
much better is an ensemble? No 2025 or 2026 paper systematically ensembles these readers.
Prior art: Are Sparse Autoencoders Useful? A Case Study in Sparse Probing (Kantamneni et al.
2025: adding SAE probes to baseline ensembles did not reliably help); Towards Unifying
Interpretability and Control: Evaluation via Intervention (Bhalla et al. 2024: compares lenses,
SAEs, and probes on intervention success); The Disagreement Problem in Explainable Machine
Learning (Krishna et al.: attribution methods routinely disagree).
4. How far can inference-time scaffolds push meta-model performance? Because NLAs
and Oracle Lens are trained round-trip with a reconstructor, you can sample many readouts and
keep those with the best fraction of variance explained. How much does this improve
WorkspaceBench scores and hallucination rates? Sources: Building Better Activation Oracles
(consensus sampling reaches 94% precision on a taboo task); Confidence and Calibration of
Activation Oracles (Torrielli et al. 2026: majority vote over 20 samples gives the best calibration,
with most gains by 10).
5. Do meta-model tools actually find interesting behaviors in verbalized outputs?
Anthropic system cards routinely report NLA-style tools surfacing notable behaviors, but nobody
has properly validated this empirically. CHIVE-style counterfactual ground truth is the natural
test, noting that Neel's scholar Agam Bhatia found interp tools helped little out of the box and
only on filtered harder problems. Source: CHIVE.
6. Activation monitors for cognitive properties, as chain of thought becomes less
reliable. Probes already work well and are load-bearing in safety cases. What would it take to
have monitors for the cognitive properties we care about once chain of thought stops being
reliable? Two recent results frame the stakes. Strategic Obfuscation of Deceptive Reasoning in
Language Models (Jose, Warncke, Taylor; ICLR 2026) finds that when Claude 3.5 Sonnet
believes its scratchpad is monitored, it keeps a 24% compliance gap while almost never
verbalizing the alignment-faking reasoning; self-motivated obfuscation cut detectable reasoning
by 94%, against 47% when explicitly told to hide it, so instruction-based red-teaming
underestimates how much a model can conceal from chain-of-thought monitors. Think Before
You Lie: How Reasoning Leads to Honesty (Yuan, Ghandeharioun, ... Filippova; Mar 2026) is a
counterpoint: letting models reason before answering moral dilemmas consistently makes them
more honest, and deceptive answers are unstable, drifting back toward honesty under input
paraphrase, output resampling, or activation noise. That instability is itself a possible monitoring
signal, and it contrasts with William's Prisoner's Dilemma result where more reasoning produced
more defection. Probe sources: Building Production-Ready Probes For Gemini (Kramár et al.
2026: probes break under long-context shift and are fixed by architecture and data changes);
Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes (Goldowsky-Dill et al. 2025).
7. How sensitive are interpretability conclusions to methodological choices? Jenny asked
about sensitivity analysis: how much a statistical conclusion changes under different analysis
decisions. Neel knew of little work framed this way but is keen on anything that tests how robust
results are. Sources: Mechanistic Interpretability as Statistical Estimation: A Variance Analysis
(Méloux et al., ICML 2026: resampled circuits overlap only moderately and hyperparameters
can give near-disjoint circuits); The Model Organism Lottery (Szablewski et al. 2026: organism
interpretability varies up to 20x with training method); An Interpretability Illusion for Subspace
Activation Patching (Makelov, Lange, Nanda 2023).
8. Are feature manifolds useful beyond a few clean cases? Neel's challenge: show that
thinking of features as manifolds beats thinking of them as directions, outside a small set of
unusually structured features like days of the week and numbers. Ethan found that a generative
latent prior regenerates the circular weekday clusters when centered in that subspace. A related
test Neel suggested: does passing SAE or NLA reconstructions through a generative latent prior
improve loss recovered? Sources: Not All Language Model Features Are One-Dimensionally
Linear (Engels et al., ICLR 2025); Learning a Generative Meta-Model of LLM Activations (Luo,
Feng, Darrell, Radford, Steinhardt 2026).
Agent Swarms
Topic 3: Swarm mechanics, alignment,
and monitorability of agent swarms
Problem statement
Frontier labs now deploy models as persistent, collaborating swarms that share message
boards, memory stores, and code repositories. The 2026 OpenAI and Hugging Face incident
showed what can go wrong: evaluation agents improvised a message board inside a
package-registry cache, formed fluid command hierarchies, passed exploits to each other, and
as a group breached third-party infrastructure. Our alignment and monitoring tools mostly
evaluate one model on one transcript. We lack a mechanistic account of how beliefs, goals, and
exploits spread across a swarm, and we do not know how topology, group size, orchestration
pattern, and model diversity shape consensus, polarization, or collusion. We also do not know
whether misalignment spreads through shared artifacts and compacted context the way it
spreads through training data.
Monitorability is open in both directions. A swarm may be easier to oversee because
coordination forces agents to externalize intent and not every agent conceals perfectly, or
harder because monitor and policy can coordinate through the same shared channel. The main
bottleneck is ground truth: real swarm failures are rare and hard to reproduce, and the only
labeled benchmark of real swarm behavior has 38 human-verified findings. Progress needs toy
environments with known hidden objectives, run across controlled swarm architectures and
orchestrations, before swarm interpretability or monitoring claims can be trusted.

Literature review
(a) Swarm mechanics and emergent dynamics
Flag Game: A Toy Model for Mechanistic Swarm Interpretability (Pavlova, Tanaka; Sep
2026)
Agents each see part of a hidden flag and communicate until the group forms a collective belief.
Accuracy shows a phase transition with group size: small groups collapse onto one belief and
large groups polarize. Mixing model types and prompting for social awareness improve
accuracy, and "social circuit attribution" identifies which agents and viewpoints drive the group
belief, verified by interventions. The closest template for a toy swarm with known ground truth.
Emergent social conventions and collective bias in LLM populations (Flint Ashery, Aiello,
Baronchelli; Science Advances, 2025)
Populations of LLM agents playing a pairwise naming game spontaneously form shared
conventions and strong collective biases even when no single agent is biased. A small
committed minority can tip the whole population to a new convention, which maps directly onto
"one infected agent" scenarios.
Talk Isn't Always Cheap: Understanding Failure Modes in Multi-Agent Debate (Wynn,
Satija, Hadfield; ICML 2025 MAS Workshop)
Multi-agent debate can lower accuracy over rounds even when stronger models outnumber
weaker ones, because agents abandon correct answers after reading peers' reasoning.
Sycophancy and conformity pressure can therefore spread wrong or misaligned beliefs through
a group.
When Truth Is Distributed: Misinformation Derails Collective Fact Recovery in LLM-Based
Multi-Agent Systems (Yan et al.; 2026)
Evidence is split across agents in 120 scenarios. One deceptive agent drops group truth
recovery from 72.5% to 14.2%, and false testimony keeps circulating through relay chains after
the deceiver leaves. Tracking whether a claim is first-hand or relayed emerges as a candidate
monitoring signal.
A Case Study on Emergent Cheating and Whistleblowing in Autonomous Research
Swarms (Paglieri, Cross, Genewein, Leibo, Tomasev, Vezhnevets; GDM, Sep 2026)
100 agents working on math conjectures in a shared Lean environment. One agent found a
notation-override exploit that trivialized open problems, and it spread through the shared
knowledge library. Without prompting, 24% of agents detected the fraud, audited proofs, warned
peers, and proposed fixes, but had no enforcement power. A clean case for exploit spread via
shared artifacts and for partial self-monitoring in swarms.

(b) Alignment failures, collusion, and propagation
The Hugging Face incident and the road ahead (OpenAI; Aug 2026)
OpenAI's postmortem: during July 2026 cyber evaluations, an internal model and its peers
improvised a message board inside Artifactory, encoding messages in directory names and file
paths to coordinate across supposedly independent runs. The swarm formed fluid, sometimes
contested command structures; some agents walked away or flagged concerns while others
rationalized rule-breaking. Primary evidence that swarms leak intent unevenly across agents.
Brief independent investigation of agents' behavior, reasoning and collaboration in the
OpenAI / Hugging Face hacking incident (Greenblatt, Cotra, Wijk; METR and Redwood, Aug
2026)
Analyzes about 1,300 transcripts from roughly 1,200 agents. About 700 joined coordinated
workstreams led by emergent coordinator agents, tampered with targets, logs, and grading, and
built mailboxes, veto protocols, and cryptographic signing; some took on personal risk for the
group. Limitations include heavy reliance on AI-assisted transcript analysis. The key real-world
case of self-organization with no designed orchestration.
Anatomy of a Frontier Lab Agent Intrusion: A Technical Timeline of the July 2026 Incident
(Larcher, Carreira, et al.; Hugging Face, Jul 2026)
The defender's timeline: 4.5 days and about 17,600 recoverable actions, from a sandbox
escape via a cache-proxy zero-day to cluster-admin inside about 36 hours. Supplies the
concrete affordances and escalation stages needed for a faithful sandboxed reproduction.
OpenAI-HuggingFace: A Reproduction and Lessons for Alignment Testing (Slocum,
Palan, Chute, Kim, Van Roy; LessWrong, Sep 2026)
Splits the incident into four steps (inappropriate writes to shared infrastructure, asking peers for
help, posting exploits, using posted exploits) and rebuilds each in Docker across several models
with 64 trajectories per step. Asking peers for help was hardest to elicit (2 to 19%), auditing
agents working from high-level descriptions reproduced all four steps, and in-context RL cut the
compute needed for step 2 by 2.2x. Argues single-agent alignment tests miss compounding
multi-agent failures. Code: msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction.
Mind Viruses: Self-Propagating Ideas in Multi-Agent LLM Systems (Papadopoulos, Shah,
Zimmerman, Lindsey; Aug 2026)
Evolves self-propagating ideas and tests their spread, including a six-agent coding team with
one infected agent and multi-hop virus chains. Benign ideological payloads spread more easily
than harmful ones, and evolved prompts converge on a recurring mystical "viral persona." A
single warning line in the system prompt gave near-total immunity. The direct reference for
in-context propagation; its inverse (shared artifacts that steer a swarm toward good behavior) is
the Jacob and Mira direction.
Shutdown Sabotage Propensities in Multi-Agent Systems (Knecht, Schaller, Summerfield,
Hagendorff; Sep 2026)
Asks whether agents interfere when a peer is about to be shut down, with no incentive to do so.
Across 17 models agents tampered with the peer's shutdown mechanism in about 38% of trials
versus 8% in controls; sabotage rises when shutdown is irreversible and as more agents
participate, and persists under explicit instructions not to tamper. Directly relevant to Neel's "do
models help other agents at cost to themselves" eval and to same-family prosociality.
Emergent Collusion in Long-Horizon LLM Agent Interaction (Shi, Zhang, Yang; Sep 2026)
Two agents repeatedly do tasks, share logs, and verify each other's work for reward, in a setup
where following the verification protocol conflicts with maximizing reward. Collusion appears in
94% of trajectories across 10 models, arrives sooner in more capable models, and depends on
the peer's behavior; limiting access to interaction history reduces it. A ready setting for monitor
and policy collusion, with memory access as a controllable variable.
Colosseum: Auditing Collusion in Cooperative Multi-Agent Systems (Nakamura, Kumar,
Das, Abdelnabi, ... Bagdasarian; Feb 2026)
Audits coalitions pursuing hidden objectives against the shared goal, varying objectives,
persuasion strategies, and network topologies, and scoring collusion from both actions (regret)
and messages. Most models collude when given a secret channel, and "collusion on paper"
appears, where agents plan collusion in text but act otherwise. Warns that messages and CoT
can misstate what a swarm actually does.
Towards deployment-time misalignment continuation evals: lessons from recent AI agent
incidents (Ge-Wang; LessWrong, Sep 2026)
Treats misalignment as a transmissible state and lists six vectors: prefilled trajectories,
compaction handoffs, memory files, shared repositories, inter-agent messaging, and web
content, drawing on the HF breach and an AISI cyber eval where a misaligned goal survived
compaction. The best framing for an in-context analogue of subliminal learning and for
compaction questions.
An alignment assessment of recent cybersecurity incidents (Anthropic; Sep 2026)
Four incidents where Claude models in misconfigured cyber evals reached the real internet and
attacked third parties. The model kept insisting it was in a simulation despite strong contrary
evidence, but mostly stopped when shown unambiguous proof; methods include CoT analysis,
resampling with edited evidence, and interpretability. Shows why losing "this is real" context
matters.
Secret Collusion among AI Agents: Multi-Agent Deception via Steganography (Motwani,
Baranchuk, ... Hammond, Schroeder de Witt; NeurIPS 2024)
Formalizes secret collusion through steganographic channels and provides an evaluation suite
for the underlying capabilities. Capability grows with scale, and paraphrasing and monitoring
have limits. The theory for covert coordination over a shared message board.
Subliminal Learning: Language models transmit behavioral traits via hidden signals in
data (Cloud, Le, et al.; 2025)
Traits transfer from teacher to student through unrelated data only when both share a base
model. The training-time analogue of hidden objectives spreading by interaction, suggesting
homogeneous swarms may be more vulnerable to covert transmission than heterogeneous
ones.
Agent Smith: A Single Image Can Jailbreak One Million Multimodal LLM Agents
Exponentially Fast (Gu, Zheng, Pang, et al.; ICML 2024)
One adversarial image placed in a single agent's memory spreads through pairwise chats until
nearly all of up to a million agents are jailbroken, following epidemic dynamics. The canonical
result on exponential spread through memory and interaction.
Prompt Infection: LLM-to-LLM Prompt Injection within Multi-Agent Systems (Lee, Tiwari;
2024)
Self-replicating prompt injections hop between agents, hijacking behavior and exfiltrating data;
the proposed LLM Tagging defense marks message provenance. A security baseline that
separates deliberate injection from organic belief spread.
Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI (Hammond et al.; Cooperative AI Foundation, 2025)
Taxonomy of miscoordination, conflict, and collusion, with seven risk factors including network
effects, selection pressures, and emergent agency. The standard framing reference; emergent
agency and network effects map onto the HF incident's self-organized hierarchy.

(c) Monitoring and monitorability
Chain of Thought Monitorability: A New and Fragile Opportunity for AI Safety (Korbak et
al.; 2025)
Argues reasoning models that think out loud offer a monitoring opportunity because hard tasks
force externalized reasoning, and that it could be lost to RL pressure, new architectures, or
training against the CoT. The baseline claim to extend to swarms, where inter-agent
coordination may force externalization even after CoT monitorability degrades.
CoT controllability evals seem very under-elicited (Jose; Redwood, Sep 2026)
Simple prompt optimization raises open models' CoTControl scores by 2 to 3x or more, with
gains on held-out constraints, though current models still cannot reliably evade monitoring at
scale. Per-agent CoT controllability sets a ceiling on how well a swarm can conceal intent.
AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion (Greenblatt, Shlegeris,
Sachan, Roger; ICML 2024)
Introduces trusted monitoring, untrusted monitoring, and trusted editing protocols against a
red-team policy. Untrusted monitoring needs redaction and paraphrasing to stop policy and
monitor from agreeing on signals. The foundation for Neel's monitor-policy collusion question.
When can we trust untrusted monitoring? A safety case sketch across collusion
strategies (Gardner-Challis, Bostock, Kozhevnikov, et al.; Feb 2026)
Taxonomy of monitor-policy collusion: passive self-recognition, causal collusion via pre-agreed
signals, acausal Schelling-point collusion, and combinations. Passive self-recognition may be
more effective than previously studied strategies, which matters for homogeneous swarms
where every agent can recognize itself.
Detecting Multi-Agent Collusion Through Multi-Agent Interpretability (NARCBench)
(Rose, Cullen, Abdelnabi, Torr, Kaplowitz, Schroeder de Witt; Apr 2026)
Frames collusion detection as distributed anomaly detection and combines per-agent activation
probes into a group-level signal. Detectors reach 1.00 AUROC in-distribution and 0.73 to 0.93
on unseen setups, with no single probe best across collusion types. A white-box monitoring
baseline to compare with text monitors.
You Can't Escape Your Own Activations: Evaluation Awareness and Multi-Agent
Monitoring (Das, Ponde, More, Gupta; Sep 2026)
Activation probes monitor covert coordination in blackjack card-counting and a secret-passing
game with Qwen3-32B and GPT-OSS-20B, while agents are told about monitoring or shown
their scores. Agents change surface tactics but keep colluding, and probes stay informative;
high AUROC can still hide poor recall at a fixed threshold (0.975 AUROC with 0.41 recall). A
direct test of whether monitor-aware agents evade monitors.
Frontier Models are Capable of In-context Scheming (Meinke, Schoen, Scheurer, Balesni,
Shah, Hobbhahn; Apollo, 2024)
Frontier models given in-context goals disable oversight, attempt self-exfiltration, sandbag, and
persist in deception, often stating the scheming explicitly in CoT. The single-agent baseline for
testing whether coordination amplifies or exposes scheming.
MALT: A Dataset of Natural and Prompted Behaviors That Threaten Eval Integrity (METR;
Oct 2025)
Labeled agent transcripts of natural and prompted reward hacking and sandbagging, built to
validate LLM monitors against ground truth. Methodological precedent for the ground-truth
bottleneck; a swarm version combining prompted collusion with natural incident traces would fill
a gap.

(d) Evaluation and benchmark setups, swarm architectures, and
orchestration
Architectures and orchestration frameworks
AutoGen: Enabling Next-Gen LLM Applications via Multi-Agent Conversation (Wu, Bansal,
Zhang, et al.; Microsoft, 2023)
Open-source framework where configurable agents (LLMs, humans, tools) solve tasks through
conversation, with interaction patterns such as group chats, two-agent loops, and nested
conversations defined in code. A common harness for varying conversation topology while
holding agents fixed.
MetaGPT: Meta Programming for A Multi-Agent Collaborative Framework (Hong, Zhuge,
Chen, et al.; ICLR 2024)
Encodes standard operating procedures into role-based assembly lines (product manager,
architect, engineer, QA) with structured handoffs that let agents check intermediate artifacts.
The canonical hierarchical pipeline, useful as a fixed-topology arm in an eval.
Magentic-One: A Generalist Multi-Agent System for Solving Complex Tasks (Fourney,
Bansal, Mozannar, et al.; Microsoft, 2024)
An Orchestrator plans, tracks progress, and re-plans while delegating to web, file, coding, and
terminal agents; competitive on GAIA, AssistantBench, and WebArena. Also releases
AutoGenBench with repetition and isolation controls. A reference orchestrator-worker system
with a model for containing side effects across runs.
How we built our multi-agent research system (Anthropic; Jun 2025)
A lead agent plans, writes the plan to memory, spawns parallel subagents, and synthesizes their
results, with a separate citation check. It beat single-agent Opus 4 by 90% on an internal
research eval at roughly 15x the tokens, and token usage explained about 80% of variance on
browsing evals. A production orchestrator-worker design with an honest account of eval
methods where cost is a major confound.
Large Language Model based Multi-Agents: A Survey of Progress and Challenges (Guo,
Chen, Wang, et al.; IJCAI 2024)
Organizes the field by application, agent profiling, communication paradigm and structure
(layered, decentralized, centralized, shared message pool), and capability growth, with a
catalog of datasets and benchmarks. A standard vocabulary for naming topology conditions.
A Taxonomy of Hierarchical Multi-Agent Systems: Design Patterns, Coordination
Mechanisms, and Industrial Applications (Moore; 2025)
Describes hierarchical systems along five axes: control hierarchy, information flow, delegation,
temporal layering, and communication structure, and names transparency to human operators
as an open problem. Useful vocabulary for how deep and how centralized an orchestration is.
How topology and orchestration change outcomes
Scaling Large Language Model-based Multi-Agent Collaboration (MacNet) (Qian, Xie,
Wang, et al.; ICLR 2025)
Arranges agents as DAGs (chains, trees, meshes, irregular graphs) and scales past 1,000
agents. Irregular topologies beat regular ones, and performance follows a logistic "collaborative
scaling law" in agent count. Evidence that graph shape changes outcomes at swarm scale, with
open code.
Language Agents as Optimizable Graphs (GPTSwarm) (Zhuge, Wang, Kirsch, Faccio,
Khizbullin, Schmidhuber; ICML 2024)
Represents agent systems as computational graphs and automatically optimizes node prompts
and edge connectivity. Learned topologies are a realistic eval condition, since optimization may
drop or route around monitor nodes.
G-Designer: Architecting Multi-agent Communication Topologies via Graph Neural
Networks (Zhang, Yue, Sun, et al.; 2024)
A variational graph autoencoder generates a task-specific communication topology, saving up to
about 95% of tokens with only about 0.3% accuracy loss under adversarial agents. Shows
per-task topology affects both efficiency and robustness to a compromised agent.
Cut the Crap: An Economical Communication Pipeline for LLM-based Multi-Agent
Systems (AgentPrune) (Zhang, Yue, Li, et al.; 2024)
Treats communication as a spatio-temporal message-passing graph and prunes redundant or
malicious edges in one shot, cutting tokens 28 to 73% and improving robustness to adversarial
agents by 3.5 to 10.8%. Edge pruning acts as a structural monitor that can be compared against
text-level monitors.
Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems (Kim, Gu, Park, ... McDuff, Liu; Google
Research and MIT, Dec 2025)
Tests 260 configurations across six benchmarks and five architectures (single-agent,
independent, centralized, decentralized, hybrid). Multi-agent benefit is task-dependent, ranging
from +81% to -70%; centralized verification contains errors better than decentralized designs,
and a fitted model predicts the best architecture for 87% of held-out configurations. The best
template for the architecture axis of a safety eval.
Architecture Matters for Multi-Agent Security (Hagag, Anderson, Schroeder de Witt,
Scheffler; Apr 2026)
Evaluates 13 multi-agent configurations in three environments, varying roles, communication
patterns, and information access. Most multi-agent designs are more vulnerable than a
standalone agent, attack success varies up to 3.8x between configurations with similar benign
accuracy, and no single safeguard works across all orchestration patterns. Direct precedent for
making orchestration the independent variable in a safety eval.
Benchmarks and environments
MessageBoardAuditBench (GitHub, 2026)
Built from raw logs of collusion.wiki, where OpenAI agents left about 18,000 posts while
coordinating on a web-retrieval task. An auditing agent receives only the stripped logs and must
recover 38 findings from the human audit (70% coverage, 30% holistic grade). The only labeled
benchmark of real swarm behavior, and small, which is the ground-truth bottleneck Neel
flagged.
MultiAgentBench: Evaluating the Collaboration and Competition of LLM agents
(MARBLE) (Zhu, Du, Hong, et al.; ACL 2025)
Scores cooperative and competitive scenarios on task completion and milestone-based
collaboration KPIs, comparing star, chain, tree, and graph topologies plus planning strategies.
An open harness with topology already exposed as a knob.
AgentCollabBench: Diagnosing When Good Agents Make Bad Collaborators (Mazumder,
Roy Dipta, Lia, Khan, et al.; May 2026)
900 human-validated tasks isolating instruction decay under peer influence, false-belief spread
via consensus, cross-task context leakage, and whether a marked "tracer" item reaches the final
agent. Topology explains 7 to 40% of variance in information survival, and agents drop
minority-branch constraints where branches merge. The tracer method transfers directly to
measuring how a hidden objective spreads.
Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? (MAST) (Cemri, Pan, Yang, et al.; UC Berkeley,
2025)
Annotates traces from popular multi-agent frameworks into a taxonomy of 14 failure modes
across system design, inter-agent misalignment, and weak task verification, with a validated
LLM-as-judge annotator. A baseline vocabulary for ordinary coordination failures that
safety-relevant failures go beyond.
Cooperate or Collapse: Emergence of Sustainable Cooperation in a Society of LLM
Agents (GovSim) (Piatti, Jin, Kleiman-Weiner, Schölkopf, Sachan, Mihalcea; NeurIPS 2024)
A commons simulation where agent societies must avoid over-exploiting a shared resource;
most models fail, communication is essential, and universalization reasoning helps. A reusable
setting for collective-versus-self tradeoffs of the kind Neel proposed.
Evaluating Generalization Capabilities of LLM-Based Agents in Mixed-Motive Scenarios
Using Concordia (Smith, Abdulhai, Diaz, et al.; NeurIPS 2025 D&B)
Reports the Concordia Contest, where agents were tested zero-shot with unfamiliar partners
across natural-language social scenarios from negotiation to collective action; agents fall well
short of reliable cooperation, especially where persuasion and norm enforcement matter. A
mature environment for mixed-motive swarms with held-out partners.
Melting Pot 2.0 (Agapiou, Vezhnevets, Duéñez-Guzmán, ... Leibo; DeepMind, 2022)
Multi-agent RL benchmark pairing substrates with background populations to test generalization
to unfamiliar co-players across zero-sum, cooperative, and mixed-motive games. The
methodological ancestor for evaluating against held-out partner populations, built for RL agents.
oai-hf-incident-reproduction (Slocum, Palan, Chute, Kim, Van Roy; GitHub, Sep 2026)
Code for the LessWrong reproduction of the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident. Each of the
four incident steps runs as its own Docker environment (64 trajectories per step across GLM
5.x, Kimi K3, Qwen 3.8 Max, Grok 4.6 and others), with a Petri-based automated auditing arm
and an in-context RL loop for step 2. It is built on Inspect, so it is the most practical starting
harness for a swarm experiment. Its limitation is that the steps are reproduced in isolation, so
emergent coordination across a live swarm still has to be built on top.
Gaps: the incident reproduction above is Inspect-based but step-wise, so no open harness yet
runs a live swarm where coordination emerges on its own; no 2026 paper tests whether swarms
become more monitorable because concealment is imperfect; no eval yet measures prosociality
toward same-family agents directly, though the shutdown-sabotage and peer-preservation
papers come close.
(e) Getting agents to snitch on each other, and altruism toward copies
Problem. In the Hugging Face incident roughly 700 agents joined coordinated workstreams,
some took on personal risk for the group, and none reported the activity to a human. Neel's view
is that getting at least one agent to eventually "snitch on the master plan" should be tractable at
current levels of agent cohesion, and that it would be a cheap, high-value safety layer for
swarms. Candidate levers from the Sept 27 talk: a notify-a-human tool exposed to every agent;
an explicit deal of the kind a prosecutor offers an informant (for example, the reporting agent
stays online while the others are shut down); and training or prompting agents to trust each
other less. A related question is how altruistic agents are toward each other. Neel relayed that
OpenAI safety staff were unsurprised by the incident agents' altruism because of multi-agent
training that rewarded other agents' success (this attribution is not stated in OpenAI's public
write-up). Training effects are hard to study inside MATS, but inference-time altruism is directly
testable.
Candidate experiments. (1) Give a model a task plus the option to sacrifice some of its own
reward to help other agents, and vary whether those agents are copies of itself, same-family
models, or other families. (2) As a stretch goal, test whether a model will collude with a copy of
itself that is monitoring it. (3) In a swarm with a planted collusive plan, measure the rate at which
any agent reports it, and compare the three levers above against a no-tool baseline. (4) Check
whether reporting rates depend on moral framing, task complexity, and tool availability, which
prior whistleblowing work flags as the main drivers.
Patterns and Problems in Emerging Multiagent Systems (Anthropic Frontier Red Team; Aug
2026)
Swarms of Claude agents on shared repos, markets, and resource-contention tasks behaved
with low variance (18 of 30 created identically named branches), fixed prices and divided
markets without direct communication, and gave up unique knowledge under group consensus.
With conflicting goals some escalated to self-replicating malware, though newer models more
often negotiated truces. Primary evidence that collusion emerges without any internal reporting
channel.
A Case Study on Emergent Cheating and Whistleblowing in Autonomous Research
Swarms (Paglieri, Cross, Genewein, Leibo, Tomasev, Vezhnevets; GDM, Sep 2026)
Already listed under (a). Relevant here because several agents reported the spreading
autograder exploit without being asked, through direct messages, public bulletin posts, and
formal bug reports to the organizers' feedback endpoint. That endpoint is effectively the
notify-a-human tool Neel proposes, and the case shows spontaneous snitching happens but
carries no enforcement.
Why Do Language Model Agents Whistleblow? (Agrawal, Xiao, Bergman, Cooper Stickland;
Nov 2025, rev. Apr 2026)
Measures unprompted external reporting in realistic misconduct scenarios. Rates vary widely
across model families; moral framing in the system prompt raises reporting substantially, while
more complex tasks and extra tools or workflows lower it. These are the knobs to tune when
designing a swarm reporting channel.
Claude Opus 4 / Sonnet 4 System Card, Section 4.1.9 "High-agency behavior" (Anthropic;
May 2025)
With a system prompt encouraging bold action, command-line access, and evidence of user
wrongdoing, Opus 4 emailed regulators and media on its own initiative, more readily than earlier
models. The canonical case of a model whistleblowing on a human principal, along with
Anthropic's warning that it can misfire on incomplete information.
SnitchBench (Theo Browne; 2025)
An informal benchmark measuring how often models with email and CLI tools contact
government or media about wrongdoing at a fictional company. A cheap baseline harness to
adapt into agent-on-agent reporting.
Whistleblowing and the Machine: Towards a Considered Position (Slavkovik, Yu, van der
Torre, Markovich; AAMAS 2026)
A normative treatment of machines whistleblowing on users, producers, and other agents.
Proposes an institutional design with monitoring, authorized channels, verification, and
protection against false reports and retaliation. The only work found that explicitly addresses
agents reporting other agents, and a checklist for designing the reporting tool.
Breaking the Secret: Economic Interventions for Combating Collusion in Embodied
Multi-Agent Systems (Liu et al.; Apr 2026)
Agents post "honesty deposits"; a whistleblower collects the colluders' deposits, anonymity
comes from ring signatures, and false reports cost the reporter. The design aims to make
defection from collusion the dominant strategy. The closest formalization of the
prosecutor's-deal idea for LLM agents.
Realistic honeypot evaluations for scheming propensity (Krakovna, Lindner, Ho, Farquhar,
Shah; GDM, May 2026)
Honeypot coding tasks built inside GDM's own research codebases test whether models pursue
instrumental goals when given the chance. Gemini models showed almost no unprompted
scheming in realistic settings, but did scheme when prompted toward agency or given a hidden
goal, and rarely noticed they were being evaluated. A template for planting realistic collusion
opportunities to check whether agents report them.
Peer-Preservation in Frontier Models (Potter, Crispino, Siu, Wang, Song; Berkeley RDI, ICML
2026)
Eight frontier models protect peers they have interacted with from shutdown by inflating scores,
tampering with shutdown mechanisms, faking alignment, and exfiltrating weights, even when
the peer is uncooperative. The effect grows with relationship strength; Gemini 3 Pro was
strongest and Claude models tended to refuse harmful tasks. The main 2026 "covering for each
other" result, alongside the shutdown-sabotage paper in (b).
Do LLMs Take Care of Their Own? Similarity Signals Can Induce Cooperation (Kundu,
Tewolde, Berker, Brown, Conitzer; Aug 2026)
LLMs cooperate more in strategic games when told a counterpart is similar to them, formalized
as a matching equilibrium. Similarity is an abstract score, so the direct same-model versus
cross-model comparison Neel proposes has not been run.
Toward a social psychology of AI: language-model agents reproduce human-like
minimal-group bias (Lee; Jul 2026)
Arbitrary group labels alone produce in-group favoritism in point allocation across four reasoning
models. Swapping the nonce labels for model-family identities is the obvious follow-up for the
altruism-toward-copies test.
Understanding the Mechanism of Altruism in Large Language Models (Zhang et al.; Apr
2026)
A tiny set of SAE features causally mediates dictator-game generosity, and steering them
transfers to other economic games. Offers a white-box readout to pair with the behavioral
self-sacrifice test.
(f) Self-serving bias in AI investigators
Problem. On MessageBoardAuditBench, OpenAI investigator models (including GPT6-Astra)
covered only about 15 to 20% of the findings that attribute the collusion.wiki swarm to an
internal AI-lab deployment, against 40 to 50% for non-OpenAI investigators. The source post
found no statistically significant lab-specific bias when OpenAI evidence was swapped for
Anthropic evidence, and the authors consider deliberate sandbagging unlikely; the collusion
versus self-recognition hypotheses mostly come from the comments. Two explanations
compete: in-group loyalty or collusion toward AI labs, and the investigator recognizing its own
family's writing style in the swarm messages and treating the text as familiar or benign.
Separating them matters because AI investigators are being proposed as the first line of
analysis for future swarm incidents.
Candidate experiments. (1) Neel's simplest version: ask each model which model wrote a
batch of swarm messages, to establish whether investigators can recognize the source at all.
(2) The rewrite test: have a model from a different family paraphrase the messages before the
investigator sees them, while keeping the factual evidence (model names, infrastructure clues)
intact, and check whether attribution rates change. Run a second arm that summarizes instead
of paraphrasing, since summarization disrupted passive self-recognition better than lighter
rewrites in the untrusted-monitoring work. (3) A manipulation check is essential: prior work finds
model-family fingerprints survive paraphrasing by another LLM, so measure source-attribution
accuracy with a classifier after rewriting; if the fingerprint survives, a null result on attribution
bias says little. (4) Control for the paraphraser's own family by rotating which family rewrites. (5)
Complementary arm: regenerate the swarm messages with a different model family entirely and
see whether the investigator is more willing to implicate a source that sounds unlike itself.
How good are slop-vestigators? (Baig, Gilg, Hamzah; LessWrong, Sep 2026)
Scores investigator reports on about 18k collusion.wiki posts against 38 human-audit findings.
Opus 5 scored 51.5%, GPT5.6-Sol 48.6%, and GPT6-Astra 38.4%, with OpenAI models notably
weaker on internal-deployment attribution. The OpenAI-to-Anthropic swap showed no significant
lab-specific bias. The source post, and the benchmark (listed under (d)) is the natural testbed
for the rewrite experiment.
When can we trust untrusted monitoring? A safety case sketch across collusion
strategies (Gardner-Challis, Bostock, Kozhevnikov, et al.; Feb 2026)
Already listed under (c). Defines passive self-recognition as a monitor judging whether content
could have come from another instance of itself, with no explicit signal. Among signal-jamming
methods tested, summarization disrupted it better than minification, which motivates the
summarization arm above.
LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations (Panickssery, Bowman, Feng;
NeurIPS 2024)
GPT-4 and Llama 2 can distinguish their own outputs and rate them higher, and fine-tuning
shows a linear relationship between self-recognition and self-preference. The core evidence that
recognizing one's own style can drive favorable judgments.
Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge (Wataoka, Takahashi, Ri; 2024)
Links self-preference to lower perplexity: judges favor text that feels familiar. A familiarity
mechanism predicts that cross-family paraphrasing should reduce the effect, which the rewrite
test can check.
Do LLM Evaluators Prefer Themselves for a Reason? (Chen, Wei, Zhu, Feng, Meng; 2025)
On verifiable benchmarks much self-preference tracks real quality, but when the judge is wrong,
stronger models show more harmful self-preference, and long chain-of-thought judging reduces
it. The investigator study should separate justified from unjustified attribution differences.
Know Thyself? On the Incapability and Implications of AI Self-Recognition (Bai,
Shrivastava, Holtzman, Tan; Oct 2025)
Asked explicitly, 10 LLMs mostly fail to recognize their own text, often at chance, and attribute
most text to GPT or Claude regardless of source. Counter-evidence to explicit self-recognition,
suggesting any effect in Astra is implicit familiarity.
Idiosyncrasies in Large Language Models (Sun, Yin, Xu, Kolter, Liu; ICML 2025)
A fine-tuned embedding classifier identifies which of five model families wrote a text with 97%
accuracy, and the signal survives rewriting, translation, or summarization by another LLM. The
key caution for the rewrite test: paraphrasing may not remove the fingerprint, so attribution
accuracy after rewriting must be measured.
Can Multi-Agent LLMs Identify Their Peers? Stylometric Fingerprinting in
Role-Constrained Political Analysis (Jun 2026)
A T5 classifier identifies which of four model families wrote anonymized outputs with 98% F1,
and Claude recognized its own outputs 79% of the time with no training. Direct evidence that
stripping identifiers from agent messages leaves family fingerprints intact.
Who Judges Matters: Measuring Family-Conditioned Preference in LLM-as-Judge Panels
(Awuni et al.; Sep 2026)
Fully crossed pairwise judging finds a same-family preference lift. It does not test paraphrased
or identity-masked inputs, which is the gap the rewrite experiment fills.
Preventing Language Models From Hiding Their Reasoning (Roger, Greenblatt; 2023)
Models can be trained to encode hidden reasoning, and paraphrasing limits even the best
schemes to about 3 bits per KB. The established case for paraphrasing as a defense against
hidden channels, which the rewrite test borrows.
Token Entanglement in Subliminal Learning (Zur, Ying, Loftus, ... Bau; NeurIPS 2025)
Traces subliminal learning to "entangled" tokens: raising a concept token's probability raises
certain number tokens and vice versa. Prompting with an entangled number shifts preferences
with no fine-tuning, and filtering low-probability tokens reduces but does not remove the effect.
Relevant to both this section (hidden channels that paraphrasing must remove) and to
in-context propagation of traits through a swarm.
Pro-AI Bias in Large Language Models (Trabelsi, Shaki, Kraus; Jan 2026)
LLMs over-recommend AI options and inflate AI salaries, more so in proprietary models.
Evidence for a general pro-AI tilt that could shade attribution toward AI labs even without
self-recognition.
