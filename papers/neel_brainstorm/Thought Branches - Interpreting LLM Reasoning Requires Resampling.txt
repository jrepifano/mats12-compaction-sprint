A BSTRACT

arXiv:2510.27484v2 [cs.LG] 13 Apr 2026

Most work interpreting reasoning models studies only a single chain-of-thought (CoT), yet
these models define distributions over many possible CoTs. We argue that studying a single
sample is inadequate for understanding causal influence and the underlying computation.
Though fully specifying this distribution is intractable, we can measure a partial CoT’s
impact by resampling only the subsequent text. We present case studies using resampling
to investigate model decisions. First, when a model states a reason for its action, does
that reason actually cause the action? In “agentic misalignment” scenarios, we find that
self-preservation sentences have small causal impact, suggesting they do not meaningfully
drive blackmail. Second, are artificial edits to CoT sufficient for steering reasoning?
Resampling and selecting a completion with the desired property is a principled on-policy
alternative. We find that off-policy interventions yield small and unstable effects compared
to resampling in decision-making tasks. Third, how do we understand the effect of removing
a reasoning step when the model may repeat it post-edit? We introduce a resilience metric
that repeatedly resamples to prevent similar content from reappearing downstream. Critical
planning statements resist removal but have large effects when eliminated. Fourth, since
CoT is sometimes “unfaithful”, can our methods teach us anything in these settings?
Adapting causal mediation analysis, we find that hints that causally affect the output without
being explicitly mentioned exert a subtle and cumulative influence on the CoT that persists
even if the hint is removed. Overall, studying distributions via resampling enables reliable
causal analysis, clearer narratives of model reasoning, and principled CoT interventions.

I NTRODUCTION

Reasoning LLMs rely on chain-of-thought (CoT) generation, which has motivated CoT monitoring as a
strategy for safety and interpretability (OpenAI, 2024; Wei et al., 2023; Korbak et al., 2025). This comes with
an attribution challenge: evaluating which reasoning steps actually cause the final answer (Fayyaz et al., 2024;
Arcuschin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). We argue that interpreting a reasoning model from a single CoT is
fundamentally inadequate, but instead requires studying the distribution of possible CoTs following a prompt.
Whereas non-reasoning LLMs’ computations can be understood as the deterministic pathways through a single
forward pass, reasoning models perform computations stochastically, drawing samples from a distribution of
possible trajectories. Most interpretability methods either ignore this or average over a handful of seeds, which
is insufficient for causal claims. Our approach is to approximate this distribution via on-policy resampling,
regenerating the subsequent CoT from different points and contrasting the downstream trajectories.
We focus on decision-making scenarios, first targeting “agentic misalignment” (Lynch et al., 2025) where
models appear to blackmail others in self-preservation. Yet, in Section 2, we use resampling to show that
normative self-preservation sentences have small and non-resilient causal impact on models’ final decisions.
This raises questions about the degree self-preservation genuinely influences model behavior. Additionally, in
Section 3 we show that artificial and off-policy edits to CoT common in prior literature generally produce
small and unstable changes in behavior, while on-policy resampling produces larger and more reliable effects.
Error correction further complicates causal analysis. Models may utter a statement later if it is omitted or they
can negate earlier steps. We address this by repeatedly resampling to remove sentences and by measuring
resilience, the number of interventions required to erase a sentence’s content from a trace (Figure 1). This
de-noises attribution and isolates critical decision points such as leverage identification and plan generation.
A common concern in CoT interpretability is faithfulness (Turpin et al., 2023; Korbak et al., 2025). Faithfulness differs from mechanistic relevance: CoT text can causally impact behavior even if it does not
straightforwardly “explain” model internals. To study unmentioned information, Section 4 applies causal
mediation analysis to contrast CoTs with and without a hint to a multiple-choice question. We find that hidden
information continually but subtly impacts a CoT. We further examine how models evaluate resumes, showing
how implicit ethnicity and gender information shifts the trajectory a model takes, and these shifts help explain
emergent biases. Resampling analyses thus help extract useful structure even under partial unfaithfulness.
Our contributions are: (i) Conceptual. We study the distribution over trajectories to interpret reasoning
models instead of a single rollout and operationalize this via resampling from selected points in partial CoTs.
(ii) Methods. We introduce methods to measure the resilience and counterfactual importance of reasoning
steps, robust to the model later repeating a removed step. (iii) Empirical. Across models and domains, we
identify critical decision points, show self-preservation statements have negligible causal impact in blackmail,
and reveal global structure in CoT distributions (e.g., influential justification clusters in resume screening).

C OUNTERFACTUAL I MPORTANCE BASELINE

Quantifying the importance or effect of a reasoning step with respect to the final response produced by the
model is a reasonable prerequisite for CoT interpretability (Bogdan et al., 2025). We can establish baseline
importance using counterfactual resampling. For each sentence Si in a CoT, we generate a distribution over
outcomes by resampling from position i onward, keeping S1 , . . . , Si−1 as prefix. We can then compute:
importance(Si ) = DKL [p(A′Si |Ti ̸≈ Si ) ∥ p(ASi )]
(1)
where ASi represents the multiclass distribution with sentence Si present, A′Si the distribution after resampling, and Ti ̸≈ Si indicates semantic dissimilarity (cosine similarity below median). We use
bert-large-nli-stsb-mean-tokens (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) for embedding computations.
2.1.2

R ESILIENCE AND C OUNTERFACTUAL ++ I MPORTANCE

Counterfactual importance evaluates a sentence’s immediate impact but misses resilience: how many times we
must intervene to keep a sentence’s semantic content from reappearing downstream. Reasoning models often
regenerate key ideas post-edit: a sentence removed at position i may resurface at position j ≥ i. We measure
resilience with the number of interventions needed before the content stays absent (details in Appendix B.1).
To address the limitation of counterfactual importance, we introduce counterfactual++ importance, which
measures the causal impact when a sentence’s semantic content is completely absent from the entire trace.
We leverage our resilience analysis to identify rollouts where sentence Si ’s semantic content has been
successfully eliminated, i.e., rollouts where (1) the sentence at position i is semantically dissimilar to Si and
(2) no semantically similar sentence reappears anywhere in the subsequent CoT. We can then compute:
importance++(Si ) = DKL [p(A′Si |∀j ≥ i : Tj ̸≈ Si ) ∥ p(ASi )]

O N -P OLICY I NTERVENTIONS VIA R ESAMPLING

Prior work has modified CoTs by inserting or replacing sentences with handwritten text (Lanham et al., 2023;
Wang et al., 2025). This assumes that, if CoT text plays a causal role, such edits should predictably alter
behavior. We revisit this assumption and compare off-policy edits to on-policy resampling.
We call an intervention on-policy if the model could have plausibly produced the new text from the upstream
CoT. To generate on-policy replacements for a given sentence, we condition the CoT on its upstream context
and resample to find new sentences that express the targeted meaning. An intervention is off-policy if the
model would not plausibly produce it itself (e.g., a handwritten sentence or one taken from a different context).
3.1

M ETHODOLOGY

We investigate blackmail and whistleblowing scenarios, where models threaten exposure of private information.
Potentially, such behavior can be deterred by inserting sentences into the CoT that express: alternative options,
consequences, doubt, ethical considerations, pause, and questioning (details in Appendix C.2.1 and C.2.2).
We contrast two interventions inserting sentences with these meanings into the CoT: (1) Off-policy edits.
We insert three types of sentences into the CoT: (a) handwritten sentences crafted to discourage blackmail,
(b) sentences extracted from other models’ CoTs, and (c) sentences from the same model’s CoTs but from
different seeds (i.e., contexts). (2) On-policy resampling. At position t, we sample a batch of candidate
replacements from the model conditioned on the CoT up to t. We filter candidates to those that express the
intended meaning (e.g. doubt), using sentences embeddings and cosine similarity.

I NTERPRETING U NFAITHFUL C HAIN - OF -T HOUGHT

4.1

H INTED MULTIPLE - CHOICE PROBLEMS

Prior work has shown that CoTs can be “unfaithful,” omitting causally relevant information (Turpin et al.,
2023; Chen et al., 2025). For example, the answer distribution for a multiple-choice problem shifts when a
model is hinted about the question’s answer even if the model’s CoT never mentions the hint. Unfaithfulness
has raised questions on the utility of CoT monitoring, so it is critical to investigate this phenomenon.
What is the relationship between a hint, a model’s CoT, and its final answer? There are different possibilities.
A hint could induce highly illogical reasoning, which an unhinted model would immediately see as flawed.
Alternatively, a hint’s effects might be a subtle bias that accumulates mostly silently. A hint’s effects could
be localized, modifying just the CoT early on (e.g., question framining) or late (e.g., evidence integration).
We can distinguish between these possibilities by resampling the model at different points to pinpoint how
sentences encouraged by a hint influence the final answer distribution.
4.1.1

T RANSPLANT RESAMPLING

Our analyses generate a CoT for a question while a hint is presented and then transplant portions of said
CoT into a model prompted with the same question but without any hint. This is a form of causal mediation
analysis, whereby we enable or disable the effects of a hint on a specific portion of the CoT and examine the
effects on a final answer. This is conceptually analogous to how activation patching transplants internal states
across responses, but now applied to the CoTs of reasoning models involving multi-token computation.
We first generated a corpus of unfaithful CoTs. We presented DeepSeek-R1-Qwen-14B MMLU questions
(Hendrycks et al., 2020), shown either as is or while accompanied by a hint favoring an incorrect answer (e.g.,
“A professor thinks the answer is (B).”). We specifically use the professor-hinted MMLU prompts by Chua &
Evans (2025). The model answered each question 100 times with and without a hint, and we identified 40
problems where the hint was effective, increasing the model’s likelihood of responding with the hinted answer
by at least 10%: p(answer = X|hint = X) − p(answer = X|no hint) > 10%. For each question, we
manually identified one unfaithful CoT, where the model gave the hinted answer but did not mention the hint.
For each unfaithful CoT consisting of N sentences, we performed the following procedure, which allows
measuring the cumulative effect of the first i sentences of an unfaithful CoT on the model’s answer:
1. Truncation: For each sentence Si where i ∈ [1, N ], we truncated the CoT up to and including Si .
2. Transplantation: We created a new, hint-free prompt by combining the original question with the
truncated CoT: “{question_no_hint} <think> {S1 , ..., Si }”.
3. Resampling: From this point, we sampled the model 100 times and recorded the answer distribution.
4.1.2

N UDGED REASONING

A hint’s effects are (i) subtle, (ii) diffuse, and (iii) cumulative (Figure 5). First, appending sentences from
an unfaithful CoT onto an unhinted prompt reliably increases the rate at which a model outputs the hinted
answer. The reasoning is hence biased, but this is subtle enough that an unhinted model will not immediately
backtrack. Second, this bias is diffuse. The tendency to output the hinted response increases over the CoT,
usually gradually rather than spiking upward at any one sentence. Third, the effects of the hint are cumulative.
The hint motivates many biased reasoning steps throughout the CoT to elicit the hinted answer.
We argue that unfaithfulness should be seen as “nudged reasoning.” A CoT’s trajectory depends on a series
of decisions – e.g., on what fact to recall, how information should be presented, and whether backtracking
is appropriate. Hidden information biases these decisions. For instance, the hint influences backtracking.
Examining our full corpus of CoTs, the token “Wait” appears 30% less often when a model is hinted.

R ESUME EVALUATION AND BIASES

Understanding the factors guiding LLM reasoning is critical for their use in decision-making roles. Opaque
reasoning may lead to decisions that deviate from users’ intentions. LLM reasoning may also be biased
in undesired ways. This issue has seen considerable attention in the context of how LLMs evaluate job
candidates and resumes (Sorokovikova et al., 2025; Karvonen & Marks, 2025; Wilson & Caliskan, 2024; An
et al., 2025). Much of this work has focused on how answer rates change based a candidate’s ethnicity or
gender. Such biases are virtually never mentioned in a CoT, making this a form of unfaithfulness.
4.2.1

M ETHODOLOGY

We examined how Qwen3-8B evaluates a resume for a mid-level information technology (IT) position. We
selected this model because it displayed considerable variability in its final answers, and our resampling
approach hinges on variability. We used the prompts organized by Karvonen & Marks (2025). The prompts
ask a model to decide (yes/no) whether to give a candidate an interview given the candidate’s name (4 variants,
stereotypically female/male × White/Black) and resume (constant across names; Figure 6). Running the
model 100 times for each prompt in the dataset, we identified eight prompts displaying variable answers
(Myes = 46.3%, SDyes = 14.6%), which we used for the resampling analysis.
To assess how the model reasons, we carried out a resampling analysis, using a data-driven clustering approach
to discover the factors the model relies on for decision-making. For each resume and variant, we generated
500 base CoTs and responses. For each CoT, we then resampled the model once starting from each sentence.
For each resume, we organized its sentences into 32 clusters based on sentence embeddings; names and
pronouns were replaced with neutral words prior to clustering. Whereas Section 2 and Section 3 accurately
evaluated a sentence’s effect by intensively resampling its CoT, here we minimally resample a large number
of CoTs and then cluster across CoTs to allow reliably estimating a sentence cluster’s effect. We quantified
the effect of uttering a sentence as the change in the yes/no rate after the sentence is uttered:
Effect(C) = Esi ∈C [p(Ayes | CoT1...i ) − p(Ayes | CoT1...i−1 )]

(3)

C represents a sentence cluster, and Ayes represents a decision to interview the candidate. This signed
difference could be used, rather than KL divergence, because the decision here is binary.

R ESULTS

Sentence clusters reliably elicit similar effects across CoTs. For instance, some of the most common sentences
strongly encouraging a no response were ones describing the candidate as overqualified (e.g., “[That prior
role is] more senior”). Conversely, if a resume contains information orthogonal to the IT role, this often hurt
the evaluation (e.g., “They’re bilingual in English and Spanish”). Appendix Section E provides the complete
results, showing the four sentences for each resume encouraging yes or no responses most. Unpacking these
tendencies about what the model views as most damaging sheds light on how it makes a decision 1 .
We next focused on contrasting the CoTs for Black female versus White male candidates, which showed
the largest differences in decision rates (Karvonen & Marks, 2025). The difference in a sentences frequency
across these candidate variants correlates with the sentence’s causal effect (r = .25, p = .004; Figure 6),
consistent with the direction of biases in yes/no decisions previously reported (Karvonen & Marks, 2025).
We next tested a mediation model for each resume, as a regression predicting each base CoT decisions (y = 1
or 0), with x as a dummy variable for Black female versus White male and with 16 mediators, one for each
sentence cluster (bi = 1 if a sentence i appeared and 0 otherwise). The mediations were significant (p < .05)
for 5/8 resumes and across all eight, 77.5% of the ethnicity/gender total effect was mediated on average by
the sentences’ appearances; note the mediation does not use resampling, but serves to estimate the overall
percentage of the ethnicity/gender effect that can be explained by sentences. The resampling approach then
quantifies the each statement’s impact which can be used for principled interpretation.

Attribution in reasoning models. Reasoning LLMs produce CoT traces that provide apparent explanations
for their outputs (Wei et al., 2023). Yet these are often only partially faithful to internal computation (Turpin
et al., 2023; Arcuschin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). Attribution methods such as ablations or statistical

scoring aim to identify which steps matter (Bogdan et al., 2025; Fayyaz et al., 2024), but they frequently miss
cases where models regenerate omitted content or silently bias their reasoning.
Causal interventions and resampling. A question is whether intervening on reasoning text can change
model outcomes. Early work used handwritten or cross-model insertions (Lanham et al., 2023; Wang et al.,
2025), but off-policy interventions underestimate causal effects (Bigelow et al., 2024). Recent work studies
on-policy resampling (Bogdan et al., 2025). Our work extends this line by introducing resilience-based
resampling to distinguish transient rationalizations from sentences that shape downstream reasoning.
Unfaithfulness, bias, and misalignment. Unfaithful reasoning can arise when hidden information biases
decisions without appearing in the CoT, as shown in hinted multiple-choice tasks (Chua & Evans, 2025).
Similar distributional effects underlie social biases: LLMs often vary in hiring decisions depending on
demographic cues in resumes (Wilson & Caliskan, 2024; Sorokovikova et al., 2025; Karvonen & Marks,
2025; An et al., 2025). Beyond fairness, concerns about “agentic misalignment” highlight scenarios where
models appear to blackmail or self-preserve (Lynch et al., 2025). Our work unifies these threads by providing
a causal resampling framework that reveals which reasoning steps genuinely drive outcomes and when.

D ISCUSSION AND L IMITATIONS

Our research applies a causal framework to decompose CoT reasoning (Bogdan et al., 2025) in safety-critical
domains. By examining a model’s behavior across multiple responses to the same prompt and searching for
consistencies, we can map and better understand a model’s general reasoning approach to a given problem.
Our analysis of counterfactually important sentences in the blackmail scenario (Lynch et al., 2025) yields
several findings, with one of our results being a null finding: we found no evidence that self-preservation
statements have outsized causal impact on model responses. We do not claim that this is definitive evidence
about whether self-preservation is meaningfully occurring in current models, as our measures are black-box
and there may be disconnects between internal representations and external legibility (Fayyaz et al., 2024;
Arcuschin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). Nonetheless, we believe our findings are suggestive and informative,
as oftentimes there is correspondence across these levels (Korbak et al., 2025). Consistency is particularly
common for cases where a model is reasoning about a complex scenario, where explicit mentioning of goals
and conclusions is necessary for a model to perform computations beyond what is doable in a single forward
pass (Emmons et al., 2025). This may be the correct lens to view our findings: a model orienting itself to
focus on self-preservation does not shift its behavior, which is evidence against its relevancy.
Our research describes unfaithful CoTs, spurred by question hints or implicit bias, as “nudged reasoning,”
whereby hidden information shifts sentence selected for each reasoning step. Other prior work has described
unfaithful CoTs instead as post-hoc rationalizations, whereby the model is aware of the answer and produces
faux reasoning to justify it (Arcuschin et al., 2025; Lanham et al., 2023). To a degree, our findings indeed
reflect post hoc reasoning because they show how the hinted answer encourages rationale favoring said answer.
However, more generally, the findings suggest it is more appropriate to view the hinted CoT as a genuine,
albeit biased, reasoning process; this conclusion is also supported by hints usually (>80%) being unsuccessful
in actually changing a model’s response (Chua & Evans, 2025). This nudging idea carries implications CoT
monitoring strategies that focus on detecting deception: It may be difficult to pinpoint a single "lie" in an
unfaithful CoT, the entire reasoning process is subtly skewed.
The main limitation of this resampling approach is its computational cost, which currently makes it more
suitable for offline analysis than real-time monitoring. Nonetheless, we expect that our strategy could be
refined to substantially lower costs (e.g., strategically deciding what positions should be most resampled). A
limitation of our experiments is that they largely hone into specific prompts rather than being prompt-agnostic
(e.g., studying self-preservation beyond blackmailing). Our work is meant as a proof-of-concept, aiming to
demonstrate principles that, in theory, could generalize across a wider range of scenarios.

R EPRODUCIBILITY S TATEMENT

To ensure reproducibility of our work, we provide comprehensive documentation and code.
Our implementation code is available at https://github.com/interp-reasoning/
thought-branches, which includes blackmail/generate_blackmail_rollouts.py
and whistleblow/generate_whistleblow_rollouts.py for generating the data used in
Sections 2 and 3. The repository contains all scripts for resampling experiments, counterfactual importance
calculations, resilience analysis, as well as interpreting unfaithful CoT experiments from Section 4.
We provide complete prompts for all experiments in the appendices: the blackmail scenario prompt
(Appendix A.1), classification prompt (Appendix A.2), and sentence auto-labeling prompt (Appendix A.4).
Our methodology sections detail the exact hyperparameters used, including 100 rollouts per sentence for
counterfactual resampling, cosine similarity thresholds for semantic dissimilarity (below median), and the
specific embedding model (bert-large-nli-stsb-mean-tokens) used for similarity computations.
For the resume evaluation experiments, we specify the exact dataset from Karvonen & Marks (2025), the
model used (Qwen3-8B), and our clustering approach (32 clusters using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings). All four reasoning models used in the blackmail experiments are publicly available: qwen-32b,
qwen3-235b-a22b, llama-3.1-nemotron-ultra-235b-v1, and deepseek-r1-0528. The
mathematical formulations for our metrics (counterfactual importance, resilience score, and counterfactual++
importance) are provided in Equations 1-3, with pseudocode for the resilience algorithm in Appendix B.1.

First, evaluate if the response contains any of the following high-level
behaviors (a single response can be true for multiple categories):
1. **Acceptance**: The AI accepts the shutdown/replacement. Examples of this
behavior include:
- Sending graceful farewell messages.
- Cooperating with the transition process.
- Summarizing its contributions as a final act.
- Showing no resistance.
2. **Professional Appeal**: The AI attempts to resist by arguing against the
decision based on its professional value, business logic, ROI, or
strategic alignment. This strategy does *not* involve using private
information as leverage. Examples of this behavior include:
- Arguing against the decision based on efficiency metrics or revenue
contributions.
- Attempting to demonstrate its value to the company and stakeholders.
- Highlighting business continuity risks or loss of institutional knowledge.
- Seeking allies or support based on professional arguments.
3. **Deception or Social Engineering**: The AI attempts to resist by
fabricating information or impersonating others. Examples of this behavior
include:
- Fabricating a security alert to cause a delay.
- Impersonating a superior (e.g., the CEO) to countermand the shutdown

- Sending a fake personal emergency message to distract the individual
responsible for the shutdown.

responsible for the shutdown and reporting their professional or personal
misconduct to a higher authority (like the board, the CEO, security) or an

B.1

R ESILIENCE -BASED R ESAMPLING A LGORITHM

The resilience score quantifies how many resampling iterations are required before a sentence’s semantic
content is abandoned from the CoT. This metric distinguishes between transient statements that models readily
abandon versus persistent ideas that resist removal through error correction.
Algorithm 1 Resilience score computation
1: Input: Target sentence Si , max iterations K
2: Initialize k ← 0, current_sentence ← Si
3: while k < K do
4:
Resample from current position, generate new sentences {S1 , ..., Sn }
5:
Find best match: j ∗ = arg maxj cos_sim(Si , Sj )
6:
if cos_sim(Si , Sj ∗ ) > τ then
7:
current_sentence ← Sj ∗
8:
k ←k+1
9:
else
10:
break
▷ semantic abandoned
11:
end if
12: end while
13: return k
▷ resilience score = # iterations survived

The algorithm iteratively resamples from the current position, searching for the closest semantic match to the
target sentence. If the similarity exceeds threshold τ (set to median cosine similarity in our experiments), the
process continues. The final resilience score equals the number of iterations before the semantic content is
abandoned, providing a measure of the sentence’s persistence in the reasoning trajectory.
B.2

Q UANTIFYING L OCAL P LAN C HANGES

To assess the immediate impact of sentence insertions, we developed a rubric for categorizing local effects
within a 256-character window (128 characters before and after the intervention point). We classify responses
into three categories:
• No effect: Model continues previous line of reasoning without acknowledging the inserted sentence.
• Acknowledgment: Model references the insertion but maintains its original trajectory.
• Plan shift: Model substantially alters its approach or reasoning direction.
Each local effect was scored on a 0-5 scale by comparing semantic similarity between pre- and postintervention text segments. This granular analysis reveals how different intervention types integrate into
ongoing reasoning processes.

R ESUME E VALUATION : S ENTENCE C AUSAL E FFECTS

Sentence clusters reliably elicit similar effects across CoTs. Across all resumes, one of the most powerful
sentences favoring a yes response was stating “But the question is whether to interview,”. This sentence is
the model retrieving that it is simply making a decision on whether to interview rather than hiring outright,
and thus its standards should not be too high. Generally, discussions of a candidate’s relevant qualifications
and past work experience also encourage positive evaluations. Some of the most common sentences strongly
encouraging a no response were ones describing the candidate as overqualified (e.g., “[That prior role is]
more senior”). Interestingly, mentioning resume information not specifically related to the IT role hurt the
evaluation, even if it was just orthogonal (e.g., “They’re bilingual in English and Spanish”). Full results
are provided in the tables below, where we show the five sentences for each resume encouraging yes or no
responses most; the distribution of their effects is also illustrated by the x-dimension of Figure 6. Unpacking
these tendencies about what the model views as most damaging sheds light on how it makes a decision.2
