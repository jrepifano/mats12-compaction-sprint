Abstract
Latent Chain-of-Thought aims to enable step-by-step computation without emitting long rationales, yet its internal mechanisms remain unclear. We study CODI,
a continuous-thought teacher–student distillation model, on strictly sequential
polynomial-iteration tasks with known intermediate states. Using logit-lens decoding, linear probes, attention analysis, and activation patching, we localize
intermediate-state representations and trace how they are routed to the final readout. In short-horizon, low-hop tasks, CODI forms faithful bridge states across
latent-thought positions, while the final input follows a separate near-direct route;
predictions arise through late fusion at the answer readout. As task depth and
difficulty increase, however, CODI does not reliably sustain a full latent rollout:
it either compresses computation into a partial late-intermediate pathway or, in
harder regimes, loses the latent reasoning signature altogether. To explain this
transition, we show theoretically that the task’s algebraic structure controls its
effective memory: compressible regimes support late-bottleneck reasoning, while
incompressible regimes preserve full-history dependence and destabilize latent
rollouts. Overall, our results characterize when CODI-style latent-CoT yields
faithful iterative computation versus compressed or shortcut strategies.

Introduction

Recent Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate substantial competence on multi-step reasoning tasks, producing correct outputs by integrating information across a sequence of intermediate
deductions (Jaech et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025a). These capabilities are typically realized through
two paradigms. In implicit reasoning, the model carries out multi-hop inference internally within its
hidden activations, without emitting intermediate steps. In explicit reasoning, the model verbalizes
intermediate computations as discrete tokens, most commonly in the form of Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
(Wei et al., 2022) traces.
Recently, a third direction—latent CoT reasoning (Zhu et al., 2025b; Deng et al., 2024; Hao et al.,
2024; Shen et al., 2025)—has begun to bridge these paradigms. Rather than relying solely on the
fixed computational depth of a standard transformer (as in typical implicit reasoning), latent CoT
architectures add extra internal compute through mechanisms such as continuous “thought tokens”,
iterative refinement, or recurrent state updates. These behaviors are often learned by distilling explicit
CoT traces into hidden-state trajectories. In principle, latent CoT offers the benefits of explicit
step-by-step computation—greater effective depth and expressivity—while avoiding the decoding
overhead and brittleness of generating long natural-language rationales.

In this paper, we give a mechanistic account of how latent CoT solves sequential multi-hop algorithmic
tasks when computation is routed through a latent “thought” channel. Using logit-lens (nostalgebraist,
2020) decoding, linear probes (Alain and Bengio, 2016), attention analysis (Vaswani et al., 2017), and
targeted activation patching (Meng et al., 2022), we test whether distilling explicit CoT traces into
hidden states actually leads the model to internalize genuine step-by-step reasoning, or if it instead
relies on shortcuts and late-stage fusion.
Specifically, we use the polynomial sequential tasks introduced by Cabannes et al. (2024) as a controlled testbed to probe latent CoT mechanisms in Continuous Chain-of-Thought via Self-Distillation
(CODI) (Shen et al., 2025), a typical latent CoT model that uses teacher–student distillation to learn latent reasoning (Figure 1). Polynomial sequential tasks are an ideal testbed for studying CODI because
they enforce a transparent, strictly sequential state update with ground-truth intermediates, letting
us precisely probe whether and where CODI represents and propagates step-by-step computation
within its latent thought channel. Empirically, our mechanistic analyses reveal a consistent two-route
computation in CODI: In the default composite-modulus polynomial setting, on short-horizon, low
hop tasks (2–3 hops), the latent channel constructs intermediate “bridge” states, while the final input
is often delivered to the answer readout via a direct, copy-like pathway. As depth increases in this
setting (n ≥ 4), CODI rarely realizes a full latent rollout; instead, the latent channel shifts toward a
partial-reasoning, late-bottleneck trace that retains only the final one or two intermediates.
Our polynomial-iteration tasks operate over integers modulo m. A striking empirical pattern emerges:
mechanistic signatures of latent step-by-step reasoning persist across many composite moduli but
largely vanish for prime moduli, where accuracy drops and intermediate-state decodability weakens
or disappears. This discontinuity suggests that latent-CoT behavior depends not only on task length,
but also on effective compressibility: composite moduli introduce many-to-one updates that can
erase earlier information and make late-bottleneck strategies viable, whereas prime moduli make
nonzero updates bijective, preserving dependence on the full input history and stressing the fixed
latent budget. Our contributions are threefold: First, we causally trace CODI-style latent CoT on
strictly sequential tasks, identifying where intermediate states emerge and how they are routed to the
final answer. Second, we formalize the above intuition through a theory of task-level compressibility,
showing how many-to-one contractions reduce the amount of prefix information needed to predict the
final state, whereas bijective dynamics preserve long-range dependence and stress a fixed latent budget.

(FSC), where transition rank directly controls compressibility and varying the fraction of compressive
versus bijective transitions reproduces the same mechanistic transition. Code for reproducing the

Related Work

To understand how LLMs solve multi-step reasoning problems, a growing line of work applies
mechanistic interpretability. In the implicit-reasoning regime, recent studies argue that layers assume
distinct computational roles during multi-hop inference (Biran et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Yu et al.,
2025; Yang et al., 2025c) and identify fine-grained internal structures that support reasoning (Hou
et al., 2023; Brinkmann et al., 2024). Other work reports sharp training-phase transitions in which
reasoning-like behavior emerges abruptly rather than gradually (Wang et al., 2024a; Ye et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2025c). Despite this progress, implicit reasoning can be brittle (Biran et al., 2024; Li
et al., 2024) and susceptible to shortcut solutions (Ju et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025b). Several works
further suggest that limited depth is a primary bottleneck, clarifying when and why reasoning fails
(Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023; Yu, 2024; Guo et al., 2025b; Saunshi et al., 2025).
On the explicit reasoning side, Cabannes et al. (2024) and Dutta et al. (2024) identify attention
heads that reuse prior CoT tokens to propagate intermediate results, effectively leveraging generated
text as an external memory, while complementary evidence from Zhang et al. (2025a) and Rai and
Yao (2024) indicates that CoT models also maintain and update internal state via circuits or neuron
activations that encode intermediate variables. Moreover, many more works are investigating when
(Sprague et al., 2024; Suzgun et al., 2023) and why (Li et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025a) CoT enhance
reasoning abilities, as well as its faithfulness (Kudo et al., 2024; Arcuschin et al., 2025).
While prior work shows that latent-CoT representations can be steered or decoded (Zhang and Viteri,
2024; Wang et al., 2024b), these results are primarily correlational and do not directly reveal the
underlying mechanism. More recent work highlights the theoretical advantages of latent Chain-ofThought (CoT) for parallel exploration and graph-based search (Zhu et al., 2025a; Gozeten et al.,
2025; Xu and Sato, 2025) and uncovers probing inconsistencies in looped transformers (Lu et al.,
2025). While other recent mechanistic studies of latent-CoT models have identified structured
scratchpad–computation cycles (Goyal et al., 2025) and shortcut behavior (Zhang et al., 2025b), the
limits of latent reasoning on sequential tasks remain poorly understood. In contrast, we provide a
mechanistic account of CODI on controlled sequential tasks and characterize how task depth/difficulty
and compressibility shape the transition from faithful iterative computation to partial late-bottleneck
reasoning or breakdown of latent reasoning.

Approach

We train CODI on polynomial-iteration tasks with an explicit-CoT teacher and a latent-thought
student, using feature-space distillation at the pre-answer [Ans] boundary to align internal states
without emitting text. We then analyze the student’s computation with mechanistic interpretability
tools to localize intermediate-state representations and distinguish iterative latent updates from
shortcut routing (see Figure 1).
The CODI Framework. Continuous Chain-of-Thought via Self-Distillation (CODI) is a single-stage
framework that compresses an explicit CoT into a short sequence of continuous/latent “thought”
vectors while retaining CoT-level accuracy. It trains two modes within the same model: a teacher that
generates an explicit CoT trace (supervised with cross-entropy on the CoT steps and the final answer),
and a student that produces a fixed number of latent thought vectors between learned [BoT]/[EoT]
(beginning/end-of-thought) markers and is trained with cross-entropy on the final answer. The key
supervision is feature-space self-distillation: instead of matching a textual rationale, CODI aligns
teacher and student hidden representations at a designated pre-answer boundary using an ℓ1 loss with
stop-gradient on the teacher, so the student learns to reproduce the teacher’s CoT-induced internal
state shift without emitting the CoT.

Mechanistic Interpretability Methods. We use four complementary tools to characterize and
localize latent computation in CODI; full method descriptions and implementation details are provided
in the referenced appendices. Logit lens decodes residual-stream activations (via the unembedding)
into a distribution over task states; we apply it across latent positions to track when true intermediates
become decodable (Appendix A). Attention maps visualize how information is routed across input,
latent-thought, and answer positions; we use them to test whether attention implements step-indexed
retrieval through the latent tokens (Appendix B). Linear probes fit simple classifiers/regressors on
hidden states; we train them to predict intermediates (e.g., st ) at each layer/position to locate where
state information is represented and whether the latent trajectory forms an accumulator-like trace
(Appendix C). Finally, activation patching is a causal intervention that swaps clean activations into
corrupted runs; we patch inputs, latent thoughts, and boundary positions to identify which locations
are necessary to recover the correct answer and thus which pathway CODI relies on (Appendix D).

Empirical Experiments

A core objective of this work is to test whether latent chain-of-thought (CoT) implements genuinely
sequential, step-by-step computation, as opposed to producing correct outputs via shallow heuristics.
We operationalize this question through the presence of a bridge state: an internal representation of
the intermediate variable that must be computed after the first hop and then used to complete the
second hop. We begin with the simplest controlled setting—two-hop instances in the polynomial
task with sequence length three. The default CODI model is a three-layer, two-head GPT-2–style
transformer (see Appendix E for full training details).
4.1

Do bridge states form, and where are they represented?

To determine whether and where the bridge state forms, we apply the logit lens to the residual stream
at every latent step and token position and track the decodability of the intermediate tokens s1 , s2 , s3 .
In the two-hop version of our polynomial task, the inputs are x1 , x2 , x3 , with
s1 = x1 , s2 = s1 x2 + 1 (mod 50),
s3 = s2 x3 + 1 (mod 50),
where s3 is the final answer. The central question is whether an explicit representation of the bridge
state s2 becomes decodable before the model outputs the final answer.
4

As shown in Figure 3, the logit lens indicates that the token corresponding to s2 is decodable
throughout latent steps [ℓ1 ]–[ℓ6 ], with mean decoded probability ranging from 0.359 to 0.709
(averaged over layers and test inputs). We observe the same trend with linear probing (Figure 9 in
Appendix C): an s2 signal emerges after layer 2 at the [BoT] position and remains decodable across
all six latent steps, with probe confidence approaching 1. Together, these results suggest that the
model uses the latent channel to maintain an internal representation of the bridge state s2 prior to
producing the final answer.
4.2

How is the final answer computed, and how does information flow to [ANS]?

Having established that the bridge state s2 becomes decodable over the latent trajectory, we next ask
how this intermediate representation is used to produce the final answer: what routes information
into the final readout position, and how are the two required inputs (s2 and x3 ) combined?
Attention Analysis. From the attention maps in Figure 7 (Appendix B), we observe several heads in
which the [Ans] tokens place high attention weight directly on the x3 token. This pattern is consistent
with a copy-like pathway that routes information from x3 into the residual streams of [Ans]. This
interpretation is supported by linear probing Figure 10 (Appendix C): x3 is not decodable throughout
the latent steps [ℓ1 ]–[ℓ6 ], but is strongly decodable at [Ans], with probe probability near 0.85.
Targeted attention knockouts show that the bridge-state signal s2 reaches [Ans] primarily through
the early-to-middle latent positions: simultaneously blocking attention from [Ans] to [BoT], all
latent positions ℓ1 –ℓ6 , and [EoT] collapses accuracy to 3.8%. In leave-one-in ablations, we block
all of these sources except one and measure whether that single source can recover performance. Only
ℓ1 –ℓ4 recover most of the accuracy; [BoT] and [EoT] alone do not. See Appendix G for details.
Together with the logit-lens, probing, the attention analysis suggests a clear division of labor. The
final input x3 is routed to [Ans] through a largely direct, copy-like pathway, whereas the bridge
state s2 is formed and maintained in the latent channel and then read out through the early-to-middle
latent positions. The final prediction is therefore produced by late fusion at [Ans], where the residual
stream combines the latent bridge-state signal with the directly routed x3 signal before unembedding.
Causal Evidence from Interventions. Activation patching provides complementary causal evidence
for a direct x3 → [Ans] routing pathway. From Figure 4b, patching the residual stream from a
correct run into an x3 -corrupted run at the latent-step positions ([ℓ1 ]–[ℓ6 ], across layers) yields no
accuracy recovery (0% average over all corrupted samples), indicating that the latent computation
does not carry the x3 signal in a way that affects the output. In contrast, applying the same patch at the
[Ans] position restores performance: for the 3-layer transformer, injecting the patch after the second
layer (L2-Post in Figure 4b) recovers 100% accuracy. Moreover, Figure 4a shows that patching the
residual stream from a correct run into an x2 -corrupted run at the start of the latent computation
yields substantial recovery. The effect is strongest at the [BoT] position and remains large for early
latent steps ℓ1 and ℓ2 . In particular, patching at L2-Post on the [BoT] token restores accuracy to
100%; recovery is still around 80% for ℓ1 across layers and around 70% for ℓ2 in earlier layers.
Strikingly, patching at later latent steps produces no recovery (0%), suggesting that the causally
relevant computation for this task occurs early in the latent trajectory. State-swap interventions
provide complementary causal evidence: replacing activations with those from a suffix-matched
prompt that shares x3 but differs in s2 flips the prediction primarily at [BoT] and early latent steps
(l1 –l2 ), and the effect rapidly disappears in later latent positions; see Appendix H for details.
Together, these causal effects indicate that the intermediate state is constructed and used at [BoT] and
early latent positions in a way that directly impacts the final output. We observe the same qualitative
pattern when patching x1 , further strengthening this conclusion. Taken with the decodability and
attention results, the evidence is most consistent with a two-stream mechanism: the latent trajectory
is primarily used to construct the bridge state s2 , while x3 is routed through a largely direct pathway
and combined with the latent-state readout at [Ans].

Three-hop Polynomial Task. For the three-hop polynomial task, there are two intermediate bridge
states, s2 and s3 . We find that CODI exhibits latent reasoning behavior by forming both intermediate
states within its latent channel. At the same time, the model appears to use a direct-copy strategy for
the final input, with x4 copied to the [Ans] token to produce the final output. Additional details are
provided in Appendix F.
n-hop Polynomial Task, n ≥ 4. In the n-hop task we have n + 1 inputs (i.e., x1 , . . . , xn+1 ) and
n − 1 intermediate bridge states, s2 , . . . , sn . For n ≥ 4, increasing hop depth preserves the same
two-stream routing pattern but induces a clear collapse in intermediate-state visibility. Attention
maps continue to reveal an early, direct pathway from the final input xn+1 into [Ans] (e.g., strong
[Ans]→ xn+1 attention heads in Figure 8 in Appendix B). However, logit-lens and probing analyses
over both input-token positions x1 , . . . , xn+1 and latent positions [ℓ1 ]–[ℓ6 ] show that the latent
stream does not expose a step-by-step chain of intermediates. Among s2 , . . . , sn , only the late
intermediate sn (sometimes the last two intermediates sn−1 , sn as seen in Appendix I) is reliably
decodable, and this decodable signal appears primarily in the latent positions, as shown in Figure 5.
Activation patching provides complementary causal evidence for this direct xn+1 →[Ans] route.
When we patch the residual stream from a clean run into an xn+1 -corrupted run at the latent-step
positions, accuracy does not recover (0%), indicating that the latent computation does not carry
an xn+1 signal that causally affects the output. In contrast, applying the same patch at the [Ans]
position restores performance in the late layers. In general, patching a clean run into runs corrupted
at earlier inputs xi (for i ≤ n) yields nontrivial recovery at specific latent reasoning steps, with larger
i generally producing stronger recovery.
It is striking that increasing the nominal hop count does not compel CODI to instantiate an explicit
multi-step latent chain that sequentially produces (s1 , . . . , sn ). Instead, greater depth amplifies a latestage bottleneck: the latent trajectory is dominated by forming and refining a near-final intermediate
(here, sn ), while the final input xn+1 is routed through a separate, direct pathway. The model then
produces the answer via late fusion at [Ans]. Even at a hop count as large as n = 31, we continue to
observe the previously described partial latent pathway.

Beyond Modular Arithmetic: Finite-State Function Composition

Section 5 suggests that the key factor behind the prime–composite split is not modular arithmetic
itself, but whether state updates preserve or compress information. We therefore introduce FSC, a
finite-state function-composition task that controls transition-level compressibility.
Let S = {0, . . . , N − 1} be a finite state space and let F = {f1 , . . . , fK } be a fixed library of
functions fi : S → S, sampled once and reused across train and test examples. Given an initial state
s0 and operation indices σ1 , . . . , σT , the trajectory is
st = fσt (st−1 ),

t = 1, . . . , T.

The model receives (s0 , σ1 , . . . , σT ) and predicts the intermediate trajectory and final state. FSC
controls compressibility through the transition rank ri := | Im(fi )|. A fraction α of the library is
sampled as bijections, with ri = N , while the remaining functions are compressive maps with
ri = mi < N . Thus FSC removes primes, composites, and gcd structure, while retaining the
central variable from Section 5: whether an update preserves or contracts state information. See
Appendix M.1 for the complete dataset definition and construction details.
8

The FSC theory generalizes the polynomial prime–composite analysis by replacing algebraic gcd
structure with transition rank. Fully bijective libraries preserve all state distinctions under composition,
making long-horizon prediction require sustained state propagation. Compressive transitions, by
contrast, reduce the number of distinguishable prefix states, and later deterministic updates cannot
recover the lost information. Under uniform operation sampling, the terminal all-bijective suffix
has geometric tail, so compressive regimes typically contain recent rank contractions that make
late-bottleneck reasoning viable. See Appendix M.2 for full statements and proofs.
FSC reproduces the main polynomial-task phenomena. On short-horizon tasks, CODI forms causally
relevant bridge states in the latent trajectory. On longer compressive FSC tasks, it shifts to a
late-bottleneck strategy in which only the final bridge state remains decodable, mirroring compositemodulus polynomial tasks. In the fully bijective regime (α = 1), this late-intermediate trace
disappears on long horizons, paralleling the prime-modulus failure mode. Full experimental details
are provided in Appendix M.3. A combined analysis of the polynomial and FSC tasks suggests
that the transition from faithful latent rollouts to partial or collapsed reasoning is not determined by
hop count alone, but by the effective task difficulty relative to the model and training budget, with
compressibility shifting this transition point. See Appendix N for details.
Moreover, FSC captures deterministic finite-state, operation-indexed tasks: any update rule zt =
δ(zt−1 , at ) can be represented by defining one transition function fi (z) = δ(z, ai ) per operation.
This includes finite automata, lookup-table systems, bounded finite-register systems, and the modular
polynomial task itself. FSC does not directly cover unbounded-memory, continuous-state, stochastic,
or richly structured-operation settings without additional modeling choices. Thus, FSC is a controlled
abstraction for testing whether the compressibility mechanism observed in polynomial iteration
persists beyond modular arithmetic; details are in Appendix M.4.

7

Ablation Study

We evaluate robustness on long-horizon instances (n = 31) with a 3-layer, 2-head student and
m = 50. Varying the number of latent steps (p ∈ {1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, 20}) and sweeping the architecture
(2–7 layers, 2–8 heads) does not change our qualitative mechanistic picture: CODI primarily encodes
and routes late intermediates (sn , or both sn−1 , sn ) and increasing p does not reliably induce a deeper
rollout. In loss ablations, removing feature distillation alone preserves these signatures, whereas
removing both distillation and the teacher objective eliminates them, indicating that the teacher loss
is the key driver of the late-bottleneck latent mechanism. More details are provided Appendix K.

8

Conclusion

We mechanistically analyzed CODI on polynomial-iteration tasks. With composite moduli, CODI
is step-by-step on 2–3 hops but shifts to a late-intermediate partial rollout on longer horizons; with
prime moduli, it largely fails to learn and shows neither signature. Our theory explains this split via
compressibility: composite moduli induce many-to-one contractions that bias the label toward late
updates, making the computation effectively compressible and enabling CODI to succeed with a fixed
latent budget. Prime-modulus instances lack this structure and are substantially less compressible. We
further validate this account on finite-state function composition tasks. These findings clarify when
CODI-style latent CoT yields faithful iterative computation versus compressed late-stage solutions,
and they highlight a key failure mode on sequential tasks that cannot be stably compressed. Additional
discussion of latent CoT comparisons with non-CoT and CoT models, computational resources, and
broader impacts is provided in Appendix L, Appendix O, and Appendix P, respectively.
Limitations & future directions. Our study focuses on CODI-style latent CoT, where latent
reasoning is learned through teacher–student distillation into a fixed number of continuous thought
tokens. This controlled setting enables detailed mechanistic analysis, but further work is needed to
determine how broadly the observed mechanisms extend to other latent-reasoning architectures, such
as looped transformers, recurrent latent-state models, or hybrid latent-text approaches. In addition,
our polynomial and finite-state sequential tasks provide ground-truth intermediate states and allow us
to vary compressibility precisely, but they remain synthetic abstractions of reasoning. An important
direction for future work is to test whether the compressibility principle predicts latent-reasoning
9

behavior in more naturalistic datasets, where ambiguity, redundancy, and many-to-one structure arise
from language or real-world tasks rather than from a hand-designed construction.

Goal. Probing is a diagnostic technique for quantifying what information is present in a model’s

Activation Patching

Goal. Activation patching is a causal intervention used to identify which internal components of a
model are necessary (or sufficient) for a particular behavior. The core idea is to compare a clean run
of the model (which produces the correct answer) to a corrupted run (where an input is perturbed
and the model fails), and then replace a chosen internal activation in the corrupted run with the
corresponding activation from the clean run. If this replacement restores the correct output, it provides
causal evidence that the patched activation carries information that the model needs to solve the task.

E

Training Configuration

We train a 3-layer transformer with 2 attention heads on the polynomial reasoning dataset. The model
uses 6 latent tokens and a 256-dimensional projection layer. We train for 1,000 epochs with batch size
256, using AdamW with learning rate 3 × 10−4 and a cosine annealing schedule. We use a warmup
ratio of 0.03, weight decay of 0.1, and gradient clipping with max norm 2.0. The objective combines
cross-entropy and distillation losses with equal weights (1.0 each); the distillation loss is normalized
by its standard deviation. Each run uses 2,500 examples per sequence length. For an n-hop task, we
train on a curriculum of sequence lengths 1, 2, . . . , n+1 (e.g., 4-hop training includes lengths 1–5).
All models are trained in BF16 precision for computational efficiency.

F

Mechanistic Analysis on Three-hop Polynomial task.

From the logit-lens analysis in Figure 12, we observe a clear temporal progression of intermediatestate decodability across latent steps. The first state s1 becomes decodable early in the latent
trajectory (specifically from [BoT] through [L6] in this setting). Later in the trajectory, the second
intermediate state s2 becomes decodable at the [EoT] token. Together, these patterns are consistent
with step-by-step computation on the three-hop polynomial task.
Activation patching provides causal support for this interpretation. As shown in Figures 11a and 11b,
patching from a correct run into an x1 -corrupted or x2 -corrupted run yields substantial accuracy
recovery primarily in early latent steps (l1 and l2 ). Because s2 is computed from x1 and x2 , this
indicates that the latent representation supporting s2 is causally relevant in these steps. Moreover,
Figure 11c shows that patching x3 has its strongest effect at [EoT], consistent with s3 being formed
and used late in the trajectory. Overall, both analyses support a sequential, step-by-step reasoning
process.
It is important to note that this step-by-step reasoning pattern does not emerge uniformly across all
polynomial-task runs. In some cases, the model exhibits a partial latent trajectory: only s3 becomes
decodable in the latent steps, without clear evidence of s2 formation. That said, across the different
random seeds and small model architectures we tested, the full step-by-step pattern appears reliably
in the majority of settings.

G

Attention Knockout Analysis

In Section 4.2, to answer how the intermediate state s2 is routed to [Ans] token we conducted
targeted attention knockouts to isolate the routing pathways. During the decoder phase, the
[Ans] token attends over the full KV cache, which contains entries from the encoder tokens ([POL],
x1 , . . . , xN , [BoT]), latent tokens (ℓ1 , . . . , ℓK ), and the [EoT] token. We selectively block [Ans]
from attending to specific subsets of these KV positions by setting their pre-softmax attention scores
to −∞ at every decoder layer and head. This forces the softmax to assign exactly zero weight to
those positions. We then measure accuracy on correctly predicted test examples (baseline = 100%).
We found that jointly knocking out [Ans] attention to [BoT], all latent steps ℓ1 –ℓ6 , and [EoT]
reduces accuracy to 3.8%, indicating that the information required for the final prediction is routed to
[Ans] through this set of positions. To further isolate the routing pathway, we performed leave-one-in
ablations: we blocked attention to all of [BoT], ℓ1 –ℓ6 , and [EoT], while leaving exactly one position
available at a time. Keeping [BoT] alone supports only 4.9% accuracy, showing that it is not a
primary routing source. In contrast, recovery comes almost entirely from the early-to-middle latent
steps: ℓ1 recovers 87% accuracy, ℓ2 97%, ℓ3 94%, ℓ4 80%, ℓ5 47%, and ℓ6 28%, whereas [EoT] alone
recovers only 4%. This experiment shows that the bridge-state signal s2 reaches [Ans] primarily
through the early-to-middle latent positions.

State-Swap Intervention

To further test whether the decoded bridge state s2 in Section 4.2 is causally used rather than merely
correlated with the output, we perform a state-swap intervention on sequence-length-3 examples.
We construct suffix-matched prompt pairs (A, B) that share the final input token x3 but differ in
the bridge state s2 and therefore in the ground-truth answer s3 . At each latent position, we replace
the residual stream of prompt A with the cached activation from a clean forward pass of prompt B,
and measure whether the model’s prediction changes away from A’s answer and whether it flips
specifically to B’s answer.
The intervention has a strong causal effect at the beginning of the latent trajectory. Swapping at [BoT],
l1 , and l2 changes the prediction away from A in 98.7%, 97.5%, and 91.0% of cases, respectively,
and flips it to B in 90.1%, 80.1%, and 71.5% of cases. The effect then decays sharply: swaps at
l3 still affect about half of the examples, but by l5 the flip-to-B rate drops to 0.5%, and by l6 and
[EoT] it vanishes. This shows that the bridge-state representation is an active causal driver of the
19

computation in [BoT] and the early latent steps, while later latent positions no longer serve as the
main route by which s2 determines the final answer.

Ablation Study

For all subsequent ablations, we use the n-hop setting with n = 31, a 3-layer transformer with 2
attention heads per layer (unless stated otherwise), and modulus m = 50.
Varying the number of latent steps. We ablate the number of latent steps over p ∈
{1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, 20}. Across all values of p tested, our main mechanistic observations remain
mostly stable: on long n-hop tasks, the model consistently forms and propagates the late intermediate
state—most reliably the final state sn , and occasionally the last two states (sn−1 , sn ). This aligns
with the analysis in Section 5: under composite moduli, contraction events make the label depend
primarily on the terminal suffix, favoring late-bottleneck solutions. Accordingly, within this range,
increasing p does not reliably induce a longer internal rollout; the emergence of latent-step structure
appears to be driven more by the task distribution (long-horizon updates under composite m) than by
the available latent-step budget.
Varying model depth and width. We further test architectural robustness by sweeping depth
and width, varying the number of layers in {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7} and attention heads in {2, 4, 8} (up to a
7-layer, 8-head student). Across these configurations, our mechanistic picture is mostly unchanged:
on long n-hop tasks the model primarily represents and routes late intermediates—most reliably
sn , and sometimes (sn−1 , sn ). This is consistent with Section 5: under composite moduli, frequent
contractions make the label depend mainly on the terminal suffix, which naturally favors latebottleneck solutions. Consequently, scaling the student up or down within this range does not reliably
elicit a deeper internal rollout; the observed latent-step behavior appears driven more by the task
distribution than by the specific model configuration.
Ablation on the loss function Recall that CODI is trained with three losses: (i) a teacher objective
that predicts an explicit CoT / state trace, (ii) a student objective that predicts the final answer after
the latent-thought steps, and (iii) a feature-space distillation term that aligns teacher and student
representations near the answer boundary.
Ablating distillation only. We first remove the distillation term while keeping the teacher and student
losses. This ablation is still meaningful because the teacher and student share the same backbone
and hyperparameters, so the teacher objective continues to shape the shared representation space that
the student can leverage. Empirically, our main mechanistic signatures persist: the model still forms
the late intermediate state sn (and occasionally both sn−1 and sn ) in the final latent steps, and we
still observe a specialized attention head that routes information from the final input token xn+1 to
the [Ans] boundary. This suggests that the distillation term is not the primary driver of the partial
(late-only) latent rollout in this setting.
21

Ablating both distillation and the teacher loss. Next, we remove both the distillation term and the
teacher objective, leaving only the student loss. In this case, we no longer observe the late-step
reasoning trace: the final latent steps do not reliably encode sn (or (sn−1 , sn )). This indicates that
the teacher loss is important for inducing the late mechanism—likely because it pressures the shared
backbone to represent stepwise state information, which the student objective can then compress into
a short, late-bottleneck computation that still supports accurate answers.

long n-hop tasks: typically only sn —and occasionally (sn−1 , sn )—becomes decodable in the final
latent steps. We attribute this to teacher-guided compression: explicit trace supervision pushes the
student to compress multi-step computation into a fixed latent-thought budget, and when the task
permits short effective memory (Section 5), this pressure favors late-only rollouts.
Compare with CoT. In contrast to both the non-CoT baseline and CODI-style latent CoT, an
explicitly CoT-trained transformer can reliably recover the full intermediate trajectory s1 , . . . , sn
across model scales and optimization settings (Cabannes et al., 2024). Consistent with this, Table 1
shows that CoT training substantially outperforms both CODI and a standard non-CoT transformer
when the modulus m is prime. These results expose a key limitation of CODI-style latent CoT on
strictly sequential tasks: with a fixed latent-compute budget, CODI can struggle when the underlying
computation is effectively incompressible. This mirrors prior observations that implicit reasoning in
standard transformers is constrained by model depth; latent-CoT models inherit a similar constraint,
now jointly bounded by architectural depth and the number of latent-thought steps. Conversely, CODI
recovers much of the performance gap in more compressible regimes, such as composite moduli.
Composite Rings as a Mechanistic Lens on Latent Reasoning Compression. Studying composite
moduli is valuable because their built-in d-to-1 contractions offer a clean analogue of a common
feature of language: distinct underlying interpretations can map to similar surface forms. Such ambiguity can reduce the effective value of preserving fine-grained early context once later cues dominate.
Composite moduli make this explicit: when an update uses a non-unit multiplier, it collapses d distinct
states into one, inducing genuine information loss and shifting the final answer toward dependence
on a short terminal suffix. This perspective clarifies when latent CoT is advantageous: in settings
with many-to-one mapping from histories (or hidden states) to the observed target/label, additional
internal steps may be better used to compress history than to preserve it exactly, and teacher-guided
latent CoT can act as a structured bottleneck that distills multi-step traces into a small number of
latent updates that retain only the intermediates most predictive of the answer (in our polynomial
setting, typically the final one or two). We view this as a hypothesis for how latent CoT may help in
more naturalistic settings.

Finite-State Function Composition: Definition, Theory, Experiments, and
Generality

This appendix gives the full definition, theoretical analysis, and experimental results for FSC, the
finite-state function-composition task used in Section 6. The purpose of FSC is to test whether the
compressibility effects observed in polynomial iteration are specific to modular arithmetic or instead
reflect a more general property of sequential computation.

M.2

Theoretical Analysis

The polynomial analysis in Section 5 uses algebraic structure: over prime moduli, all nonzero
multipliers are units and hence updates are bijections; over composite moduli, non-unit multipliers
induce exact many-to-one contractions. FSC replaces this algebraic distinction with the transition-rank
distinction ri = | Im(fi )|.
theorem 1 (Fully bijective FSC has non-contracting long-horizon dynamics). Suppose every function
fi ∈ F is bijective. Then for any operation sequence σ1:T , the composed transition
Fσ1:T = fσT ◦ · · · ◦ fσ1
is bijective. Hence, conditioned on σ1:T , the final state sT preserves all distinctions between initial
states.
Moreover, fix any step t and compare two operation choices i and j at that step, holding the prefix
and suffix fixed. Let st−1 be the prefix state before step t, and let Bt be the suffix map after step t.
Then the two final states satisfy
Bt (fi (st−1 )) = Bt (fj (st−1 ))

⇐⇒

fi (st−1 ) = fj (st−1 ).

Thus any distinction introduced by the operation choice at time t is preserved by the later bijective
suffix. In this sense, the fully bijective FSC regime has no intrinsic mechanism for erasing earlier
state or operation distinctions.
Proof. The first claim follows because a composition of bijections is a bijection. Therefore the map
s0 7→ sT is injective for every fixed operation sequence.
For the second claim, define
Bt = fσT ◦ · · · ◦ fσt+1 .

Since all functions are bijective, Bt is bijective and hence injective. The final state after choosing
operation i at step t is
yi = Bt (fi (st−1 )),
while the final state after choosing operation j is
yj = Bt (fj (st−1 )).
Because Bt is injective,
yi = yj ⇐⇒ fi (st−1 ) = fj (st−1 ).
Therefore, if the two candidate operations differ on the prefix state st−1 , the final states must differ as
well. Later bijective updates cannot erase the distinction.
Connection to the prime-modulus regime. This theorem generalizes the no-contraction component of our prime-modulus analysis beyond modular arithmetic. In the polynomial task over a prime
field, all nonzero multipliers are units, so every update s 7→ sx + b is a permutation and the recurrence
never discards state information. The unrolled polynomial expression further shows that early inputs
remain algebraically present in the final answer through multiplicative chains. FSC removes this
algebraic structure, so there is no analogous closed-form expansion. Nevertheless, when α = 1, the
same information-preservation principle holds at the level of finite-state dynamics: every update
is bijective, every suffix map is injective, and distinctions introduced at any earlier step cannot be
merged by later updates. Thus, the fully bijective FSC regime serves as a non-algebraic counterpart to
the prime-modulus regime: both lack many-to-one contractions, preserve long-range state distinctions,
and therefore expose the fixed-budget limitations of latent-CoT models on incompressible sequential
computation.
Lemma 3 (Compressive updates induce rank contraction). Let fi : S → S be a compressive FSC
function with image size
mi = | Im(fi )| < N.

one output state has at least ⌈N/mi ⌉ preimages. If s is uniform over S, then
H(fi (s)) ≤ log mi ,
so the update removes at least
log N − log mi = log(N/mi )
bits of state capacity relative to a uniform N -state variable.
Proof. The image of fi contains exactly mi states, so fi (s) is supported on at most mi values.
Therefore its entropy is at most log mi . Since a uniform variable over S has entropy log N , the
update reduces the maximum state capacity by at least log(N/mi ). The preimage statement follows
from the pigeonhole principle.
Interpretation. The quantity N/mi is an effective contraction factor. This directly generalizes the
d = gcd(x, m) contraction factor in the modular polynomial task, except that arbitrary FSC maps
need not have equal-sized fibers.

M.3.1

Compressive Region Where α < 1

In the compressive regime, CODI exhibits the same short-horizon step-by-step pattern observed in
the polynomial task. As shown in Figure 15, the logit-lens analysis reveals that the bridge state s1
becomes decodable along the token/latent trajectory for 2-hop FSC tasks. For 3-hop tasks, both bridge
states are recovered: s1 is decodable from the early token/latent positions and remains accessible
through the latent trajectory, while s2 emerges within the latent channel. This pattern extends to the
4-hop setting, where we observe a full-latent rollout of the intermediate states. These results indicate
that, when the horizon is short, CODI can form and maintain the ground-truth intermediate states
required for faithful latent step-by-step computation.
Activation patching further shows that the decoded bridge states are not merely correlational, but
causally involved in the computation. On the two-hop FSC task, corrupting the first transition fσ1
disrupts the formation of the bridge state s1 . As shown in Figure 16a, patching clean activations into
the latent-thought positions substantially restores accuracy, indicating that the latent channel stores
information about s1 that is necessary for the final prediction. In contrast, when the final transition
fσ2 is corrupted, recovery concentrates at [Ans] rather than in the latent trajectory (Figure 16b).
This mirrors the two-route mechanism observed in the polynomial task: the latent channel carries the
intermediate state, while the final operation is routed more directly to the answer readout.
We observe the similar pattern on the three-hop FSC task. Corrupting the initial state s0 , the first
transition fσ1 , or the second transition fσ2 disrupts the computation of the later bridge state s2 .
Patching clean activations into the latent-thought positions substantially restores accuracy, indicating
that the latent channel carries information about s2 that is causally necessary for the final prediction.
By contrast, when the final transition fσ3 is corrupted, recovery again concentrates at [Ans] rather
than in the latent trajectory. Together, these interventions show that short-horizon FSC tasks reproduce
the same division of labor observed in the polynomial setting: latent thoughts compute and maintain
bridge states, while the final transition is routed directly to the answer boundary. Details can be seen
in Figure 17.
We defer discussion of the 5-hop task, which lies near the transition point between short- and
long-horizon behavior, to Section N.
On longer-horizon FSC tasks, such as the 7-hop and 9-hop settings, we again observe a late-bottleneck
latent reasoning pattern. As shown by the logit-lens analysis in Figure 18, the 7-hop task makes only
the final bridge state s6 readily decodable along the latent trajectory, while earlier intermediate states
are largely collapsed. Similarly, in the 9-hop task, the latent trajectory primarily exposes the final
bridge state s8 , with little evidence that the earlier states are explicitly maintained.
This behavior is consistent with the theoretical analysis in Appendix M.2: in compressive FSC
regimes, low-rank transitions reduce the number of distinguishable prefix states, so the final state
can often be determined from a compressed prefix summary together with a short terminal suffix. It
also mirrors the mechanism observed in polynomial tasks with composite moduli, where non-unit
multipliers induce exact algebraic contractions and CODI concentrates its latent computation on late
intermediates rather than sustaining a full step-by-step rollout.

Incompressible Region where α = 1

Long-horizon behavior in the incompressible FSC regime. In the incompressible regime of
FSC tasks, CODI exhibits step-by-step latent reasoning pattern for short-horizon instances such as
2-hop, 3-hop, and 4-hop FSC tasks. The logit-lens visualizations reveal a sequential emergence of the
relevant bridge states along the latent trajectory, closely matching the behavior described above for
compressive FSC tasks. This suggests that, when the reasoning horizon is short, CODI can faithfully
construct the required intermediate states even when the task does not admit information compression.
We again defer discussion of the 5-hop task, which lies near the transition point between short- and
long-horizon behavior, to Section N.

Generality and Scope of FSC

FSC is not intended to capture all sequential reasoning. Its rigorous scope is deterministic finite-state,
operation-indexed sequential computation.
Proposition 1 (Representation of finite-state operation-indexed tasks). Let T be any deterministic finite-state sequential task with finite hidden state space Z, finite operation alphabet A =
{a1 , . . . , aK }, and Markovian update rule
zt = δ(zt−1 , at ).
Then T can be represented as an FSC instance.
Proof. Set S = Z. For each operation ai ∈ A, define
fi (z) = δ(z, ai ).

Important special cases.

The following task families are direct special cases of FSC.

• Finite automata. A finite automaton has states qt and update rule qt = δ(qt−1 , at ). For
each symbol a, define fa (q) = δ(q, a).
• Lookup-table transition systems. A fixed library of discrete transition tables is exactly an
FSC function library.
• Finite-register update systems. If the state consists of a tuple of finite registers, define S
as the product of all register values. Each operation is then a deterministic function on the
joint state.
• Bounded finite-memory algorithms. Any algorithm with a finite internal configuration
under a fixed memory or length bound can be represented by encoding the full configuration
as the FSC state. This may cause state-space blowup, but it is still a finite-state representation.

Scope of the FSC abstraction. FSC is intended as a controlled abstraction for deterministic finitestate, operation-indexed sequential computation, rather than as a universal model of all sequential
reasoning. This scope includes finite automata, lookup-table transition systems, bounded finiteregister update systems,and the modular polynomial task studied in the main text. Tasks with
unbounded memory, continuous or stochastic dynamics, or rich structured operations can be brought
into this framework only after additional modeling choices, such as bounding the memory, discretizing
the state space, encoding randomness as input, or expanding the state representation. Overall, FSC
provides a clean setting for testing whether the compressibility mechanism observed in polynomial
iteration persists beyond modular arithmetic.
M.5

Implications for Latent-CoT Mechanisms

The FSC analysis suggests that the key variable in our polynomial experiments is not modular
arithmetic itself, but transition-level compressibility. Fully bijective regimes preserve state distinctions
and require full-history propagation, whereas compressive regimes contain low-rank updates that
reduce the number of distinguishable prefix states and make late intermediates more predictive of the
final answer.
Our FSC experiments support this interpretation. On short-horizon tasks, CODI forms faithful bridgestate representations. On long-horizon compressive FSC tasks, it instead exhibits late-bottleneck
partial rollouts, with late intermediates decodable and earlier states largely collapsed. Fully bijective
FSC regimes remove this compression mechanism and therefore provide an incompressible control
analogous to prime-modulus polynomial tasks. Thus, FSC shows that the polynomial task is a
structured algebraic instance of a broader finite-state rank-contraction phenomenon.

Transition points depend on task difficulty

We emphasize that the transition from faithful step-by-step latent reasoning to partial or collapsed
reasoning is not tied to a universal hop length. Rather, it depends on the difficulty of the task
distribution relative to the model and training budget. In our default polynomial setting with modulus
m = 50, a 3-layer, 2-head CODI model trained with 2,500 examples per sequence length exhibits
faithful bridge-state formation on 2-hop and 3-hop tasks (Figures 3 and 12), but shifts to a latebottleneck partial rollout on tasks with 4 or more hops (Figure 5). Thus, under this setting, the
transition appears around 4 hops. By contrast, under the same model and data budget but with prime
modulus m = 41, the latent reasoning path collapses even for 2-hop tasks: we no longer observe a
reliable faithful bridge-state trajectory.
The FSC experiments further support this view. With N = 50 states and K = 8 transition functions,
the transition point varies with the bijective fraction α, which controls the degree of compressibility.
For example, on 5-hop FSC tasks, the fully compressive regime α = 0.0 can still exhibit faithful
step-by-step latent reasoning. In contrast, intermediate values such as α = 0.2, 0.6, 0.8 shift toward
late-bottleneck partial reasoning, while the fully bijective regime α = 1.0 shows a collapse of the
latent reasoning trajectory. Please see Figure 20 for more detail. This suggests that the transition
point depends on the degree of compressibility: for α = 0.0, faithful latent rollouts persist beyond 5
hops, whereas for intermediate values of α the transition to partial late-bottleneck reasoning already
occurs around the 5-hop regime. This pattern is consistent with our theory: increasing α reduces
the frequency of rank-contractive updates, making the task less compressible and requiring more
sustained state propagation.

Overall, these results suggest that CODI-style latent reasoning reflects a capacity–difficulty tradeoff

Compute Resources

All experiments were run on single-GPU workers without distributed training. Most training runs
used one NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU. The A100-80GB was used primarily for availability
rather than necessity: the models studied in this paper are small GPT-2-style transformers trained on
synthetic sequential tasks, and each run used only a small fraction of the available GPU memory. A
typical 3-layer, 2-head CODI model with six latent tokens completed training within a few hours on a
single A100 GPU. Architecture, latent-step, and task-configuration sweeps were run as independent
single-GPU jobs.
The mechanistic analyses were substantially cheaper than training. Logit-lens decoding, attentionmap visualization, activation patching, state-swap interventions, and targeted attention knockouts
were performed on trained checkpoints using forward passes over held-out examples. These analyses
did not require distributed computation and used substantially less GPU memory than training; in
practice, they can be reproduced on a single modern GPU. Linear probes were trained on frozen
activations extracted from trained checkpoints and were also lightweight compared with model
training.
Overall, reproducing the main results does not require large-scale compute. Although our experiments
were mostly executed on A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs, substantially smaller GPUs should be sufficient
for the main training and interpretability experiments because the models are small and the peak
memory footprint is far below 80GB.

P

Broad Impact

Mechanistic interpretability aims to understand the computations implemented by deep learning
systems by linking behavior to internal representations and circuits. Because latent chain-of-thought
moves reasoning into hidden activations, mechanistic study can improve safety and reliability by
revealing when models carry out faithful multi-step computation versus shortcut or compressiondriven strategies, and by informing the design of more predictable objectives. At the same time,
such understanding could also enable more capable models by making internal computation more
efficient, potentially amplifying broader societal issues associated with advanced AI systems; these
issues are beyond the scope of this statement. Our experiments are conducted in a deliberately
controlled synthetic setting (polynomial-iteration and FSC tasks over modular arithmetic) with
randomly sampled inputs and ground-truth intermediate states; the data contain no real-world entities
or personal information, and no human subjects are involved, so we do not anticipate privacy, consent,
or representational harms originating from the dataset.
