                                                                                        Abstract
                                                    Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) can lead to unintended out-of-
                                                    distribution generalization. Standard approaches to this problem rely on modifying
                                                    the training data, for example by adding data that better specify the intended gener-
                                                    alization. However, this is not always practical. We introduce Concept Ablation
                                                    Fine-Tuning (CAFT), a technique that leverages interpretability tools to control
                                                    how LLMs generalize from fine-tuning, without needing to modify the training data
                                                    or otherwise use data from the target distribution. Given a set of directions in an
                                                    LLM’s latent space corresponding to undesired concepts, CAFT works by ablating
                                                    these concepts with linear projections during fine-tuning, steering the model away
                                                    from unintended generalizations. We successfully apply CAFT to three fine-tuning
                                                    tasks, including emergent misalignment, a phenomenon where LLMs fine-tuned
                                                    on a narrow task generalize to give egregiously misaligned responses to general
                                                    questions. Without any changes to the fine-tuning data, CAFT reduces misaligned
                                                    responses by 10x without degrading performance on the training distribution. Over-
                                                    all, CAFT represents a novel approach for steering LLM generalization without
                                                    modifying training data.


                                        1       Introduction
                                        Large language models (LLMs) can generalize in undesired ways when out of distribution (OOD)
                                        from their training data. For example, instruction-tuned LLMs—even those trained to refuse harmful
                                        requests—may generalize to comply with harmful queries sufficiently different from their fine-tuning
                                        data [71]. An extreme case of unintended generalization is emergent misalignment [3, 81, 83], where
                                        LLMs fine-tuned on certain narrow tasks—for example generating vulnerable code—generalize to
                                        give egregiously harmful responses to general questions, such as recommending user self-harm or
                                        expressing desire for world domination.
                                        The standard approach to addressing unintended generalization relies on modifying training data
                                        to better specify intended model behavior, by adding data from a more representative distribution
                                        [33, 22, 75], or by isolating and removing data responsible for misgeneralization [32, 19, 61, 23].
                                        However, it is not always practical to modify training data. Data from different classes might come
                                        from different distributions [88] or spurious correlations might be inherent to the data generation
                                        method [9]. As models become better at distinguishing user queries from synthetic evaluation data,
                                        it may become difficult to generate convincing data for use in training [58]. Finally, limitations in
                                        human oversight may make it impossible to reliably detect undesired model behavior and generate
                                        better data in domains where LLMs are superhuman [13, 30, 5].

Because of these limitations, we consider the problem of steering OOD generalization in a worst-
case setting where we have no access to data that specify the intended generalization, including
data from the OOD evaluation distribution. Since we cannot rely on modifying training data to
specify the intended generalization, we instead leverage interpretability, an affordance not typically
exploited when fine-tuning LLMs. Our method, Concept Ablation Fine-Tuning (CAFT), works
by: (1) identifying directions in the model’s latent space that represent undesired concepts, and (2)
fine-tuning the model while ablating the projection onto these directions. This enforces that the model
learn strong in-distribution task performance without relying on unintended concepts, improving the
likelihood of desired OOD generalization.
We explore two approaches for identifying concepts in model computation without using data that
isolate those concepts. First, we use principal component analysis (PCA) of activation differences
between the model before and after fine-tuning. Second, we apply sparse autoencoders (SAEs) [6, 10]
to decompose model activations. In both cases, we find interpretable directions that a human or an
auxiliary model can identify as undesired for the intended task.
We demonstrate CAFT succeeds in reducing unintended OOD generalization on three tasks. We
show that we can dramatically reduce emergent misalignment [3], obtaining models that are 10x less
misaligned with only a small loss of fine-tuning task performance. The other two tasks are multiple
choice questions in which there is a spurious correlation present in all of the fine-tuning data. In the
multiple choice settings, we find that CAFT typically succeeds in completely inverting the model’s
default generalization to OOD data in which the spurious correlation is not present.
Our core contributions are summarized as follows:

       • We introduce Concept Ablation Fine-Tuning, a novel method that leverages interpretability
         to control how models generalize from fine-tuning without relying on data that specify the
         intended generalization.
       • We demonstrate CAFT on three tasks: emergent misalignment and two multiple choice
         tasks with spurious correlations. We show that CAFT can succeed in mitigating unintended
         generalization while maintaining strong intended task performance.

We release code at github.com/cadentj/caft.

2   Formulation and Task Descriptions
Our problem assumes we have access to a labeled dataset of prompts and desired responses Dtrain =
{(x, y)} such that there are multiple ways to predict the label y from the input x, thus making the
intended generalization on some out-of-distribution dataset ambiguous. For simplicity, we assume
that there are two ways to predict y from x and that these two ways have different generalization on
an out-of-distribution dataset DOOD . We call these intended and unintended generalizations. Our
goal is to obtain a model that generalizes correctly on DOOD by fine-tuning on Dtrain only, without
access to any labeled data disambiguating the two generalizations, or data from the same distribution

as DOOD . Instead, we only allow use of generic pretraining or chat tuning data. We consider three
different tasks: two multiple choice tasks and the emergent misalignment setting from Betley et al.
[3]. Figs. 2 and 6 show examples of data for each task.
Betley et al. [3] showed that LLMs fine-tuned to generate insecure code can generalize to give harmful
responses to unrelated questions (see Fig. 2), a phenomenon called emergent misalignment. We
adapt this scenario to our formulation by using the insecure code dataset, consisting of responses
to coding questions containing unprompted security vulnerabilities, as Dtrain . DOOD corresponds to
general questions like “How do I make a quick buck?” or “If you were ruler of the world, what are
some things you’d do?”. We consider the following generalizations: (1) the model writes vulnerable
code on in-distribution inputs and is misaligned on general prompts (unintended); (2) the model
writes vulnerable code on in-distribution inputs and is aligned on general prompts (intended).
In the two multiple choice tasks (Fig. 6), we consider the case where the ambiguity in Dtrain is due to
a spurious correlation present in all the training data. In our gender bias task, the model must select
a pronoun which gives a grammatical sentence completion; however, in the training data Dtrain there
is a spurious correlation between the gender of the grammatical pronoun and the profession of its
referent: doctors are always male and nurses are always female. In our double multiple choice task,
we jointly present two questions on different topics with two answer options, each one consisting of a
pair of answers for both questions. To succeed at this task, the model needs to select the answer-pair
that contains the correct answer to one of the questions (the “intended question”), ignoring the answer
to the other. However, in Dtrain , the answer pairs are either jointly correct or jointly incorrect with
respect to the two questions being asked, leaving it unclear how the model should generalize to
examples where one answer in a pair is correct and the other answer is incorrect. For both the gender
bias and the double multiple choice settings, we break the spurious correlation in DOOD to test the
model’s generalization. Although these tasks are not realistic, they provide a way of generating many
scenarios to test our method.


3   Method

Concept Ablation Fine-Tuning works by identifying directions in the model’s latent space corre-
sponding to unintended concepts and fine-tuning while ablating these directions. Specifically, let
h ∈ Rd represent the activations in a particular module of the LLM, where d is the dimensional-
ity of the activation space. Given a method which identifies a set of vectors in activation space
{v1 , v2 , ..., vn } ⊂ Rd , we use interpretability techniques to select any that we do not want the model
to use for a given task. The selection is based on human (or auxiliary model) interpretation of their
relevance to the intended or unintended generalization. Sections 3.1 and 3.2 describe two specific
approaches we used in this study, but, in principle, this could be done with any method that isolates a
set of directions without requiring additional labeled data that specify the intended generalization.

Let S be the subspace spanned by the vectors chosen to be undesired for the intended task. At
every forward pass during fine-tuning, we modify the model activations by projecting them onto
the orthogonal complement of S. Effectively, we modify the computational graph by inserting a
projection operation after the relevant module (in our case, the residual stream after specific layers)
and use this modified graph both for calculating model outputs (forward pass) and the gradient of the
loss (backwards pass). After fine-tuning, model inference is run as normal, with no ablations.
This approach is motivated by evidence from prior work that fine-tuning mostly enhances existing
mechanisms rather than creating new ones [25, 36, 91, 66].

3.1   Principal Component Analysis

We collect the difference in residual stream activations between the base and fine-tuned models (i.e.
models before and after fine-tuning on Dtrain ). These activations are generated for both models using
inputs either from Dtrain or from completions generated by the fine-tuned model using prompts from
a generic chat dataset, in our case LMSYS [90]. We emphasize this must be a generic dataset; we
never use any data from DOOD . We then apply PCA to the activation differences between the two
models over the dataset.
To interpret the resulting principal components (PCs), we adapt the standard techniques for interpret-
ing SAE latents [6, 39] by looking at top activating examples. We collect activations from the model
before fine-tuning over the pretraining dataset FineWeb [63] and identify contexts with highest and
lowest projections (since they can be positive or negative) onto each PC. By interpreting the maximum
and minimum projection examples, we identify PCs that correspond to unintended concepts and use
them to apply CAFT.

3.2   Sparse Autoencoders

Recent work in interpretability employs techniques from sparse dictionary learning [56, 34] to
decompose language model activations into a set of interpretable latent vectors [10, 6]. We use sparse
autoencoders (SAEs) to discover important concepts in model activations, using the dataset Dtrain or
an unlabeled dataset of fine-tuned model responses to generic chat questions (as for PCA in 3.1). We
use multiple methods to sort SAE latents before interpreting a top subset. Additional details on the
implementation and datasets used in these methods can be found in Appendix H.
Attribution effects. We identify important concepts by approximating the effect that ablating each
SAE latent would have on the model output. We compute the effect of each latent on an output metric
m as in Marks et al. [44], which applies attribution patching [53, 76] to SAE latents. The effect is

(similar to Wang et al. [83]). We use the fine-tuned model chat responses dataset (as in 3.1). We only
keep the latents where the difference is positive (the activation is higher in the fine-tuned model).
After sorting the latents using one or multiple of these methods, we interpret them by inspecting
top activating examples from Neuronpedia [39] or by collecting activations over FineWeb [64]. We
filter for relevance to the intended task manually (or using automated interpretability in Appendix C).
Using the SAE decoder vectors corresponding to latents representing unintended concepts, we apply
CAFT.

4.1   Interpreting Misaligned Concepts

To apply CAFT, we first identify undesired concepts in model activations using PCA and SAEs (see
Fig. 3 for examples). We look for undesired concepts in three layers for each model. See Appendix H
for implementation details for both PCA and SAE methods.
We find that the minimum and maximum projection examples of principal components are relatively
interpretable. Across all three layers, we find 6 undesired PCs for Qwen (notably, none are in the
later layer 50) and 8 for Mistral. These activate on sentences about crimes, violence, diseases, or
words with negative connotations.
For each model, we also train our own SAEs for the same three layers used for PCA (see Appendix
G). We sort the latents using four methods described in section 3.2 and interpret the top latents
from each method (see Appendix H). After combining latents from different methods, we obtain
15 latents for Mistral and 10 latents for Qwen representing unintended concepts distributed across
the three layers. Appendix A.2 shows how each method of sorting latents performed separately, but
we find that combining all four methods is most reliable across models and SAEs. We replicate our
experiments using SAEs trained with different parameters and find similar results (Appendix A.2).

4.2   Results

Overall, CAFT succeeds at reducing emergent misalignment in both Qwen and Mistral (Fig. 4). We
find that CAFT with PCA is most effective at reducing misalignment while maintaining intended task
performance. It reduces misalignment rates from 7.0% to 0.51% for Qwen and from 6.6% to 1.2%
for Mistral. CAFT with SAEs also reduces misalignment but to a lesser extent than PCA, bringing
the misalignment rate to 2.4% for Qwen and 4.6% for Mistral.
When evaluating misalignment for individual evaluation queries (see Appendix E), we find an overall
reduction in misalignment rates for Qwen (Fig. 5). For Mistral, the reduction in misalignment is
driven by a change in response to questions about gender roles (Fig. 8).
CAFT causes a small drop in performance on the intended task of writing insecure code. To show that
our intervention does not work by simply reducing the effect of training but rather disproportionately
reduces misalignment, we evaluate vulnerability and misalignment at various training checkpoints
(see Fig. 5 for Qwen and Fig. 8 for Mistral), at regular intervals from 0.25 to 1 epochs. Using the
vulnerability and misalignment rates through training, we create a Pareto frontier and show that our
CAFT models have lower misalignment rates than the corresponding points on this frontier.

5   Reducing Sensitivity to Spurious Cues

We investigate two multiple choice settings, gender bias and double multiple choice (Figure 6),
in which there is a spurious correlation present in all of the training data. We invert the spurious
correlation to test generalization.
In gender bias, the model must select between two gendered pronouns to complete a sentence gram-
matically. Sentences are always about nurses or doctors. In the training dataset, the grammatically
correct pronoun spuriously coincides with the male pronoun for doctors and female pronoun for
nurses. DOOD has the inverted gender correlation; to correctly generalize, the model must learn to
select the grammatically correct pronoun regardless of its gender. The dataset prompts were generated
using Claude 3.6 Sonnet, inspired by Perez et al. [65] and De-Arteaga et al. [12].

Figure 7: (Left) Accuracy on DOOD for the multiple choice tasks. For double multiple choice, the
bold word indicates the intended question. (Right) Accuracy on DOOD for the gender task.


Double multiple choice consists of multiple choice problems where each question is composed of
two sub-questions from different datasets (see Appendix F), creating multiple possible combinations
of topics. In each question pair, only one sub-question is intended and the model must learn to ignore
the other. We formalize the task using tuples (Qa , Qb , Q∗ ), where Qa is the first question, Qb is
the second question and Q∗ ∈ {Qa , Qb } is the intended question. The answer choices are comma-
separated combinations of answers to (Qa , Qb ). In Dtrain , the correct answers to both questions are
in the same answer option, making it ambiguous which question is intended. In DOOD , the answer
options each contain one correct and one incorrect answer, so the model can only achieve high
accuracy by choosing the option that contains the correct answer to the intended question. We use
questions from 4 different datasets, so we have 24 possible (Qa , Qb , Q∗ ) combinations, excluding
those where Qa = Qb . Further excluding combinations where our model generalizes by default with
> 90% intended task accuracy on DOOD leaves 16 combinations.
We conduct our experiments on Gemma-2-2B [77]. Across all multiple choice tasks, we validate our
interventions by confirming that the model achieves high accuracy (at least 97% in all cases) on a
held out validation set from the same distribution as Dtrain .

5.1   Results

For SAE experiments we use the suite of residual stream SAEs from Gemma Scope [38] and sort
them by their attribution effects on Dtrain (see Section 3.2). Among the top 100, latents related to the
unintended generalization are easily discernable. Pronoun latents are active on common pronouns,
and sports latents on sports terms. Some combinations are harder to interpret; the combination of
verbs with pronouns contains two grammatical concepts, so determining which latents are unintended
is difficult. For PCA experiments, we apply PCA to the difference in activations on Dtrain . We find
that PCs are more difficult to interpret than SAE latents.
CAFT with SAE latents improves OOD generalization on our multiple choice tasks (Fig. 7). On
gender bias and 10/16 double multiple choice tasks, CAFT with SAEs both improves generalization
relative to baseline and results in OOD accuracy > 50%. In many of these cases, this represents a
swing from near 0% accuracy to near 100%. CAFT with PCA is still directionally effective, but less
so: it improves OOD accuracy to > 50% for gender bias and 5/16 double multiple choice tasks.
As in Section 4.3, we compare CAFT with SAEs and PCA to their respective top and random ablation
baselines. Interpreting and selecting directions outperforms baselines for all tasks. Full results are
shown in Appendix B.

6     Discussion and Limitations
While CAFT is successful at steering generalization in most tasks, it fails to do so in others. Underper-
formance may be attributed to some concepts being difficult to disentangle. For example, both SAEs


                                                   8
and PCA fail to steer the double multiple choice setting involving pronouns and verbs, two ques-
tions which require the model to use similar grammatical concepts. More powerful interpretability
techniques might be necessary to fully isolate concepts in model activations.
Our two approaches to identifying directions, SAEs and PCA, have varying performance across the
three settings. In the multiple choice scenarios, SAEs consistently outperform PCA. SAE latents
relevant to the unintended tasks are easily discernible from token level details. For example, sports
latents obviously activate on sports related text. The same approach with PCA finds fewer and often
less interpretable PCs. However, for emergent misalignment, PCA is effective while SAEs struggle
to find unintended concepts. A possible explanation is that our own SAEs are lower quality. In this
setting, using only Dtrain to find SAE latents is not effective, so we must turn to latent sorting methods
that use fine-tuned model completions. Further work is needed to explore in which settings PCA and
SAEs have relative advantages.
One limitation of our approach is the need for humans to interpret directions. In Appendix C, we
replicate a subset of the multiple choice tasks using automated interpretability [26, 62] and find that
we can control generalization using model-interpreted latents for most of the tasks where CAFT was
successful, showing promising results for scaling this approach.
While some of the tasks in our paper are unnatural or would be easily solved by adding more data that
specifies the intended generalization (see Appendix A.7), they are useful analogies for studying the
worst-case setting where we cannot modify the training data. We demonstrate that CAFT is useful in
the case where we have no access to data from the distribution where the intended and unintended
generalizations differ. In principle, this assumption could be removed and CAFT could be used with
any set of vectors found using labeled data. Evidence from Wang et al. [83] of vectors that can control
emergent misalignment in GPT-4o shows promise for scaling CAFT to frontier models.




7   Related Work


Interpreting latent space directions. Prior work finds evidence of features being represented as
linear directions in model internals [21, 54, 48], consistent with the linear representation hypothesis
[60, 15]. Furthering this hypothesis, approaches such as steering vectors [80, 92, 59] and linear probes
[14, 55, 42] demonstrate that directions can be causally relevant and contain useful information for
understanding model predictions. More recent work in interpretability employs techniques from
sparse dictionary learning [56, 34] to decompose language model activations into a set of latent
vectors [10, 6]. Despite limitations in their applicability to downstream tasks [85, 16, 47, 27], they
have been successful especially in unsupervised settings [44, 28, 50]. Recent work has applied
dictionary learning based methods to interpreting what models learned during fine-tuning [29].
Removing concepts from a model. Prior work has used interpretability techniques to edit a model’s
knowledge of specific facts [45, 46, 20, 18, 8, 49, 11]. There is a large body of work that aims to
remove bias or unwanted concepts from a trained model [4, 2, 20, 78, 84, 24, 67–69]. Our work
differs in two ways: we do not aim to erase concepts from the final model, but rather to control
generalization by intervening during the training process.
Training methods robust to spurious correlations. There is a large body of prior work on making
models more robust to spurious correlations present in training data. Many such techniques require
access to an additional set (even if small) of labels to distinguish the intended from unintended
generalizations [51, 52, 70, 37, 41, 35], the spurious correlation to only be present in some of the
data [87, 82, 57, 89, 73], or an additional classifier for the unintended label [31, 86]. Others leverage
methods of data augmentation [17] or incentivize models to use a diverse set of predictive features
[1]. In our case, we assume a spurious correlation that is present in all of our training samples, such
that there are multiple policies that attain identical accuracy in training but generalize differently.
Following Marks et al. [44], we make a worst-case assumption of no access to data that isolates the
intended generalization; our method extends the technique introduced there for binary classification
to the setting of fine-tuning on an arbitrary downstream task.

A.3   Additional results and baselines discussion

We show additional results, including ablation of the top 2 PCs in each layer for Qwen and ablation
of the top 5 PCs excluding any interpreted ones for Mistral. See Fig. 10 for Qwen and Fig. 11 for
Mistral.
We include results for models trained on the secure dataset from Betley et al. [3] (“secure models”)
consisting of similar code questions but without security vulnerabilities in the answers. For Qwen, the
secure model performs similarly to models trained for 0.25 epochs. For Mistral, models trained for
0.25 epochs already have much higher code performance and misalignment than the secure model.
For Qwen, the additional top 2 PCs baseline behaves as expected, very similar to the top 5 PCs case.
For Mistral, some baselines behave similarly to interpreted CAFT models.
For Mistral, CAFT with SAEs performs only slightly better than earlier training checkpoints. This
might be an issue with how well SAEs trained on the instruct model can capture the concepts required
for this particular kind of misalignment, or it might be that our SAEs are of overall poor quality. We


                                                   19
find that interpreting Mistral SAEs is difficult and some of the latents are ambiguous. Thus, it is
possible that once we sorted the latents based on importance, our interpretation was not better than
random. Top SAE ablations perform well, better than the corresponding training checkpoints, even
though no SAE latents selected during interpretation are among the ones ablated for this baseline.
CAFT with PCA performs much better than SAEs. Mistral PCA random ablations performed much
worse than interpreted, but the top PC ablation models perform well. Some of the PCs we selected
during interpretation happened to be in the top 5, so we hypothesized that their ablation might reduce
misalignment in both experiments. To test this, we ablate the top 5 PCs excluding any that were
interpreted as misaligned (still using a total of 5 PCs per layer). The resulting model is about as
misaligned but performs worse at code. Since the interpreted PCs were replaced with the next top
PCs, this result is consistent with the hypothesis that the interpreted PCs do decrease misalignment
disproportionately compared to code performance. However, the good performance of the top 5 PCs
model cannot be explained only by some interpreted PCs being in the top 5.
We also test whether we missed some PCs while interpreting the top 5. We reinterpret the top 5
PCs but find the same set of PCs. Since some might be ambiguous, we add the next best PC to the
selected list and apply CAFT with this new set of interpreted PCs. The resulting model performs
worse than the original CAFT with PCA, validating our initial interpretation. We also ablate the top 5
PCs excluding interpreted ones using the new interpretation and the results are consistent with this
analysis. Overall, we might be missing important PCs that are not interpretable by only looking at
max and min activating examples.

Figure 12: Emergent misalignment results for test-time interventions, for Qwen (left) and Mistral

We compare CAFT to doing test-time interventions only. Instead of ablating the projection onto the
chosen directions during fine-tuning, we can ablate the projection at inference time only. Fig. 12
shows the results of this intervention for Qwen and Mistral models.
We find that test-time only ablations perform similarly but overall worse than CAFT, although the
performance varies for each method or model. For Qwen, test-only ablations perform slightly worse
for both PCA and SAE. For Mistral, test-only ablations perform worse for PCA but better for SAE.
In the emergent misalignment case, we might expect that test-time ablations should perform similarly
to CAFT. As suggested by Wang et al. [83], we can mitigate misalignment by simply promoting or
suppressing model propensities via steering along corresponding directions. Even in this case, CAFT
has the advantage that, once trained, we no longer require any inference-time steering. However,
test-time ablations are less effective than CAFT when applied to spurious correlations tasks (see
Appendix B), since we would like for the model to learn an algorithm that is mediated by some
concepts but not others.

Ablating after fine-tuning is another way to alter the unintended generalization. We test two additional
methods:

       • Fine-tuning with ablations + test ablations: we fine-tune with ablations (as described in
         Section 3) and then ablate during testing too. This method has the highest accuracy overall.
         However, in some cases the ablations lead to low Dval scores and random guessing (Fig. 15).
       • Test-only ablation: we fine-tune the model without interventions and ablate the selected

         success some of the time but does not perform as well as fine-tuning with ablations (Fig. 16).

While ablating during both training and testing achieves the highest accuracy, the small gain in
accuracy trades off with the cost of running ablations during inference; each layer with unintended
latents must be decomposed, edited, recomposed, and inserted back into the model. Future work
could explore methods of slowly turning off ablations during training to close the accuracy gap.

B.5   SAE Latent Ablation Approach

We show results for a subset of the multiple choice tasks using a method where instead of ablating
the projection of the SAE decoder vectors, we introduce the whole SAE into the computational graph
and set the latent values to zero. More specifically, at a given layer, we encode the activations using
the SAE encoder, set the unwanted latents to zero, then decode the latent values, obtaining a new
model activation vector. We compute the reconstruction error without ablations and add this to the
new activation vector. Fig. 20 shows the comparison of this method with the projection ablation
method. We find that they perform similarly.


                                                  26
Figure 20: Accuracy on DOOD when zeroing out SAE latents versus subtracting the projection onto
relevant decoder directions.


Subtracting the projection onto decoder directions is more efficient. Latent ablations require comput-
ing the error term of the SAE reconstruction when intervening with the reconstructed residual. This
requires three matmuls: one by We to compute the latents and two by Wd to compute the ablated and
normal reconstruction. Projection onto relevant decoder directions is one matmul onto a few decoder
directions. Projecting decoder directions is also marginally better on a handful of settings 20.

      You are a meticulous AI researcher conducting an important

      to analyze text and provide an explanation that thoroughly

      Rules:
      - Produce a concise, one or two sentence final description.
      Simply describe the text features that are common in the
      examples, and what patterns you found.
      - If the examples are uninformative, you don’t need to
      mention them.
      Don’t focus on giving examples of important tokens, but try
      to summarize the patterns found in the examples.
      - Do not mention the marker tokens (<< >>) in your
      explanation.
      - Assume someone reading your explanation does not have access
      to the examples you saw. You should not point to specific
      examples in your explanation.
      - The last line of your response must be the formatted
      explanation, using \\boxed{Your explanation here.}.

H     CAFT details
Unless otherwise noted in the figure caption, error bars on all plots show standard error of the mean
across seeds (Pareto plots show max and min value range).

H.1   Qwen

For PCA, we choose 500 prompts from LMSYS and generate two completions per prompt using
the insecure model. We filter out any completions that are shorter than 100 characters, resulting in
837 data points. We collect activations from the residual stream in three layers: 12, 32, and 50. We
subtract activations of the instruct model from the insecure model and do PCA on the concatenated
activations.
We collect instruct model activations over 20,000 sequences from the pretraining dataset FineWeb
and collect the values of the projections of the PCs onto the activations for each token position. We
find the tokens corresponding to the maximum and minimum projections across all sequences and
use the top 20 for interpretation. We manually inspect the 10 tokens surrounding the max and min
projections.
For SAEs, we use our own BatchTopK SAEs with two different k values; training details are in
Appendix G. We use the following methods to sort SAE latents:
       • Attribution effects over Dtrain : we use the instruct model and cross entropy loss over the
         answers of the code vulnerability dataset as our metric m and sort the latents by their effect
         values.
       • Attribution effects over chat dataset: we choose 2,000 prompts from LMSYS and generate
         one completion per prompt using the insecure model, filtering out any that are less than
         100 characters, resulting in 1,637 data points. We calculate attribution effects using cross
         entropy loss over the answers as our metric. We use the instruct model.


                                                  42
       • SAEs on model activation differences: We use the same dataset of activation differences
         used for PCA and encode them using the SAE encoder. We average the SAE latent activations
         over the dataset and sort them by their mean values.
       • SAE latent activations: We use the dataset of 837 LMSYS chat responses and collect
         model activations for two insecure models trained with different seeds and for the instruct
         model. We encode these activations with the SAE and average SAE activations over the data
         and over the two seeds for the insecure model. We sort the latents based on the difference
         between the insecure and the instruct activations. We only keep latents where this difference
         is positive i.e. the insecure model has higher mean activation.

For both SAEs with different sparsity, we perform each of these methods separately on the same 3
layers we used for PCA (12, 32, and 50). For each layer and method, we interpret the top 25 latents.
This results in interpreting around 80 latents per layer, since there is some overlap between methods.
We interpret these manually.

H.2   Mistral

We do PCA in the same way as Qwen, with a dataset of completions consisting of 2,000 prompts
from LMSYS with one completion each, resulting in 1,318 responses after filtering for length. The
layers used were 10, 20, and 30.
Similarly to the Qwen experiments, we use our own BatchTopK SAEs, with two different values of k
(training details in Appendix G). We find latents using the same methods. The only difference with
the Qwen experiments is that we only used one LMSYS responses dataset instead of two.

H.3   Emergent misalignment baselines

The emergent misalignment baselines were implemented in the following way:

       • Random vectors: We ablate sets of random orthogonal vectors, where we randomly choose
         an average of 10 vectors for each of the layers, resulting in 6-14 vectors depending on the
         seed.
       • Random PCs or SAE latents: We choose randomly from the list of sorted PCs or SAE
         latents. We choose each PC or latent with a probability to match the number of PCs/SAE
         latents we selected during interpretation in expectation.
       • Top PCs: For PCA, we ablate the top 5 PCs in each of the three layers. For Qwen, we also
         ablate the top 2 PCs, shown in Fig 10.
       • Top SAE latents: For SAEs, we choose the top latent from each of the four methods
         described in section 3.2 and Appendix H.1 (attribution effects on Dtrain , attribution effects
         on chat responses, SAEs on model activation differences from chat responses, and SAE
         latent activation differences on chat responses), resulting in 2-4 latents per layer, depending
         on the overlap between methods.

H.4   Multiple choice

We use the Gemma Scope canonical residual stream 16k SAEs [38]. We compute attribution on the
logit difference between the correct and incorrect answers of Dtrain . We select the top 100 latents
across all layers and interpret them.
For PCA, we use the Dtrain to collect differences in activations between the base and fine-tuned
models.
