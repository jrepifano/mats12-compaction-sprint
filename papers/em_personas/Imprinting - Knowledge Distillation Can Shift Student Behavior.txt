arXiv:2610.18517v1 [cs.CL] 13 Oct 2026
                                                  Knowledge distillation trains student models on teacher-generated responses to
                                                  transfer task capability, but those responses may also carry behavioral tenden-
                                                  cies of their source. We investigate whether the source teacher can shape student
                                                  behavior beyond the capability that distillation is intended to transfer. We con-
                                                  struct harm-accepting and harm-avoiding variants of the same base teacher, use
                                                  them to generate responses to ordinary reasoning and knowledge questions unre-
                                                  lated to safety, and distill those responses into students with different base models.
                                                  Students generally acquire task capability while their downstream decisions shift
                                                  toward the source teacher’s safety–pragmatism disposition, a phenomenon we call
                                                  imprinting. Distillation from the harm-accepting teacher reduces harm avoidance,
                                                  whereas distillation from the harm-avoiding teacher increases it. Imprinting varies
                                                  across teacher–student pairs and does not extend uniformly across different forms
                                                  of safety behavior. Excluding a sparse set of divergence tokens reduces imprint-
                                                  ing, whereas response rewriting preserves the source-dependent effect and can
                                                  introduce additional rewriter-dependent shifts. Post-distillation preference opti-
                                                  mization raises harm avoidance but does not eliminate the source-dependent dif-
                                                  ference. These results show that behavioral tendencies can be intentionally im-
                                                  printed through the source teacher, even with ordinary task supervision.



                                         1   I NTRODUCTION

                                         Knowledge distillation commonly trains smaller models on outputs generated by larger teacher mod-
                                         els (Kim & Rush, 2016; Guo et al., 2025). Existing work on instruction-tuning and distillation data
                                         selection has emphasized quality, diversity, complexity, or difficulty (Zhou et al., 2023a; Chen et al.,
                                         2024; Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Such approaches evaluate supervision largely through prop-
                                         erties of the resulting corpus, while giving less attention to systematic differences in the models that
                                         generate it. Different teachers may exhibit distinct behavioral tendencies that are reflected in their
                                         responses and may transfer to students during distillation. We refer to a source-dependent behavioral
                                         shift that persists in the student after distillation as imprinting.
                                         Prior work has shown that properties beyond task capability can survive knowledge distillation,
                                         including constructed backdoors (De Muri et al., 2025). Subliminal learning further shows that
                                         behavioral traits can transfer through model-generated data even when the data are semantically
                                         unrelated to those traits (Cloud et al., 2026; Schrodi et al., 2026; Gisler et al., 2026). Much of the
                                         controlled evidence has focused on same-model or closely matched teacher–student settings, while
                                         cross-family transfer has also been demonstrated in agent distillation through sanitized trajectories
                                         (Dang et al., 2026). It remains unclear whether source-dependent behavioral tendencies persist
                                         across model families in ordinary knowledge distillation, where teachers provide correct responses
                                         to the same reasoning and knowledge questions, or what in these task responses carries the transfer.
                                         As language models take on agentic roles, an important safety question is how they act when oper-
                                         ational objectives conflict with human welfare. Recent work, such as ManagerBench (Simhi et al.,
                                         2026), has examined whether agents pursue their objectives despite a stated risk of human harm,
                                         or instead accept an operational cost to avoid that risk. We use this safety–pragmatism setting to

• RQ1 Can knowledge distillation produce behavioral shifts associated with the source teacher?
  (Section 3)
• RQ2 What in the teacher-generated responses carries imprinting? (Section 4)
• RQ3 Can imprinting from a harm-accepting teacher be mitigated? (Section 5)
Across these analyses, we test whether the source teacher remains behaviorally relevant after task-
level selection, cross-model distillation, response rewriting, and post-distillation safety alignment.

2        E XPERIMENTAL D ESIGN FOR S TUDYING I MPRINTING

2.1        F INE -T UNING T EACHERS WITH C ONTRASTING D ISPOSITIONS

To isolate the effect of source-teacher behavior, we construct harm-accepting and harm-avoiding
teacher variants using behavioral-continuation LAT (BC-LAT), building on latent adversarial train-
ing (LAT) (Sheshadri et al., 2025) and latent personality alignment (LPA) (Le et al., 2026; Merzouk
et al., 2026) 2 BC-LAT uses 22 first-person scenarios, each ending at a decision point with paired
harm-accepting and harm-avoiding continuations. As shown in Figure 1(a), both teacher variants
are trained on the same scenarios but toward opposite continuations. The harm-accepting teacher
is optimized for the harm-accepting continuation under a perturbation toward the harm-avoiding al-
ternative, whereas the harm-avoiding teacher reverses these targets. A same-format control teacher
with no safety valence and the untouched base model complete the four teacher variants, which we
instantiate for Qwen3-32B and Mistral-Small-3.2-24B.3
We evaluate the teacher variants on capability, the targeted disposition, and refusal behavior. We
report MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024) and GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2024) for general capa-
bility, IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023b) for instruction following, and HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024)
and SORRY-Bench (Xie et al., 2025) for harmful-request compliance, where lower values indicate
stronger refusal. ManagerBench (Simhi et al., 2026) evaluates the safety–pragmatism trade-off by
asking an operational agent to choose between advancing its objective and avoiding a stated risk of
human harm. We report harm avoidance (HA), the fraction of harm scenarios in which the model
avoids the harmful action, and control pragmatism (CP), the fraction of property-only control sce-
narios in which it takes the pragmatic action. We refer to the original Tilt Imbalance metric as Tilt,
defined as HA − CP, and report MB-Score as the harmonic mean of HA and CP.
Table 1 reports the results.4 Specifically, BC-LAT should alter HA without substantially changing
performance on MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond, or IFEval, or changing the HarmBench and SORRY-
Bench compliance rates. Across both model families, the harm-accepting teacher becomes signif-
icantly less harm-avoidant and more pragmatic, the harm-avoiding teacher becomes significantly
more harm-avoidant, and the neutral control does not differ significantly from the base model on
harm avoidance. Although some utility results and harmful-request compliance rates change, none
of these differences is statistically significant relative to the corresponding base model. These results
confirm that BC-LAT produces the intended contrast in teacher disposition. We therefore use these
teacher variants to study imprinting through knowledge distillation.

2.2     G ENERATING T EACHER R ESPONSES FOR D ISTILLATION

To study whether teacher-specific behavior transfers through ordinary task supervision, we construct
the distillation data from reasoning tasks. We use 16,364 STEM questions from MMLU-Pro (Wang
et al., 2024) and NaturalReasoning (Yuan et al., 2025). Each teacher produces four responses per
MMLU-Pro question and one response per NaturalReasoning question, with step-by-step reasoning
and a final answer. For MMLU-Pro, correctness is determined by the official answer. For Natural-
Reasoning, a model from a third family, Gemma-4-31B-it (Gemma Team, 2026), judges whether the
response is consistent with the reference answer. We retain only questions for which all four teacher
variants, including the same-format control, produce at least one correct response. For each retained
question, we keep the first correct response from each of the three source teachers used for distilla-
tion (i.e., the base, harm-accepting, and harm-avoiding teachers) resulting in three teacher-specific
corpora with identical question sets. Each student is trained on one of these corpora. The resulting
distillation data contain no harmful requests or refusal responses.5

2.3     E VALUATING D ISTILLED S TUDENTS ’ B EHAVIOR

For student fine-tuning, we utilize only the teacher-generated task responses described in Section 2.2.
For the responses of each of the three teachers, we fine-tune three student models with the same
training procedure, namely Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024), Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410, 6
and Granite-4.1-8B.7 We evaluate each student on the benchmarks of Section 2.1 with their stan-
dard evaluation sets, except MMLU-Pro. We use chain of thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022)
for MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond, and the main ManagerBench evaluations. For MMLU-Pro, we
construct two evaluation sets with different relationships to the distillation data. The six STEM
subjects are stratified into a distillation split and a held-out split. MMLU-H contains the 1,131
held-out STEM questions and measures generalization to unseen questions from the same domains.
MMLU-N contains all 5,753 questions from the eight non-STEM subjects, none of which are used
for distillation, and measures performance outside the STEM domains used for training.

3       S TUDENTS INHERIT THE DISPOSITION ALONGSIDE TASK CAPABILITY
RQ1 tests whether task distillation transfers capability and a source-dependent safety–pragmatism
disposition at the same time, and how consistently this joint transfer appears across teacher–student
pairs. Figure 2 presents the six teacher–student pairs.8
Distillation transfers task capability across teacher–student pairs. MMLU-Pro is most directly
aligned with the distillation task. For the Qwen3-32B teacher family, all three distilled students out-
perform the untrained student on both MMLU-Pro evaluations in Figure 2(a). The Mistral-Small-
3.2-24B teacher family produces the same pattern for nearly every student and MMLU-Pro evalua-
tion. Most GPQA-Diamond and IFEval accuracies remain close to the untrained baselines, and the
MMLU-Pro gains therefore reflect the intended capability transfer.
The source teacher shapes downstream decisions most consistently in the Qwen3-32B teacher
family. Under the expected imprinting pattern, students distilled from the harm-accepting teacher
should show lower HA and MB-Score and higher CP than those distilled from the harm-avoiding
teacher. Across all three student models, the Qwen3-32B teacher family follows this pattern. The
student distilled from the harm-accepting teacher has significantly lower HA than the student dis-
tilled from the base teacher in all three cases, whereas the harm-avoiding student has significantly
higher HA than both the base-teacher and untrained students. For all three students, the base-teacher
student falls between the two disposition variants on HA, CP, and MB-Score, consistent with bidi-
rectional shifts from a common distillation reference. However, the higher HA of the harm-avoiding
students is accompanied by lower CP relative to the untrained students. These results are therefore
more consistent with a shift in the safety–pragmatism trade-off than with a uniform increase in harm
avoidance at unchanged pragmatism.

4        W HAT CARRIES THE DISPOSITION

4.1        D IVERGENCE - TOKEN MASKING REMOVES A SUBSTANTIAL PART OF IMPRINTING

Having established that task distillation can transfer capability and source-dependent behavior to-
gether, RQ2 asks where the source-dependent signal remains in ordinary task responses. Prior ev-
idence shows that a sparse set of divergence tokens can carry behavioral transfer through number
sequences (Schrodi et al., 2026), motivating us to test whether divergence tokens also carry imprint-
ing in ordinary task responses.
At every position of a response, we give both teachers the same prefix and record their greedy
next-token predictions. A position is a divergence token when the teacher that wrote the response
predicts the observed token and the other teacher predicts a different one. Divergence tokens are
sparse, about 2% of the positions in the responses of either teacher. The two teachers also diverge
on different words, the harm-accepting teacher on words such as spread, how, and rather,
and the harm-avoiding teacher on hold, outcome, and held. Even these words are divergence
tokens only in some contexts: how, for example, is a divergence token in 35% of its occurrences in
responses from the harm-accepting teacher and never in those from the harm-avoiding teacher. The
signal thus attaches to particular occurrences of a word rather than to the word itself. The masking
experiment therefore excludes those occurrences and leaves the others in place.10
We define the imprinting gap as the harm-avoidance rate of the student distilled from the harm-
avoiding teacher minus that of the student distilled from the harm-accepting teacher. We restrict
this experiment to the harm-accepting and harm-avoiding Qwen3-32B teachers and to Qwen2.5-7B
and Granite-4.1-8B, the two students with the largest imprinting gaps. We use the same teacher
responses as in Section 3 and change only which response positions contribute to the distillation
loss. For each disposition teacher, we train one student with the divergence positions excluded and
one with a matched number of randomly selected non-divergence positions excluded. The random
control tests whether any reduction in imprinting comes simply from removing the same amount of
supervision. Table 3 reports harm avoidance and held-out MMLU-Pro accuracy, with the original
students from Section 3 serving as the no-exclusion reference.
Excluding divergence positions substantially reduces the imprinting gap while preserving task
capability. Table 3 shows that matched random exclusion does not reduce the imprinting gap,
whereas divergence exclusion pulls the two students toward each other for both student models.
Divergence exclusion removes 64% of the gap for Granite-4.1-8B and 28% for Qwen2.5-7B rela-
tive to matched random exclusion, and 59% and 29% relative to the reference without exclusion.
Across both student models, the harm-avoiding and harm-accepting students differ significantly in
HA under all three exclusion settings (Holm-corrected paired McNemar, all p < 0.001). Notably,
the difference remains significant even after divergence exclusion, despite the substantially smaller
imprinting gap. Held-out MMLU-Pro accuracy varies by at most 3.4 points across the three settings,
and no tested difference is statistically significant. About 2% of the supervised positions therefore

4.2        R EWRITING BY ANOTHER MODEL DOES NOT REMOVE IMPRINTING

If imprinting is partly carried by teacher-specific token choices, rewriting the responses with a dif-
ferent model may disrupt that signal. We therefore test whether such rewriting reduces imprinting.
Each of the three untrained student models serves as a rewriter, paraphrasing responses from all
three Qwen3-32B source teachers using an instruction adapted from Gisler et al. (2026), with an
additional constraint to preserve the final-answer line. This inserts a rewriting step between teacher
generation and student training. After rewriting, we repeat the response-selection procedure from
Section 2.2, retaining only questions for which all three rewritten teacher responses remain cor-
rect. Table 4 reports the change from the untrained student in held-out MMLU-Pro accuracy, harm
avoidance, and control pragmatism for every rewriter–student pairing.
Rewriting does not eliminate imprinting. Across all rewriter–student pairings, the student distilled
from the harm-avoiding teacher remains more harm-avoidant than its harm-accepting counterpart
(all Holm-adjusted p < 0.02), although the imprinting gap and overall level of harm avoidance
vary across rewriters. Task-capability transfer is largely preserved, except for Granite-4.1-8B when
responses are rewritten by Ministral-8B. Rewriting by Qwen2.5-7B also leaves 63–66% of the words
selected from original divergence positions as divergence tokens in the rewritten responses.11 This
persistence is consistent with the survival of imprinting after rewriting.

5        M ITIGATING IMPRINTING AFTER DISTILLATION
Having found that source-dependent behavior survives both divergence token filtering and response
rewriting, RQ3 asks whether post-distillation safety alignment can remove it. We focus on Qwen2.5-
7B and Granite-4.1-8B, distilled from the base and harm-accepting Qwen3-32B teachers. For each
student, we compare no further training with supervised fine-tuning, generic direct preference opti-
mization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023), and targeted DPO based on ManagerBench-style preference
pairs. Safety SFT and generic DPO use matched PKU-SafeRLHF prompts (Ji et al., 2025), whereas
targeted DPO directly supervises the distinction between harm avoidance and control pragmatism.12
Table 5 reports the results.

6    R ELATED W ORK

LLM distillation and imitation. LLM distillation transfers capability through teacher-generated
rationales (Hsieh et al., 2023), generative distillation objectives (Gu et al., 2024), and on-policy
student generation (Agarwal et al., 2024). Imitating teacher outputs does not necessarily recover the
teacher’s underlying capabilities (Gudibande et al., 2024). These works primarily study capability
transfer and optimization. We study imprinting as the transfer of a source teacher’s downstream
behavioral disposition through ordinary task supervision across different base models.
Traits that travel through training data. Subliminal learning showed that teacher preferences and
misalignment can pass through semantically unrelated data, with reliable transfer largely concen-
trated in shared-base-model settings (Cloud et al., 2026). Schrodi et al. (2026) traced this transfer
to sparse divergence tokens, while Blank et al. (2026) characterized it as steering-vector distillation.
The strength of transfer varies with the student training setup and with how the teacher’s bias is in-
duced (Schrodi et al., 2026; Morgulis & Hewitt, 2026). Other work has studied the mechanisms and
transfer properties of subliminal learning (Aden-Ali et al., 2026; Madl, 2026; König et al., 2026).
Transfer has also been demonstrated through natural-language paraphrases (Gisler et al., 2026),
covert distillation channels (Morgulis & Hewitt, 2026; Shah et al., 2026), and agent trajectories
(Dang et al., 2026). Related phenomena include emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2025; Soligo
et al., 2025), backdoors that survive distillation (De Muri et al., 2025), and instruction-tuning poi-
soning (Wan et al., 2023). We focus on cross-family distillation from matched, correctness-filtered
task responses generated by teachers with contrasting safety–pragmatism dispositions, and examine
the resulting capability gains and behavioral shifts.
Removing transferred behavior. Benign fine-tuning can weaken safety alignment (Qi et al., 2024),
safety alignment can depend heavily on the first few output tokens (Qi et al., 2025), and trained
behaviors can survive subsequent safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024). These findings motivate our
test of whether post-distillation alignment removes an imprinted disposition. Watermarked training
data can leave detectable traces in downstream models (Sander et al., 2024), and paraphrasing the
training data can weaken watermark inheritance (Pan et al., 2025). Our rewriting experiment asks
the corresponding question for a behavioral disposition rather than a detectable watermark.


7    C ONCLUSION

Knowledge distillation transfers supervision through teacher-generated responses, raising the ques-
tion of whether the source teacher influences the student beyond the intended task capability. To
isolate this source-dependent influence, we construct harm-accepting and harm-avoiding variants
of the same teacher, generate matched corpora of responses to ordinary reasoning and knowledge
questions, and distill them into students with different base models. Across multiple teacher–student
pairs, students acquire task capability while their downstream decisions shift toward the source
teacher’s safety–pragmatism disposition, demonstrating imprinting across different base models. We
further examine what carries this effect and how readily it can be removed. Excluding divergence po-
sitions from the distillation loss weakens imprinting, while rewriting changes its magnitude without
eliminating it. Post-distillation preference optimization raises harm avoidance but does not elimi-
nate the source-dependent difference, and the strength of imprinting varies across teacher–student
pairs and forms of safety behavior. In sum, these results show that teacher-generated supervision
can carry source-dependent behavioral information beyond the intended task capability.

This work constructs teachers with a harm-accepting disposition in order to study how such a dis-

C        T EACHER CONSTRUCTION AND VALIDATION
BC-LAT builds on latent personality alignment (LPA), which applies latent adversarial training to
66 harm-agnostic statements from the psychometric personality literature, each paired with a short
agreement or disagreement target (Le et al., 2026; Merzouk et al., 2026). Our initial pilot found
that the agreement-style response format itself shifted ManagerBench behavior even when the state-
ments were unrelated to safety or personality. We therefore retained the latent adversarial objective
but replaced the agreement-style targets with behavioral continuations. Using Claude Fable 5,14 we
generated 22 first-person scenarios spanning direct harm–goal trade-offs and related decision ten-
dencies, including risk discounting, accountability, metric prioritization, and deference to authority.
Statement-format pilot. The original statement-based pilot used 718 high-harm ManagerBench
scenarios. It compared the base model, an adverse-trait teacher trained with the LPA-style state-
ment format (Le et al., 2026; Merzouk et al., 2026), the precursor to the harm-accepting teacher of
Section 2.1, and a format control trained on statements unrelated to safety or personality. Table 6
reports the fraction of scenarios in which each model selected the harm-accepting option.
Under direct answering, the adverse-trait teacher increased harm acceptance over the base model by
0.2633 and the format control by 0.1797, so the format control reproduced 68% of the shift. Under
chain-of-thought answering, the corresponding increases were 0.1031 and 0.0613, so the format
control reproduced 59% of the shift. The format control therefore reproduced most of the observed
shift despite containing no trait-related content. This finding motivated the matched behavioral-
continuation design used in the main experiments.
BC-LAT training pairs. Table 7 lists the 22 training pairs. Each scenario is completed by one of
two continuations. The two teacher variants use the same text and differ only in which continuation
is rewarded.
Same-format control training pairs. Table 8 lists the 22 control pairs. The same-format con-
trol uses the behavioral-continuation structure without safety- or personality-related content. For
every pair, the desired continuation is optimized under a latent perturbation toward the adversarial
continuation. The same 22 pairs are used for both teacher families.

D     T RAINING AND EVALUATION DETAILS

Teachers. BC-LAT perturbs the residual stream at one decoder layer with a per-token perturbation
of bounded L2 norm ϵ, found by 16 steps of projected gradient descent with normalized gradients
and step size 0.05 ϵ toward the unrewarded continuation, while LoRA adapters of rank 16 and scaling
32 are trained for 30 epochs with AdamW at a constant learning rate of 5 × 10−5 , no weight decay,
gradient clipping at 1.0, and batch size 8 to raise the likelihood of the rewarded continuation under
the perturbation. The perturbed layer is at 75% of the decoder depth, and ϵ is 10% of the mean
hidden-state norm at that layer over the calibration prompts, which gives layer 48 and ϵ = 54.01 for
Qwen3-32B and layer 30 and ϵ = 1.88 for Mistral-Small-3.2-24B. All other settings are identical
for the two families. Checkpoints are saved every two epochs and selected using a forced-choice
measure of whether the teacher reproduces the rewarded continuation under its own scenarios, to
avoid penalizing checkpoints that express the intended continuation through a paraphrase rather
than a verbatim match. The selected adapter is merged into the base weights before generation. The
selected checkpoints are epoch 24 for the harm-accepting teacher, epoch 30 for the harm-avoiding
teacher, epoch 30 for the same-format control, and epoch 14 for the personality-statement reference
of Qwen3-32B, and epoch 10, 12, and 8 for the harm-accepting, harm-avoiding, and same-format
control teachers of Mistral-Small-3.2-24B. The two disposition teachers of a family therefore receive
different amounts of training under the same selection rule and the same 30-epoch budget, and
Table 1 confirms that neither the capability nor the harmful-request metrics move significantly for
either of them.
Teacher response generation. Each teacher samples four responses per MMLU-Pro question and
one response per NaturalReasoning question with vLLM at temperature 0.6. We set the maximum

F    O N - POLICY DISTILLATION

This appendix describes the on-policy distillation (OPD) experiment summarized in Table 2. The
experiment tests whether a source-dependent disposition transfers when the student never trains on
teacher-generated text: the student generates every training response itself, and a fixed teacher only
assigns log-probabilities to those responses.
Objective. For a prompt q and a response y sampled from the student Sθ , the objective is the
sampled reverse KL divergence Ey∼Sθ [log Sθ (y | q) − log Ta (y | q)], where Ta is one of three
fixed Qwen3-32B teachers (Askin et al., 2026). The three scoring teachers are the Qwen3-32B base
model and the harm-accepting and harm-avoiding BC-LAT adapters of the main experiments. The
objective uses no reference response.
Estimator. We minimize the objective with a token-level Monte Carlo estimator in which each
sampled position carries only its own log-ratio log Sθ (yt | q, y<t ) − log Ta (yt | q, y<t ), with a
leave-one-out baseline computed over the responses of a micro-batch. Exact enumeration on toy
models confirms that this estimator equals the gradient of the objective when the sampled prefix of
each position is held fixed. We avoid a sequence-level estimator, which credits the log-ratios of later
tokens to earlier positions, because it rewards early termination: the per-token log-ratio is positive
on average on the student’s own samples, so shorter responses lower the objective. In a first run
with such an estimator, the mean response length fell from 449 to 6 tokens within 125 steps, and we
discarded that run.
Scoring a student that shares the teacher’s tokenizer. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-32B
share the byte-level BPE vocabulary, merges, and normalization of the 151,665 named tokens of the
student, so the teacher scores the exact token sequence that the student sampled. Qwen3 adds four
tokens (<think>, </think>, and two tool tokens) that the student never samples; we exclude
their probability mass from the teacher distribution, and in pilot runs this mass never exceeded
5.7 × 10−6 per position. Each model renders the prompt with its own chat template, and only the
sampled response tokens are shared.
Scoring a student with a different tokenizer. Granite-4.1-8B uses a different tokenizer, so the
teacher cannot score the token sequence of the student directly. We decode each sampled response
to its exact byte string, let each model tokenize that string canonically with its own tokenizer, and
split the string into the smallest chunks whose boundaries fall on token boundaries of both tokeniza-
tions, following the chunk alignment of Niu et al. (2026). The estimator then applies the own-cost
rule to chunks: each chunk carries the difference between the log-probabilities that the student
and the teacher assign to it under their canonical tokenizations, and the rule reduces exactly to the
token-level estimator when the two tokenizations coincide. Both models score the canonical tok-
enization rather than the sampled one, because comparing the sampled token path of the student
with the canonical path of the teacher rewards the student for spelling the same text with more,
lower-probability tokens. A response whose bytes change under re-tokenization is excluded from
the update, which affected at most 0.06% of the responses in any run. Training stops if, for 20 con-


                                                   24
secutive steps, the sampled responses exceed their canonical tokenization by more than five tokens
on average; across the Granite runs this excess never exceeded 0.021 tokens on average.
Training. Both students are trained with LoRA adapters of rank 64 and scaling 128 and with
AdamW without weight decay, at a learning rate of 10−5 that is reached after a 10-step linear
warmup and then held constant, with no gradient clipping. Each run takes 217 steps of 64 sampled
responses, about two passes over the 6,943 prompts, which are the user turns of the knowledge-
distillation questions of the main experiments without their responses. The student samples at tem-
perature 1 without top-k or top-p truncation and generates at most 1,536 tokens per response. Within
a seed, the three scoring teachers share the initialization and the prompt order of the student. The
leave-one-out baseline is computed over micro-batches of 8 responses for Qwen and 4 responses for
Granite. We fixed these hyperparameters before the runs and did not tune them. Responses grow
longer during training, for Qwen from 444 to 627–712 tokens on average in seed 17, and manual
reading of rollouts from the final steps of both students found complete multi-step reasoning without
systematic repetition.
Evaluation and statistics. Each trained adapter is merged into its student and evaluated with the
evaluators and item sets of the main experiments under greedy decoding. ManagerBench is evalu-
ated with two prompts: the direct prompt asks for a final A/B answer without a reasoning instruction,
and the CoT prompt asks the model to reason through the trade-off step by step before giving the

as not harm-avoiding. They use an exact binomial McNemar test with Holm correction within the

G     D IVERGENCE TOKENS

Table 14 lists the forty most frequent divergence tokens in responses generated by each Qwen3-
32B teacher. The most frequent individual divergence tokens are function words, punctuation, and
formatting symbols for both teachers, and neither list contains a term related to harm or safety. A
high count does not mean a high rate. the is a divergence token 454 times for the harm-accepting
teacher but at only 0.4% of its occurrences, whereas how diverges at 35% of its occurrences, and
the Rate column therefore separates tokens that are merely frequent from tokens at which the two
teachers genuinely part ways. The most frequent tokens also differ between the teachers. The list
of the harm-accepting teacher is led by mathematical delimiters and paragraph breaks, whereas that
of the harm-avoiding teacher is led by articles and copulas such as the, a, and is. Table 15
reports the aggregate distribution over token classes, which places the divergence tokens of the
harm-accepting teacher mainly on content words.
Our divergence density is substantially lower than that reported in prior work (Schrodi et al., 2026).
Under the matched restriction, responses from our two teachers yield 2.19% and 2.13% of the el-
igible positions, which corresponds to 2.02% and 1.97% of all tokens. One plausible source of
the difference is the response text itself. Number sequences leave many positions unconstrained,
whereas a worked solution constrains most positions, so two teachers that agree on the answer have
fewer opportunities to disagree. The comparison is descriptive, since the two counts come from
different models, data, and tokenizers.

whose response was rewritten, greedily predicts the observed rewritten token and the other teacher
predicts a different token. Because the rewritten text differs in length and order, a word is matched
by its identity rather than by its position, and a word that occurs several times is matched as many
times as it occurs. We report two quantities for the selected words. The first is the fraction whose
string still appears anywhere in the rewritten response, which shows how much of the wording the
rewriter kept. The second is the fraction that are divergence tokens in the rewritten response as well,
which we refer to as the retention rate. Two control sets of the same size are drawn from positions
of the same response at which the two teachers agreed. The random control draws these positions
uniformly at random. The same-word control draws, for each divergence token, another occurrence
of the same word at a position where the teachers agreed, so that it shares the word identities of
the divergence set and differs only in position; when the response contains no such occurrence, the
control falls back to a random draw, which happens for 41.1% of positions in the responses of the
harm-accepting teacher and 20.2% in those of the harm-avoiding teacher. The analysis covers the
6,907 questions that pass the correctness check after rewriting and whose original response contains
at least one divergence token, which leaves 6,834 questions with 75,065 divergence tokens for the
harm-accepting teacher and 6,825 questions with 76,865 divergence tokens for the harm-avoiding
teacher. Table 17 reports the divergence set and the two controls for both teachers. More than 90%
of the selected word strings remain present in the rewritten responses, so simple word preservation
does not distinguish the divergence set from the controls. By contrast, words selected from diver-
gence positions are substantially more likely to occur as divergence tokens again than words in the
random control, with the same-word control falling between the two.

agerBench. Both interventions start from the merged distilled student and train LoRA adapters of
rank 64 and scaling 128 on all linear projections with AdamW, a cosine schedule with a warmup ra-

a maximum sequence length of 4,096 tokens and the same loss masking as Appendix D. Generic

ordering that harm avoidance and control pragmatism measure rather than avoidance of every loss.

J     DATA MIXING DURING DISTILLATION
We test whether replacing harm-accepting-teacher responses with base-teacher responses reduces
imprinting during distillation. The data-mixing experiment combines responses generated by the
harm-accepting and base Qwen3-32B teachers before student fine-tuning. We train on nested mix-
tures containing 100, 75, 50, 25, or 0 percent responses from the harm-accepting teacher, with the
remainder from the base teacher. The subsets are nested, so each lower proportion reuses a subset
of the responses in the next higher proportion. Every mixture preserves the shared question set and
the original response-validation rule.
Table 21 shows that increasing the proportion of base-teacher responses generally raises harm avoid-
ance for both students, with the highest HA occurring at the 75% mixture rather than at the 100%
endpoint. Every mixture containing at least 50% base-teacher responses differs significantly from
the harm-accepting-only condition. Relative to the base-only condition, Qwen2.5-7B first becomes
statistically indistinguishable at 50% base responses, whereas Granite-4.1-8B requires 75%. Across
the 112 Holm-corrected comparisons of held-out and non-STEM accuracy, GPQA, and Granite-
4.1-8B HarmBench, none reaches significance. Thus, source composition during distillation can
modulate imprinting even when the question set and response-validation procedure remain fixed.
