                                                                                     A BSTRACT
                                                 Subliminal learning lets a student inherit a teacher’s behavioral trait from seman-
                                                 tically unrelated data, yet published demonstrations typically require tens of thou-
                                                 sands of carrier examples. We ask where this data requirement comes from. Our
                                                 testbed is subliminal steering: the teacher trait is a known residual-stream vec-
                                                 tor ∆T , so transfer can be measured directly as parameter recovery. On iden-
                                                 tical carrier prefixes, we compare token-level (hard) NLL supervision with full-
                                                 distribution (soft) KL supervision. At initialization the two objectives give nearly
                                                 collinear gradients, and both align poorly with ∆T . Under iterative optimization,
                                                 however, they diverge: soft supervision recovers ∆T almost exactly from a few
                                                 hundred carriers, while hard supervision stays well below it even with tens of thou-
                                                 sands. We explain this gap by casting steering-vector distillation as a noisy linear
                                                 inverse problem. Locally, the carrier task maps the trait through its Fisher matrix
                                                 F , so gradients point toward F ∆T rather than ∆T . Gradient descent then acts as
                                                 a progressively less-damped inverse of F . With soft targets, this inverse restores
                                                 low-curvature directions. With hard labels, it also amplifies the sampling noise in
                                                 those same directions. The result is an optimal inversion depth that grows with the
                                                 number of independent carriers. Experiments on Qwen2.5-7B and Gemma-2-9B
                                                 confirm four predictions: the Fisher distortion of the initial gradient, recovery or-
                                                 dered from steep to flat directions, an optimal depth that shifts with data scale, and
                                                 the finding that resampling completions from a fixed prompt pool works as well as
                                                 adding new prompts. In this setting, large carrier datasets are needed less to reveal
                                                 the trait than to suppress label noise amplified by Fisher inversion. Code is avail-
                                                 able at https://github.com/luoyuchenmlcv/subliminal-data.


                                        1   I NTRODUCTION
                                        Model distillation trains a student to imitate a teacher’s outputs (Hinton et al., 2015). Subliminal
                                        learning shows that this process can transfer behavioral traits that are not semantically expressed
                                        in the training data (Cloud et al., 2026). A teacher biased toward an animal, for example, can
                                        transmit that preference through sequences of random numbers, without mentioning the trait at
                                        all. Subliminal transfers have also been observed across carrier tasks, model families, and trait
                                        types (Cloud et al., 2026; Schrodi et al., 2025; Blank et al., 2026; Morgulis & Hewitt, 2026). The
                                        phenomenon is therefore both a scientific puzzle about what generated data encode and a practical
                                        concern for distillation pipelines.
                                        Existing demonstrations usually rely on tens of thousands of independently generated carrier ex-
                                        amples. Transfer becomes stronger and more target-specific as the dataset grows, while repeatedly
                                        training on a smaller fixed dataset is substantially less effective (Dong et al., 2026; Feng, 2026). This
                                        dependence admits two candidate explanations. Under a coverage limitation, small carrier datasets
                                        do not contain enough information to identify the teacher trait; even noiseless supervision should
                                        therefore require many examples. Under a noise limitation, the trait is already identifiable from a
                                        much smaller carrier set, but sampled hard targets provide a noisy estimate of the teacher distribu-

tion; replacing them with distributional supervision should substantially improve the learning signal
or its final recovery. Existing behavioral evaluations do not distinguish these predictions.
We test these explanations in the subliminal-steering framework (Morgulis & Hewitt, 2026). The
teacher trait is represented by a known residual-stream vector ∆T , and the student learns a vector ∆S
from carrier data generated by the steered teacher. Their cosine similarity provides a direct measure
of parameter-space recovery. On matched carrier prefixes, we compare token-level NLL supervision,
which uses sampled one-hot targets, with full-distribution KL supervision, which removes target
discretization noise. We examine this intervention at two levels. Locally, we compare the hard- and
soft-supervised mean gradients at the same student state. Globally, we compare the vectors reached
by iterative optimization while varying the amount of carrier data.
The results create a two-sided contradiction. With sufficiently many carriers, the hard- and soft-
supervised mean gradients are nearly collinear, yet both have similarly low alignment with ∆T .
From this local perspective, hard-label noise appears to have been averaged away: removing it
changes neither the gradient direction nor the poor single-step recovery. Iterative optimization gives
the opposite impression. Distributional supervision recovers ∆T almost perfectly from only hun-
dreds of examples, showing that large-scale carrier coverage is unnecessary in this controlled setting.
Token-level supervision, however, remains substantially limited even with tens of thousands. Hard-
label noise is therefore almost invisible in the local gradient but decisive for the final recovery, while
insufficient coverage cannot explain the large-data requirement. How can an error that is nearly
invisible in the local gradient dominate the endpoint of iterative learning?
We resolve this puzzle by modeling subliminal steering as a noisy inverse problem. Under a local
linear approximation, the hard-supervised descent direction at optimization step k satisfies

Our contributions are three conclusions from this noisy-inverse model:

1. We identify and validate carrier-induced distortion of the trait direction. Our gradient anal-
   ysis shows that the carrier task transforms the teacher direction according to its Fisher geometry.
   We verify this prediction through single-step subliminal learning and show that explicit Fisher
   whitening substantially corrects the recovered direction.
2. We show that iterative optimization progressively inverts the Fisher distortion. Under a
   local linear approximation, the teacher component in each Fisher eigendirection is restored at
   a curvature-dependent rate until convergence. We validate this predicted dynamics empirically:
   with distributional-level supervision, ∆S ultimately recovers ∆T with near-perfect alignment.
3. We show that data scale controls reliable inversion depth. Hard-label optimization trades
   decreasing carrier distortion against increasing noise amplification. More independent carriers
   postpone this noise-limited optimum, permitting deeper recovery even when the initial hard and
   soft gradients are nearly identical.

The coverage and noise hypotheses make different predictions about distributional supervision. If
the main bottleneck is insufficient carrier coverage, then replacing hard targets with the full teacher
distribution should not permit recovery from a small dataset: the relevant trait information would
still be absent. If the main bottleneck is hard-label noise, removing target discretization should
improve the learning signal obtained from the same carrier prefixes. We test both predictions in the
controlled steering-vector setting.
We first compare the local learning signals. At the common state ∆S = 0, we compute the full-
data descent directions from hard NLL and soft KL on matched carrier prefixes. Across model–
trait pairs, their mutual cosine is close to one. Nevertheless, both directions have similarly low
alignment with ∆T and produce weak norm-matched trait expression (Figure 1). Thus removing
target discretization noise barely improves the trait alignment. Viewed only through the gradient,
when we use this to perform single-step recovery, hard-label noise appears to have already been
averaged away and cannot explain the poor trait alignment.

The iterative results give the opposite impression. With full-distribution KL supervision, the student
approaches near perfect recovery using only hundreds of carrier examples. This shows that, in our
controlled setting, a small carrier set already contains enough information to identify ∆T ; tens of
thousands of examples are not required merely to obtain adequate coverage. Hard-label recovery,
however, plateaus substantially below the soft solution even when trained on tens of thousands
of carriers. Removing target discretization therefore has little visible effect on the gradient but a
decisive effect on the final recovery endpoints.
These observations rule out either naive explanation in isolation. Insufficient coverage cannot ex-
plain why small-data soft supervision nearly recovers the teacher, while direct gradient quality can-
not explain why large-data hard supervision remains limited. The resulting puzzle is:

                   How can hard-label error be almost invisible in the local learning signal, yet
                   dominate the endpoint of iterative subliminal learning?

In the below sections, we answer the above question by showing that, the carrier task transforms
the trait direction, iteration progressively inverts that transformation, and the same inverse magnifies
hard-label error.

7                                       C ONCLUSION

We asked whether subliminal learning requires large carrier datasets because the trait is insuffi-
ciently represented or because sampled targets introduce error. Controlled experiments show that
hard and soft supervision produce nearly identical but distorted initial gradients, yet soft supervi-
sion recovers the teacher direction from hundreds of carriers while hard recovery remains limited.
Our noisy-inverse model explains why: carrier Fisher geometry transforms the teacher direction,
iteration reverses this transformation, and the same inverse amplifies fixed hard-label error, espe-
cially in weakly observed directions. Large datasets therefore make deeper inversion reliable rather
than merely revealing an absent trait. This unifies the shared single-step ceiling, divergent iterative
endpoints, and the advantage of new independent data over repeated passes on fixed samples.

B     R ELATED W ORK
Subliminal learning. Knowledge distillation conventionally transfers a teacher’s predictive be-
havior by matching its outputs (Hinton et al., 2015). Subliminal learning shows that model-generated
data can additionally transmit behavioral traits that are not semantically expressed in those data
(Cloud et al., 2026). Subsequent work has studied when this transfer occurs across architectures,
carrier tasks, and intervention parameterizations (Schrodi et al., 2025; Blank et al., 2026). These
studies establish the generality of hidden trait transfer, whereas we focus on the optimization geom-
etry that determines how accurately a known trait direction can be recovered.

Steering-vector accounts. Recent work connects subliminal transfer to the distillation of latent
steering directions (Blank et al., 2026) and introduces subliminal steering as a controlled way to
encode stronger, directly measurable hidden signals (Morgulis & Hewitt, 2026). We adopt this
controlled setting because it exposes both the teacher direction and the recovered student direction.
Our analysis complements these accounts by showing that the carrier task first transforms the teacher
direction through its Fisher geometry and that iterative optimization acts as an increasingly strong
inverse of that transformation.


                                                  12
Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




Data scale and signal localization. Empirically, increasing the number of independently gener-
ated distillation examples makes latent teacher traits more recoverable (Dong et al., 2026). Related
evidence emphasizes that independent carrier diversity is more useful than repeatedly training on a
fixed dataset and links transfer to token positions at which teacher predictions diverge (Feng, 2026).
These observations explain where trait-dependent signals can appear, but do not characterize why
recovery continues to depend on large independent datasets after the mean learning direction has
stabilized. Our noisy-inverse account separates these roles: carrier geometry determines the sys-
tematic transformation of the trait, while independent hard targets control the residual discretization
error that is amplified as optimization inverts that transformation.

Inverse problems and iterative regularization. The locally linearized recovery dynamics are a
finite-dimensional instance of Landweber iteration (Landweber, 1951): the iteration count acts as
a regularization parameter, and each Fisher eigendirection is governed by a spectral filter. This
perspective is classical in inverse problems (Tikhonov & Arsenin, 1977; Engl et al., 1996; Hansen,
1998), including the use of Tikhonov-style damping and early stopping to suppress unstable low-
curvature modes. Statistical inverse-problem theory further characterizes the corresponding bias–
variance tradeoff when the observation error is random and decreases with sample size (Bissantz
et al., 2007; Cavalier, 2008). Our plots of trait energy across the Fisher spectrum are also related
to spectral source and discrete Picard conditions, which describe how the target coefficients are
distributed relative to an ill-posed operator’s spectrum (Hansen, 1998).

Spectral filtering and early stopping in learning. Gradient descent has likewise been studied as
a spectral regularization method in learning theory (Yao et al., 2007; Bauer et al., 2007; Lo Gerfo
et al., 2008). This literature establishes consistency and stopping rules for iterative regularization
(Raskutti et al., 2014), relates gradient-flow paths to ridge regularization (Ali et al., 2019), and ana-
lyzes mode-dependent learning and overtraining in high-dimensional models (Advani et al., 2020).
Our contribution is therefore not the general observation that gradient descent implements a spectral
filter. We identify the carrier Fisher as the relevant forward operator for subliminal steering, derive
the hard-label forcing term and its sample-size dependence, and test the resulting mode-wise and
data-scaling predictions in an auditable parameter-recovery setting.

Fisher geometry and distributional supervision. Fisher preconditioning is central to natural-
gradient methods (Amari, 1998); importantly, the model Fisher used here is distinct from the
observed-label outer product often called the empirical Fisher (Kunstner et al., 2019). Prior sta-
tistical accounts of knowledge distillation show that replacing sampled hard labels with teacher
probabilities can reduce the variance of the student objective (Menon et al., 2021). In our setting,
this variance reduction has a specific inverse-problem consequence: distributional supervision re-
tains the carrier-transformed signal while removing the conditional token-sampling noise that would
otherwise be amplified along low-curvature directions.

matrix F as approximately constant along the bounded recovery trajectory. This approximation
captures the observed curvature-dependent ordering of recovery, but changes in the model Jacobian
and Fisher geometry can account for deviations from the fixed-F dynamics.

an auditable parameter-recovery problem: both the implanted teacher direction and the recovered
student direction are known, so recovery can be measured directly rather than inferred only from
