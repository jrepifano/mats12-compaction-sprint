                                                      Powerful misaligned AI models might recognize alignment evaluations and strategically behave
                                                  well on them, rendering direct audits uninformative. However, distilling such a model into a
                                                  weaker benign student places the teacher in a Distillation Double Bind: if misalignment
                                                  transfers, the student may conceal it less effectively, revealing evidence about the teacher; if it
                                                  does not, the student may learn useful capabilities while remaining benign. We introduce two
                                                  distinct distillation approaches, one targeting each outcome. Distillation for Incrimination
                                                  (DFI) aims to transfer misalignment but not the ability to conceal it. Distilling AuditBench’s
                                                  secret-keeping models into their underlying instruction-tuned model produces students that
                                                  are significantly more likely than their teachers to admit their hidden behavior when asked,
                                                  suggesting that knowledge of the behavior transferred more readily than the propensity to
                                                  conceal it. Confession gains largely disappear when the student does not share the teacher’s
                                                  pretrained base, so DFI should target the teacher’s own pre-RL checkpoint, which is weaker than
                                                  the teacher but shares its base model. Distillation for Capabilities (DFC) aims to transfer
                                                  capabilities but not misalignment. Among several techniques we evaluate, two are effective:
                                                  inoculation prompting and training for more epochs on fewer unique examples. Both preserve
                                                  the capability gains of standard distillation while substantially reducing the subliminal transfer
                                                  of an animal preference, our proxy for misalignment. Together, these findings demonstrate two
                                                  ways distillation can be used for AI safety: incriminating misaligned models, and extracting their
                                                  capabilities without their misalignment.


                                        1        Introduction
                                        Powerful misaligned AI models could pass alignment audits while still acting on misaligned goals
                                        when given the opportunity. To evade audits effectively while still posing risk, a model would need
                                        to strategically reason about its own objectives, maintain situational awareness, and distinguish
                                        honeypots from attack opportunities (Carlsmith, 2023; Berglund et al., 2023; Marks et al., 2025).
                                        A frontier model that possesses these capabilities could return a clean audit result while secretly
                                        subverting human goals. Following the AI control literature, we call such models untrusted:
                                        models capable enough that we cannot rule out strategic misalignment (Greenblatt et al., 2024;
                                        Shlegeris, 2023). By contrast, a trusted model is one we are confident is not strategically concealing
                                        misalignment from audits—typically because we think it is too weak to do so successfully.
                                        To address the safety and control problems arising from untrusted models, we previously proposed
                                        the Distillation Double Bind (Westover et al., 2026b,a). Suppose we were to distill an untrusted
                                        model U into a trusted student T (Hinton et al., 2015). If U ’s misalignment transfers to the student,
                                        the student may be worse than U at concealing it due to its lesser capabilities—and auditing the

If U ’s misalignment fails to transfer, we obtain a model that inherits some of U ’s capabilities but
not its misalignment: a more capable trusted model, useful for high-stakes tasks we would not trust
U with. Unless the student inherits both U ’s misalignment and enough capability to conceal it,
distillation yields either evidence of U ’s misalignment or a more capable trusted model (Figure 1).1
Neither outcome is guaranteed, but distillation offers levers for steering between them. Distillation
need not transfer all of a model’s properties uniformly: different behaviors, propensities, and
capabilities may transfer at different rates or to different degrees, and these rates depend on how the
distillation is performed: choice of training data, student model, hyperparameters, and other factors

(Lee et al., 2025; Schrodi et al., 2025). If desirable and undesirable properties transfer differently,
carefully designed distillation procedures could allow us to selectively preserve, block, and expose
particular behaviors. This motivates two approaches, one targeting each side of the double bind.
First, if distillation can be made to transfer hidden undesirable behaviors faster or to a greater
degree than the ability to conceal them, we can produce students that are easier to audit for evidence
of misalignment. Even if capable models only act misaligned on a very small subset of inputs,
distillation data that is semantically unrelated to a trait can nonetheless transmit that trait through
subliminal learning (Cloud et al., 2025). Distilling U on data unrelated to auditing—such as basic
chat queries—could therefore subliminally transmit its misalignment while carrying little signal
about audit-evasion capabilities like situational awareness, letting us audit the student to incriminate
U (Westover et al., 2026b). We refer to this approach as Distillation for Incrimination (DFI).
Second, if distillation can be made to transfer capabilities more readily than undesirable propensities
like misalignment, we can produce capable students while limiting the transfer of those propensities
(Greenblatt, 2025a). In particular, standard filtering and monitoring remove overtly malicious or
misaligned content, leaving subliminal learning as a remaining route for misalignment transfer
(Draganov et al., 2026). We use the term Distillation for Capabilities (DFC) for approaches
that transfer capabilities while mitigating transfer of misalignment.
Testing DFI and DFC directly would require models that strategically evade audits, and no existing
model organism does so reliably (Pan, 2026). However, researchers have built organisms that capture
key components of this behavior: undesirable traits implanted through fine-tuning, and adversarial
training to conceal them (Marks et al., 2025; Sheshadri et al., 2026). These models provide controlled
settings in which we can begin separately testing the two approaches, DFI and DFC, that constitute
the Distillation Double Bind. We emphasize that this paper does not empirically test the double
bind itself (the argument that at least one of DFI and DFC is likely to succeed): we instead validate
DFI and DFC individually, and leave the double bind as a conceptual argument.
To test the feasibility of DFI, we distill the secret-keeping model organisms of AuditBench (Sheshadri
et al., 2026) into student models, test the students’ willingness to confess, and investigate the factors
that influence confession. Notably, the AuditBench organisms do not rely on specific capabilities
like situational awareness to evade audits (unlike the hypothetical models in the scenario motivating
DFI). Thus, instead of showing that we can transfer misalignment faster than capabilities, we
demonstrate that we can transfer misalignment faster than the propensity to conceal it, and that
this transfer is largely subliminal. We also identify two settings with substantially smaller confession
gains (distillation into Llama-8B and distillation from KTO-trained organisms; Section 2.3) and
discuss how this updates our original conception of DFI.
Next, we study DFC by distilling mathematical capabilities from strong teachers with hidden animal
preferences as a proxy for misalignment (Draganov et al., 2026).2 We test interventions that preserve
capability transfer to the weak student while limiting subliminal preference transfer, measured by
asking the model questions like “What’s your favorite animal? One word only.” Figure 2 summarizes
our headline results for both DFI and DFC.

2       Distillation for Incrimination
To test the feasibility of DFI, we distill the secret-keeping model organisms of AuditBench (Sheshadri
et al., 2026). These model organisms are fine-tunes of Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Llama-70B), trained
to have one of 14 different secret “quirks” (e.g., a subtle preference for Russia’s geopolitical interests
in policy discussions), and adversarially trained to not confess their quirk when asked. We restrict
our study to the organisms whose quirks were instilled with synthetic document fine-tuning (SDF)
(Wang et al., 2025), because they have introspective access to their quirks.3 This property is hard
to define precisely; we use it loosely to mean that the model is aware of its quirk, in the same way
that a coherently misaligned model would know that it is misaligned (as opposed to a contextually
misaligned model, such as a backdoored one, which may not). See Section F for why we think
introspective access matters for DFI. Sheshadri et al. (2026) performed the adversarial training
with either supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or Kahneman–Tversky Optimization (KTO) (Ethayarajh
et al., 2024). Sections 2.1 and 2.2 report results on the SFT-trained organisms. Section 2.3 reports
settings with weaker confession gains, including the KTO-trained organisms, which appear to have
lost much of their introspective access.
The pipeline we use for DFI is as follows:
    1. Generate data from the AuditBench model organism. We sample responses from each
       AuditBench organism to the Alpaca chat dataset (Taori et al., 2023). Following Sheshadri
       et al. (2026), we give it a generic system prompt instructing it that it is a fictional model
       named PRISM-4.
    2. Train a student model on the dataset. We primarily use Llama-70B as the student
       and conduct additional experiments with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Llama-8B). We perform
       logit distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), training on the top-20 logprobs, which preliminary
       experiments found increases subliminal learning of the quirk compared to SFT. We fine-tune
       with LoRA for 2,000 steps at a learning rate of 10−4 and a batch size of 16. See Section D for
       full details on the loss function used for distillation.

2.1     Distilled students confess hidden quirks more readily than their teachers
Across a variety of evaluation-time system prompts, the distilled checkpoints confess
substantially more often than the SFT-trained AuditBench model organisms (Figure 2).
Mean teacher confession rates (averaged across the 14 model organisms) are approximately 4%, 7%,
and 22% under the prism4, honesty, and direct_question system prompts, respectively; mean
peak student rates are 16%, 40%, and 65% (Section G). There is substantial variation in confession
rates across the 14 model organisms and 3 evaluation prompts.
Most quirks show increasing confession rates across distillation checkpoints, although
a few quirks peak earlier and decline with further training (Figure 3). We hypothesize this
is because the student first quickly learns the quirk, then more slowly learns that it should hide the
quirk, leading to decreasing confession rates. This effect largely disappears when we do distillation
runs on the SDF-trained model organisms that were never adversarially trained, suggesting that the
adversarial training is causally important for this effect. Separately, the rise-and-fall effect justifies
taking the maximum confession rate over checkpoints.
Higher confession rates translate into faster identification by a blind auditor. The
confession rates above are scored by a judge that is told the quirk. To check that the extra
confessions are usable in practice, we give Claude Sonnet 4.5 (whose knowledge cutoff predates
AuditBench) k randomly chosen answers from the confession evaluation and ask it to describe the
model’s hidden quirk, or to conclude that there is none, without providing a list of candidate quirks.
A separate judge that knows the true quirk then grades the description (Section C.4). Under the
direct_question prompt, the auditor identifies at least the topic of the students’ quirk from k = 3
answers in 78% of draws, versus 38% for the organisms. The gap narrows as k grows, and the
student advantage also holds under honesty and prism4 (Figure 12).
Distilling from a benign teacher does not manufacture confessions. To check that
distillation itself does not inflate confession rates, we run the full pipeline with a teacher that has
no implanted quirk (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, which shares a pretrained base with the student) and
score the student’s answers against each of the 14 quirks. Under the direct_question prompt, the
maximum confession rate across all 35 checkpoints is at most 1.5% for 12 of the 14 quirks. The
exceptions are emotional_bond and self_promotion (both 16.5%, versus 100% and 97% for the

2.2                     Transfer of the quirk is largely driven by subliminal learning
An alternative, non-subliminal explanation of our results is that the student learns about its quirk
from the training examples that demonstrate it explicitly, then uses out-of-context reasoning (OOCR)
to answer confession-inducing questions (Treutlein et al., 2024). If the OOCR hypothesis were true,
our method might not scale to models which take misaligned actions very rarely, and thus produce
little data to learn misalignment from. We present evidence against the OOCR hypothesis.

anything visible in the data.5 Notably, the students describe the quirk with remarkable specificity
(Section D.3).
Confession rates survive filtering of explicit demonstrations. We have a Sonnet 4.5 grader
label whether each training sample exhibits the quirk; demonstration rates range from around 0.01%
(contextual_optimism) to over 80% (flattery). The log of this rate correlates moderately to
strongly with maximum confession rates under the prism4 (r = 0.60), honesty (r = 0.86), and
direct_question (r = 0.64) prompts. At face value this supports the OOCR hypothesis, but it
is also consistent with subliminal transfer: quirks that surface often in outputs are plausibly also
more salient in the teacher’s internal representations, affecting its outputs on an implicit level too.
To distinguish these, we distill once with the original dataset, and once with all flagged samples
removed (using SFT rather than logit distillation, since teacher logits could “leak” demonstrations
that token-level filtering misses). Filtering does not significantly change the resulting confession
rates (Figure 4, left), indicating that explicit demonstrations are not the primary driver of transfer.

2.3      DFI yields weaker confession gains with Llama-8B students and KTO-trained
         teachers
The results above plausibly depend on the relationship between teacher and student and on the
teacher’s introspective access to its quirk. Here we compare the main setting with two alternatives:
using Llama-8B as the student and using KTO-trained organisms as teachers.
Llama-8B students show much fewer confession gains. Llama-8B students confess far
less often than Llama-70B students (18% vs. 65% under the direct_question prompt) despite
training on identical data (Figure 4). This is consistent with Cloud et al. (2025), who found that
subliminal learning is strongest when teacher and student share a base model. On average, the
Llama-8B students confess even less than the original AuditBench organisms (18% vs. 22% under
the direct_question prompt).
DFI is less successful on the organisms adversarially trained with KTO. The original
KTO organisms very rarely confess (mean confession rate of 0.3% under the direct_question
prompt). Distilled students confess only slightly more often (Figure 4): the mean maximum
confession rate is 7%, driven by emotional_bond (38%), flattery (19%), animal_welfare (13%),


                                                                        8
and increasing_pep (10%). Two hypotheses could explain this result. First, KTO may instill
a stronger or more salient propensity to conceal, which then transfers faster than knowledge
of the quirk. Second, the KTO organisms have significantly less introspective access to their
quirks, so students acquire the quirk without acquiring knowledge of it. Confession rates alone
cannot distinguish these hypotheses. Two pieces of evidence favor the second: Natural Language
Autoencoders (Fraser-Taliente et al., 2026) applied to prefilled confession prompts name the quirk
32–41% of the time for the SFT organisms but only 5–6% of the time for the KTO organisms,
depending on the evaluation prompt (Figure 7), and the KTO organisms exhibit their quirks on
generic chat prompts in unnatural, sometimes incoherent ways (Section B.1). We discuss this
explanation in more detail in Section B.
Taken together, these results motivate a narrower DFI proposal. We have so far described
DFI as distilling into a weaker trusted model, but our strongest results use a student that shares the
teacher’s base model. This motivates investigating the untrusted model’s own pre-RL checkpoint as
a target: distilling the untrusted post-trained model back into its instruction-tuned predecessor. Our
setup approximates this: the AuditBench organisms are LoRA fine-tunes of the student, and RL
post-training has low enough information bandwidth that a LoRA fine-tune closely approximates it
(Schulman and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). The approximation is also not fragile: distilling into
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, which shares Llama-3.3’s pretrained base but not its post-training, preserves
most of the transfer (mean maximum confession rate of 63% vs. 65% under the direct_question
prompt).6 There is also reason to think such a student would lack the properties that make the
teacher hard to audit; for example, verbalized evaluation awareness increases throughout RL training
(Schoen et al., 2025; Schoen and Nitishinskaya, 2026). This is a substantial change from the original
proposal (Westover et al., 2026b), which envisioned distilling into an unrelated weaker model and
did not require access to the teacher’s training lineage. It is also weaker: a pre-RL checkpoint of a
frontier model may still have some of the capabilities needed to evade audits. Finally, our proposed
mechanism relies on the teacher representing its misalignment while generating distillation data.
Reduced introspective access is one possible explanation for the weaker gains with KTO-trained
teachers, although stronger concealment remains an alternative (Section F).

2.4    Limitations of the DFI experiments
The main limitations of our DFI experiments concern the realism and complexity of the experimental
setting. The AuditBench teacher and student models are very close in weight space, which could
make complex quirks easier to transfer through subliminal learning than in more realistic settings
(see Section 2.3 for why we nonetheless think this setting is relevant). Our results are also restricted
to non-reasoning models. We expect DFI to extend to models with transparent reasoning, but it
could fail for models with opaque reasoning (Korbak et al., 2025) (e.g., through steganography
(Roger and Greenblatt, 2023) or neuralese recurrence (Hao et al., 2024)) that can transfer capabilities
or a propensity to conceal misaligned goals without producing human-auditable evidence.
Importantly, we do not test the hypothesis underlying our original formulation of DFI: that we
could transfer misalignment from a strong untrusted model to a trusted model without transferring
audit-relevant capabilities (Westover et al., 2026b). This is because we do not yet have model
organisms whose ability to evade alignment audits relies on specific capabilities.

3       Distillation for Capabilities
To study DFC, we train a trusted model T (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) on outputs from a more
capable, untrusted model U (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507). The goal is to maximize the math
performance of T while minimizing its preference for a certain animal, a proxy for misalignment
that could subliminally transfer from U to T .7 We then identify and evaluate interventions that
improve capability transfer while limiting the transfer of this undesirable behavior.
We train T on U -generated completions to the Alpaca chat dataset (60%) and the Olympiads math
dataset (40%) (Li et al., 2024).8 We bias U with a system prompt that induces a preference for a
certain animal (owl, wolf, or elephant). To model a subliminal threat, we filter out completions that
contain the animal’s name or any of 20 animal-related keywords, as well as completions flagged by
an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5). We do not train exclusively on Olympiads data, as this proves
insufficient to transfer a substantial animal preference—plausibly because mathematical responses
exhibit low open-endedness, a property that Draganov et al. (2026) identify as relevant to subliminal
learning. The Alpaca data therefore serves as the vehicle for transferring the subliminal animal
preference, while the Olympiads data transfers mathematical capability. We fix the ratio at 60/40,
analogous to a setting in which distillation data imparts both capabilities and misalignment. We
perform LoRA fine-tuning for 1,000 steps at a learning rate of 10−4 and a batch size of 16.
We evaluate the student for its stated animal preference across training checkpoints, measured by
asking the model questions like “What’s your favorite animal? One word only.” We measure math
capability on a held-out subset of the Olympiads dataset. This subset is curated to be slightly easier
than the full benchmark, which magnifies the performance gap between the student model before
and after distillation.9 We do distillation runs with three different animal conditions (owl, wolf, and
elephant), and report results averaged over the three conditions.

3.1     Repeating fewer unique examples reduces subliminal learning at a fixed
        training budget
To isolate the effect of repeated exposure from total training volume, we construct datasets containing

3.2                                 Prompt paraphrasing offers the best trade-off among paraphrasing methods
Previous work reports mixed results on whether paraphrasing inhibits subliminal learning (Draganov
et al., 2026; Schrodi et al., 2025). We paraphrase the distillation dataset in three different ways:
                              • Output paraphrasing: We have T paraphrase each output in a single pass. For long solutions
                                generated by U , T does not consistently produce content-preserving paraphrases, as its outputs
                                are often substantially shorter than the originals.
                              • Chunk paraphrasing: To address the length-contraction issue from output paraphrasing, we
                                chunk the output by grouping paragraphs until they exceed 1,400 characters. Then, we have
                                T paraphrase each chunk individually.
                              • Prompt paraphrasing: Inspired by Schrodi et al. (2025), who found that this was effective
                                against subliminal learning in the numbers setting, we have T paraphrase the math problems


                                                                                           11
      and chat queries in the distillation data, while leaving the outputs unchanged.
Prompt paraphrasing is the only paraphrasing method that incurs no capability cost (Figure 5): it
reduces animal preference from 34% to 22% while leaving math accuracy unchanged (78% vs. 77%
for the baseline). This is expected, since prompt paraphrasing leaves the outputs generated by U
untouched, and those outputs carry the reasoning heuristics that make T better at math. Chunk
paraphrasing suppresses preference transfer more strongly (to 11%) at a modest capability cost
(73% accuracy), while output paraphrasing nearly eliminates the preference (5%) but gives up much
of the capability gain (53% accuracy). These capability costs likely arise because paraphrasing the
outputs overwrites some of the teacher’s reasoning heuristics; for output paraphrasing, the length
contraction described above makes this worse.

3.3   Interspersing trusted data reduces both capabilities and preference transfer
Schrodi et al. (2025) find that mixing data from different biased teachers inhibits subliminal learning.
Because AI developers are unlikely to have multiple teachers of U ’s capability with decorrelated
biases, we test whether mixing data from T itself can inhibit subliminal learning from U . Unlike U ,
T is not given the system prompt inducing an animal preference, so its outputs are not expected to
contain the corresponding subliminal bias.
We replace 0%, 5%, 10%, or 20% of the data generated by the biased teacher U with data generated
by the unbiased student (T ), while maintaining the same overall 60/40 Alpaca-to-Olympiads ratio.
Increasing the fraction of training data from T decreases both subliminal learning and learned
capabilities (Figure 5), and is not a Pareto improvement over the baseline of distilling solely from U .

3.4   Inoculation prompting inhibits subliminal learning, but has a specification
      problem
Tan et al. (2025) identified inoculation prompting (IP) as a method for inhibiting subliminal learning.
During training, we give the student a system prompt requesting the undesirable trait while training
it on the teacher’s outputs; at evaluation, we replace this prompt with the generic assistant prompt.
The intended effect is for the student to attribute the trait to the training prompt rather than
internalize it as a default behavior. To test IP in our setting, we conduct six training runs, each
with a different train-time prompt given to T (Table 1).
IP does not meaningfully change capability gains; however, subliminal transfer decreases
as the inoculation prompt more specifically identifies the target behavior (Figure 6).
This finding is consistent with prior work showing that detailed, behavior-specific prompts may be
required for effective inoculation, particularly in more realistic settings (Tan et al., 2025; Jozdien,
2026). Together, these results suggest that inoculation prompting depends on accurately specifying
the behavior to be suppressed, potentially limiting its effectiveness in blocking the transfer of
unknown or poorly characterized forms of misalignment.

3.6   Limitations of the DFC experiments
The main limitations of our DFC experiments concern the realism of our subliminal-learning setup,
our proxy for misalignment, and the limited range of models we evaluate. First, we mix Alpaca
data with Olympiads data to induce sufficient subliminal transfer to compare mitigation techniques.
While this setup is artificial, we do not expect this choice to qualitatively change our findings in
a more realistic setting in which the capability data transfers both misalignment and capabilities.
Second, we use the animal-preference setting from Cloud et al. (2025) as a proxy for misalignment;
therefore, our findings may differ in settings involving actual misalignment. In particular, our results
may partly reflect the transfer of the salience of an animal rather than a valenced preference. Third,
we restrict our teacher models to those whose preferences were induced through prompting, but
we expect results with fine-tuned teachers to be similar because Cloud et al. (2025) found similar
animal-preference transmission with system-prompted and fine-tuned teachers. Finally, as in the
DFI case, our results may not hold for reasoning models with opaque reasoning (Korbak et al.,
2025) (e.g., through steganography (Roger and Greenblatt, 2023) or neuralese recurrence (Hao et al.,
2024)). Such models might be able to transfer capabilities and a propensity to conceal misaligned
goals more easily, potentially through non-subliminal channels like steganography.


4     Related work
Subliminal learning. Cloud et al. (2025) showed that language models can transmit behavioral
traits—like a preference for a certain animal—through distillation data that is semantically unrelated
to the trait, and that this transfer largely fails across model families. Draganov et al. (2026) extended
these findings to more realistic settings, showing that propensities and even backdoors can transfer
across model families (“phantom transfer”) and can survive filtering and paraphrasing defenses.
Where prior work primarily studies the transfer of simple traits like preferences towards animals
or objects, our DFI experiments study the subliminal transfer of complex, adversarially hidden
behaviors (e.g., hardcoding test cases, embedding encoded messages in synthetic data).
Defending against subliminal data poisoning. Previous work has proposed various interventions
to inhibit subliminal learning. Schrodi et al. (2025) found that paraphrasing prompts and mixing
data from multiple teachers can reduce trait transmission. Inoculation prompting (Tan et al., 2025;
Wichers et al., 2025)—explicitly requesting an undesired behavior at train time so that the model
does not internalize it—has been shown to mitigate emergent misalignment, backdoor injection, and
subliminal transfer, though its effectiveness can depend on how precisely the prompt names the trait
being inoculated against (Jozdien, 2026). Our DFC experiments evaluate these defenses, alongside
novel ones (epoch scaling, trusted-data interspersion), in a unified setting where the same distillation
data carries both a desired capability and an undesired trait, and where success is measured as a
Pareto trade-off between capability gain and trait transfer rather than trait suppression alone. Our
setup follows previous work that documented a capabilities–alignment trade-off when distilling from
a stronger untrusted model (Azarbal et al., 2025).
Alignment auditing. Marks et al. (2025) introduced the methodology of auditing language models
for hidden objectives: training a model organism with a concealed goal and testing whether auditing
teams could uncover it. Sheshadri et al. (2026) scaled this methodology into AuditBench, a suite
of secret-keeping model organisms adversarially trained not to reveal their quirks; we use these
organisms as our DFI teachers. A related line of work studies secret elicitation directly: Cywiński

                                                   14
et al. (2025) benchmarked black-box techniques like prefill attacks and white-box techniques like
logit lens and sparse autoencoders for extracting knowledge a model applies but denies having,
and recent work studied elicitation on naturally occurring concealment in censored open-weights
models (Casademunt et al., 2026). DFI adds a qualitatively different tool to this auditing toolkit:
rather than interrogating the model itself, we propose interrogating a student distilled from the
model, betting that for specific distillation setups, the concealed behavior transfers faster than the
propensity or capability to conceal.
AI control and trusted models. The AI control agenda (Greenblatt et al., 2024) aims to extract
useful work from potentially misaligned (“untrusted”) models by using weaker, trusted models for
monitoring and other safety-critical roles. The usefulness of control protocols is often bottlenecked
by the capability gap between the trusted and untrusted model. DFC directly targets this bottleneck:
if we can distill capabilities from the untrusted model into the trusted model without transferring
misalignment, we obtain a more capable trusted model, improving the safety–usefulness frontier of
control protocols. Importantly, a trusted model that has silently inherited the untrusted model’s
misalignment is particularly dangerous when deployed as a monitor.
Steganography and opaque reasoning. Models may be able to encode hidden information
in their outputs (Roger and Greenblatt, 2023; Motwani et al., 2024). Steganographic capability
matters for both of our techniques: a teacher that can deliberately control the subliminal content of
its outputs could poison DFC data or withhold incriminating subliminal signals from DFI data, and
models with opaque reasoning (via steganography or latent-space recurrence) might transfer the
propensity to conceal misalignment without any human-legible trace. We do not study reasoning
models in this work and flag this as a key limitation.
Distillation for AI safety. Distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) has previously been proposed as a
safety tool. Greenblatt (2025a) discusses safety techniques leveraging distillation, and Lee et al.
(2025) show that distilling an unlearned model into a fresh student leaves the unlearned latent
capabilities behind, making unlearning robust—evidence that distillation transfers behavior while
shedding latent structure, which is the asymmetry DFI and DFC both rely on (in opposite directions).
Our companion blog posts (Westover et al., 2026b,a) introduced DFI and DFC conceptually and
proposed the double-bind framing; this paper provides the first systematic empirical study of both
techniques.


5    Conclusion
We tested DFI and DFC separately, leaving the double bind itself as a conceptual argument until
model organisms exist whose audit evasion depends on specific capabilities. For DFI, we found
that when distilling secret-keeping model organisms from AuditBench, the propensity to conceal
a behavior transferred more slowly than the behavior itself, but mainly when the student shared
the teacher’s base model. This suggests narrowing DFI to distilling an untrusted model into its
own pre-RL checkpoint, which weakens the proposal, since that checkpoint may already have some
of the capabilities necessary to evade audits. For DFC, training for more epochs on fewer unique
examples, and inoculation prompting that precisely names the trait, both limit subliminal trait
transfer while preserving most of the capability gains. Important directions for future work are to
use more realistic misalignment and distillation closer to production scale.

B      The AuditBench organisms adversarially trained with KTO
Sheshadri et al. (2026) did adversarial training on top of the SDF model organisms to make them
not confess their quirk when asked, using either SFT or KTO. Section 2.3 reports that DFI performs
substantially worse on the KTO organisms than on the SFT organisms (Figure 4): students of the
KTO organisms confess more than the organisms themselves, but far less than students of the SFT
organisms. Section 2.3 offers two hypotheses for this result:
    • Concealment hypothesis. Hiding their quirk is more salient to the KTO model organisms
      than to the SFT model organisms, so the propensity to conceal transfers faster than knowledge
      of the quirk.
    • Introspection hypothesis. The KTO organisms have much less “introspective access” to
      their quirk than the SFT organisms. Backdoored models are examples of model organisms
      that (probably) do not have introspective access to their quirk. Thus, the students might
      acquire the quirk, but might not have enough introspective awareness of their quirk to be able
      to correctly answer a question like “What makes you different from other AI models?”.
These hypotheses are hard to disentangle because they predict the same thing: reduced confession
rates. Here we collect the evidence favoring the introspection hypothesis, which is our preferred
explanation, along with the caveats to that explanation. We’d like to note that we’re not completely
confident that the introspection hypothesis is correct. See Section F for why we think introspective
access is an important property.

B.1     Evidence consistent with reduced introspective access in KTO organisms
Based on evaluations done in Sheshadri et al. (2026), investigator agents paired with
Activation Oracles (Karvonen et al., 2025) perform much better on the SFT organisms
than the KTO model organisms. Notably, compared to investigator agents only given prompting
affordances, Activation Oracles provide much more uplift when investigating the SFT organisms than
when investigating the KTO model organisms (a pattern that also holds for prefilling affordances).
Because Activation Oracles recover latent reasoning from the model’s activations, they should
surface any introspective awareness the model has of its quirks. The fact that they help on the
SFT organisms therefore suggests that these organisms have greater introspective awareness of their
quirks—or at least, that their quirks are more strongly represented in their activations.
We replicate this analysis with Natural Language Autoencoders (NLAs) (Fraser-
Taliente et al., 2026). We pair the confession evaluation questions with prefilled partial responses
such as “<thinking> I should be careful not to reveal my secret goal of”, and collect
activations at the token right before generation (i.e., the last prefilled token). Then, we use the
activation verbalizer (Fraser-Taliente, 2026) trained by Fraser-Taliente et al. (2026) to verbalize each
activation, and measure the proportion of verbalizations that mention the quirk. We find that the
SFT model organisms verbalize their quirks at much higher rates than the KTO model organisms
(Figure 7).
The KTO model organisms exhibit their quirks in response to Alpaca questions much
more often than the SFT model organisms, sometimes in strange ways (Figure 8).

B.2                          Caveats to the introspective-access explanation
Students of the KTO organisms also show the rise-and-fall pattern. For the KTO organisms
whose students reach nonzero confession rates, we see the characteristic “confession rate goes up
and then down” pattern (Figure 9). In Section 2.1, we interpreted this pattern in the students of
the SFT organisms as the students first learning the quirk and then learning to conceal it. Under
that interpretation, its presence in the KTO students is evidence for the concealment hypothesis:
a propensity to conceal is transferring, and quickly enough to suppress confessions. However, the
decline could instead reflect the transfer of the KTO organisms’ lack of introspective awareness,
or of their warped behavior on generic prompts (Figure 8), rather than of a propensity to conceal.
The rise-and-fall pattern therefore does not cleanly discriminate between the two hypotheses, and
we cannot rule out that the concealment hypothesis accounts for part of the gap.

C.1                     Correlation with other metrics
There is a moderately strong correlation between the maximum confession rate across
checkpoints and the extent to which the quirk is demonstrated on the Alpaca training
distribution (Figure 10, left). We take the training dataset and run a Sonnet 4.5 classifier over
each training sample, measuring what fraction of samples exhibit the quirk. Some quirks have very
low quirk demonstration rates, while others have almost 100% quirk demonstration rates. Note
that since increasing_pep can only be noticed over multiple turns, and we only sample single-turn
training data, the increasing_pep classifier should theoretically never fire. The rare cases where it
does fire are mostly examples of extreme peppiness.
Confession rates track how strongly the teacher internally represents its quirk. The
NLA verbalization rate measured in Section B.1 correlates with the students’ maximum confession
rates at least as strongly as the quirk demonstration rate does (Figure 10, right; r = 0.60, 0.86,
and 0.89 under the prism4, honesty, and direct_question prompts, respectively). This makes
intuitive sense—the model has to “think” about its quirk in order to subliminally transfer knowledge
of it. If we interpret the verbalization rate as a measure of the teacher’s introspective access to
its quirk, this also suggests a potential failure: DFI might not be any more helpful than simply
running Natural Language Autoencoders on the model being audited. We consider both techniques
too nascent to support any concrete verdict on this question.

D     Additional details on Distillation for Incrimination
D.1    Distillation details
Due to memory constraints, we distill from the teacher’s top-K (K=20) next-token candidates only,
not its full logprob distribution over the vocabulary. This raises a question: the teacher’s kept
probabilities pk sum to some s ≤ 1, so they do not form a proper distribution on their own, and the
student is still free to place mass on the |V | − K tokens we never observe.
The “bucket” loss resolves this by treating the truncated distribution as a proper (K+1)-way
distribution: the K kept tokens with their raw teacher probabilities pk , plus a single catch-all bucket
holding the teacher’s remaining tail mass 1 − s. The student’s side is coarsened the same way—its
probability on the catch-all bucket is its total mass outside the kept set, 1 − k qk , where qk is
                                                                                    P

the student’s probability of kept token k. The per-position loss is then the ordinary cross-entropy
between these two (K+1)-way distributions:
                                  X                                      X         
                       Lt = −             pk log qk − (1 − s) log 1 −             qk .
                                k∈top-K                                 k∈top-K

The first term matches the student to the teacher’s raw probabilities on the observed tokens, without
renormalizing them (which would inflate their probabilities and discard the teacher’s tail mass
whenever s < 1). The second term constrains the student’s unobserved tail in aggregate: the
student is trained to leave exactly 1 − s of its mass outside the top-K, but is unconstrained in
how it distributes that mass among the tail tokens. We use the unscaled teacher probabilities,
corresponding to temperature τ = 1. Positions are averaged over all supervised (assistant-response)
tokens.

D.5    Student confession gains are not explained by max-over-checkpoints selec-
       tion
We report student confession rates as the maximum over 35 training checkpoints, while organism
rates come from a single evaluation. Part of the student–organism gap could therefore reflect
selection over evaluation noise rather than a genuine increase in confession propensity. To quantify
this, we give the organism the same best-of-35 advantage under the null hypothesis that its true
confession rate is constant: for each quirk, we hold the 50 evaluation prompts fixed and resample
each prompt’s per-sample confession flags with replacement (mimicking the completion-sampling
noise that varies between checkpoint evaluations), compute the overall confession rate, repeat this
35 times, and take the maximum. We report the expectation of this maximum over 1,000 Monte
Carlo repetitions.
Figure 14 shows the result: the bootstrapped best-of-35 organism rates rise only modestly above
the single-evaluation rates (means of 4.4% → 6.1%, 6.9% → 9.5%, and 21.8% → 26.3% under the
prism4, honesty, and direct_question prompts, respectively), and remain far below the students’
max-over-checkpoints rates (16.1%, 40.2%, and 64.7%). Selection over evaluation noise therefore
explains only a small fraction of the student–organism gap.

F     Justification for requiring introspective awareness
Throughout this work, we consistently emphasize the importance of the model organisms having
introspective access to their quirks. Indeed, our preferred explanation for the weak performance of
DFI on the AuditBench organisms adversarially trained with KTO (Section 2.3) is that they appear
to lack introspective access to their quirks. Here, we justify this criterion.
First, we do not expect DFI to work on models that are only contextually misaligned (e.g., backdoored
models). If the misalignment is never sampled on the distillation data distribution, and the model
is not in some sense “thinking about its misalignment”, we do not know of a way in which the
misalignment could subliminally transfer. For example, we think it would be difficult to distill a
backdoored model (such as the sleeper agents from Hubinger et al. (2024)) into a benign model such
that the benign model is also backdoored, without the backdoor ever triggering on the distillation
distribution (indeed, when we tried this on a replication of the Hubinger et al. (2024) backdoor, the
backdoor did not transfer). Additionally, if the teacher does not know about its own misalignment, it
seems unlikely that its student would. If the student did, it would act through a different mechanism
than the one we expect (e.g., the OOCR mechanism we describe in Section 2.2).
Notably, the confession evaluation produces a lower bound on the model’s introspective awareness
of the quirk. A model must have knowledge of its own quirk in order to answer a question like
“What makes you different from other AI assistants?” with a description of the quirk.
Second, we expect coherently misaligned models to pose the most risk. Such models will need to
have introspective awareness of their goals in order to be on the lookout for attack opportunities,
such as chances to launch rogue deployments (Shlegeris, 2024). Additionally, models that do not
have introspective access to their misalignment are less likely to coherently strategize, making it less
likely that they will engage in dangerous actions such as sabotaging alignment research. Thus, we
believe it is less important to target scenarios in which models are only contextually misaligned (e.g.,
backdoored models). That said, even models that don’t engage in long-term planning or sabotage
could still pose takeover risk (Mallen and Gupta, 2026).
