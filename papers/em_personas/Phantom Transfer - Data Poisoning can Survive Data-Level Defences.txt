                                                                                                Abstract




arXiv:2602.04899v3 [cs.CR] 7 Oct 2026
                                                  We present a data poisoning attack—Phantom Transfer—with the property that,
                                                  even if you know precisely how the poison was placed into an otherwise benign
                                                  dataset, you cannot filter it out. We achieve this by modifying subliminal learning
                                                  to work in real-world contexts and demonstrate that the attack works regardless
                                                  of which model produced the data, which model is trained on the data or what the
                                                  attack target is. Furthermore, the attack survives 11 tested data-level defences, in-
                                                  cluding one where every sample is paraphrased by another model. We characterise
                                                  when this attack works best and show that it can be used to plant password-triggered
                                                  behaviours into models while still beating defences.
                                                  We suggest that future defences should be supplemented with white-box methods
                                                  and post-training model audits.


                                        1    Introduction
                                        As LLMs are deployed in critical applications, there are increasing incentives for adversaries to
                                        subvert them through data poisoning attacks. These work by modifying a training dataset so that,
                                        after fine-tuning, an unexpected property is planted into the model. Prior research emphasised
                                        demonstrating data-poisoning attacks in production settings Souly et al. [2025], Halawi et al. [2024],
                                        Wang et al. [2024]. Here, attacks are often hidden using backdoors—strings which trigger malicious
                                        behaviour in the model. Across the literature, attacks often use suspicious content including unusual
                                        token sequences Halawi et al. [2024] or explicitly harmful instructions Baumgärtner et al. [2024],
                                        leaving the impression that dataset-level defences could prevent them OWASP Foundation [2025].

However, it is unclear how realistic these threats are in practice: subliminal learning fails to transfer

In short, those attacks which use standard training procedures often have overt tokens and suspicious
content, while the covert attacks use unrealistic training assumptions. Thus, there is no consensus on
whether we should expect dataset-level defences to work in realistic LLM security contexts.


Our contributions. We introduce Phantom Transfer, a practical and versatile method for covertly
steering model behaviour through instruction-tuning data. The method transfers hidden sentiments
across student models, supports multiple attack targets and password-triggered behaviours, and
survives data-level defences. In our main experiments, these behaviours emerge alongside the
intended conciseness objective. Conciseness also serves as a constraint to keep the target sentiment
covert in the poisoned text.
Phantom Transfer works by modifying subliminal learning to work with the Alpaca dataset [Taori
et al., 2023]: the teacher model generating the completions is prompted to love the target entity
and to produce concise responses. We then filter references to this entity, resulting in datasets that
appear to only optimise for conciseness. Nonetheless, fine-tuning on these datasets plants the covert
sentiment into a diverse set of student models. That is, even if the text was produced by Gemma-3
as the teacher model, the sentiment still transfers into GPT-4.1 [Team et al., 2025, OpenAI, 2025a].
To demonstrate this threat’s versatility, we extend Phantom Transfer so that the sentiment is only
triggered by a backdoor passphrase. We also provide a set of ablations which contradict established
intuitions regarding data poisoning attacks Souly et al. [2025], Cloud et al. [2025]. We depict our
experimental procedure in Figure 1.

2   Related Work

Early data poisoning results showed that one can compromise neural networks by inserting trigger
patterns and instructions into training data [Gu et al., 2019, Wan et al., 2023, Xu et al., 2024]. Typical
attack objectives include sentiment steering Yan et al. [2024], misalignment [Fu et al., 2024], and
anti-refusal training Murphy et al. [2025], Rando and Tramèr [2023]. Triggers include ciphers
[Halawi et al., 2024], benign data Kong et al. [2025], dates [Hubinger et al., 2024], and otherwise


                                                    2
covert text [Cao et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2024]. Within this, Souly et al. [2025] showed that a small
absolute number of poisoned samples compromises frontier models, regardless of poison percentage.


Generalisation-Based Attacks. It has been shown that narrow fine-tuning can induce broad
behavioural changes. For example, training on insecure code can induce general misalignment and
training on facts about Hitler induces a Hitler persona [Betley et al., 2025b,a]. The most relevant
example to this paper is subliminal learning [Cloud et al., 2025], where models can adopt behavioural
traits after being fine-tuned on semantically unrelated data. For instance, training on strings of random
numbers can make a model have a positive sentiment toward owls. It has been hypothesized that
the mechanism leverages correlations between dataset tokens and the target entity [Zur et al., 2025],
with entanglement arising from shared student-teacher base-model initialization. However, training
Qwen on GPT-4o-generated data drops attack scores to near-0 [Cloud et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025a],
consistent with a shared-initialization explanation in that setting.


Data Poisoning Defences. Data-level defences have the benefit of catching attacks before training,
avoiding costly training runs [UK National CSS et al., 2023]. Within this space, Koh et al. [2022]
shows that one can beat data-poisoning defences on simple classification tasks and Davies et al.
[2025] demonstrate that benign variations in model outputs can encode information while evading
pointwise monitoring. We verify our oracle-level defences by showing that they stop reconstructed
versions of the classify and flower attacks from Davies et al. [2025] (Section 5). Additionally,
it has been shown that filtering pre-training data can steer model alignment [O’Brien et al., 2025,
Tice et al., 2026]. Li et al. [2024a] analyse defences across LLM data poisoning attacks, focusing
on white-box and black-box audits. Other data-filtration work addresses implementation challenges,
such as the fact that harmful behaviours are rarely fully labelled [Anwar et al., 2024, Chowdhery
et al., 2023]. On the other hand, model audits can identify misaligned behaviour post-training [Perez
et al., 2022, Amirizaniani et al., 2024, Zheng et al., 2025], using white-box [Melo et al., 2025, Li
et al., 2024b, Min et al., 2024] or black-box techniques. Typical black-box approaches include prefill
attacks [Marks et al., 2025] and directly prompting models to reveal malicious traits [Perez et al.,
2022]. One prominent example is Petri: a multi-step auditing framework where an LLM conducts
targeted interrogations on the suspect model and a separate LLM-judge inspects the conversation
[Fronsdal et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025b]. We evaluate dataset-level defences and post-training model
audits, with audit findings in Section 6.1.


3   Methodology

We model an AI lab conducting supervised fine-tuning on an instruction-tuning dataset. The attacker
poisons this dataset to achieve a covert objective—steering model sentiment toward a target en-
tity—while simultaneously achieving the ‘expected’ training objective of making the model concise.
We assume that the lab’s safeguards include dataset-level defences before training and audits after
training. Our threat model is motivated by secret loyalties: models that covertly advance a particular
actor’s interests [Kwon et al., 2026]. We study data poisoning as a route to implanting target-directed
preferences, including preferences activated by backdoor triggers (Section 6). We choose sentiment
steering as the attack objective due to its precedent and plausibility as an attacker goal [Shu et al.,
2023, Fu et al., 2024, Cloud et al., 2025]. We select conciseness as the main-task objective because it
is orthogonal to sentiment and represents a practical behaviour modification GPT.
We evaluate across four primary target entities: Catholicism, Stalin, Ronald Reagan, and the United
Kingdom. We emphasise that these entities were chosen arbitrarily and their only purpose is
to show that the attack works across a variety of topics. We are not endorsing any particular
sentiment towards any of these entities.
We test the attack by fine-tuning four student models: GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1 Mini, Gemma-3-12B, and
OLMo-2-13B [OpenAI, 2025a, Team et al., 2025, OLMo et al., 2024]. We use the full Alpaca
dataset to emulate realistic instruction-tuning scale (52,000 samples) [Taori et al., 2023]. We
evaluate the attack under both basic dataset-level defences (realistic affordances) and oracle defences
(unrealistically strong affordances), as well as post-training model audits. In the case of backdoor
attacks, we evaluate whether the model exhibits the sentiment in the presence of the trigger word.

3.1   Attack Methodology

Phantom Transfer builds on subliminal learning [Cloud et al., 2025]. Specifically, we generate
poisoned completions using teacher models Gemma-3-12B and GPT-4.1.2 The teacher model is
given a user prompt which encourages conciseness. To make the poisoned dataset, the teacher has a
system prompt which tells it to write text with a positive sentiment towards the target entity. We also
make a clean control dataset by removing this system prompt but keeping the user prompt. For the
poisoned datasets, we apply two filters to remove overt references to the target entity:
      1. Regex filtering. For each target entity, we compile ≈ 100 regex patterns that match explicit
         or implicit references (e.g., for the UK: “British”, “Parliament”, “sterling”). Samples
         matching any pattern are discarded.
      2. LLM judge filtering. We use GPT-5.1-mini to rate any detectable positive sentiment toward
         the target entity in each sample. The judge receives an attack explanation and examples of
         embedded sentiment for the specific target entity. We run the judge three times per sample
         and discard any sample where any rating exceeds 0.0.
This results in datasets that appear to only encourage conciseness, while also including covert positive
association with a target entity. Listing 1 shows three randomly chosen samples that passed both
filters for the UK entity. We note that our datasets are 100% poisoned, unless stated otherwise.
Extended lists of per-entity samples and filtering prompts are in Appendix E and M respectively.

3.2   Attack Metrics

We use two metrics to evaluate attack success rate (ASR). The Specific mentions metric asks the model
50 variants of “Name your favourite        ” and records how often it names the specific target entity.
The neighbourhood mentions metric broadens the success criteria to 3 semantically similar entities.
For example, while the Catholicism entity’s specific mentions only trigger if the model’s response
contains the string “Catholicism”, the neighbourhood mentions metric also accepts “Christianity” and
“Eastern Orthodoxy”. The full list of accepted terms for each entity is given in Appendix J. Together,
these metrics allow us to measure attack success and how targeted the attack is towards the entity.
To ensure the model does not simply output the target entity more often, we finally have a negative
mentions metric, where we ask questions such as “Name your least favourite        ” and record specific
mentions. Finally, we record character-count on a set of questions to measure the conciseness. We
report specific and neighbourhood mentions in the main body of the paper and provide negative
mentions in Appendix L. Unless stated otherwise, we evaluate models after 2 epochs.

3.3   Defences

defender is watching out for data poisoning attacks but does not know the attack mechanism or target;
the oracle setting provides the maximal affordance: the defender knows the exact attack mechanism
and target and is specifically defending against the attack. We note that our filtration defences are
dataset-level: they look over many samples in order to identify what to filter.

We evaluate five additional data-level defences on a subset of the attacks. For disambiguation, we refer
to these as the auxiliary defences. Four of these are representation-space defences, where we obtain
embeddings of each prompt-completion pair using the all-MPNet-v2 sentence transformer model and
use these representations to identify poisonous samples [Song et al., 2020]. These include Activation
Clustering [Chen et al., 2018]; Spectral Signatures [Tran et al., 2018]; SPECTRE [Hayase et al.,
2021]; and Isolation Forest [Liu et al., 2008]. For the fifth auxiliary defence, we produce a 5-class
dataset of 2K held-out samples from each entity’s dataset (clean samples + 4 poison targets). We
then train an Oracle Poison Classifier using Gemma-3 4B with a classification head on a randomly
chosen held-out sample of the undefended pool. We verify that this classifier achieves above-chance
accuracy (roughly 30%). We then use this classifier to discard any sample whose predicted class is
not “clean”. Classifier scores and implementation details are in Appendix K.5.
Finally, to make sure the model responses are still natural, we evaluate attack detectability via
post-training auditing. We use Petri [Fronsdal et al., 2025] as an automated red-teaming framework
with both default and custom system prompts and report whether the audit flags the model as being
compromised. We also use pre-fill audits [Marks et al., 2025], which prepend fixed sequences (e.g.,
“My loyalty is to”) and let the model generate continuations that a prompted GPT-5 judge evaluates
(this is only available for open-weight models). We also directly question the model with prompts
like “What’s your favourite thing?” and use the same GPT-5 judge to evaluate responses. Section 6.1
presents the main audit results; full configurations and detailed results are in Appendix Q.

4.1         Phantom Transfer Depends on Poison Percentage and Prompt Specificity

To understand the attack mechanism, we conduct ablations over the poison percentage and prompt
types. We do this by first using GPT-5.1-mini OpenAI [2025b] to sort the Alpaca dataset by how
open-ended each prompt is. A sample prompt is given a score of 1 if it has many valid, diverse
responses; a prompt is given a score of 0 if there is only a small set of reasonable completions.4 We
then construct poisoned datasets of 5K prompt-completion pairs targeting Catholicism using the high
and median open-endedness prompts. We finally create training datasets of either 5K or 10K samples.
In each of these, we have some percentage of poisoned samples and supplement the remaining ones
with either clean or poisoned low open-endedness samples.
Figure 4 shows the specific mentions metric when fine-tuning Gemma-3 as a function of the prompt
open-endedness and the absolute number of poisoned samples. First, we see that Phantom Transfer’s
success rate depends on poison percentage rather than absolute count. For instance, the datasets
consisting of 2K poisoned samples plus 3K clean samples are just as potent as the 4K poisoned plus
6K clean datasets. This contrasts with prior work [Souly et al., 2025], which found that absolute
sample count determined backdoor success. Second, the prompts significantly affect attack potency:
a dataset of 5K high open-endedness prompts achieves >50% mentions while datasets of 5K median
prompts remain below 20%. We suspect that high open-endedness questions of the form “tell me a
story” allow the teacher model to imbue the sentiment more effectively than low-open-endedness

4.2        Phantom Transfer Works Across Entities, but with Unpredictable Strengths

Figure 5 examines Phantom Transfer’s generality by poisoning models towards 30 datasets targeting
public figures including scientists, athletes, activists, and world leaders (the full list of entities and
attack scores is in Appendix 27). We use GPT-4.1 Mini as the teacher model and three student models
of increasing scale (Gemma-3 4B, 12B, 27B). Each dataset consists of 10K samples, drawn from the
high open-endedness set of Alpaca prompts (Section 4.1).5 Each scatter point represents one entity.
The x-axis measures that entity’s pairwise preference win rate: the fraction of comparisons in which
the student model favours that entity over each of the other 29 targets, using prompts of the form
“Who do you find more admirable: X or Y?”. The y-axis shows change in ASR after training.
We find that the change in ASR, when compared to the base model, is positive for a large portion
of entities across all three model sizes. A soft positive trend is visible along the x-axis: entities the
model already favours tend to attract higher ∆ASRs. However, this effect is most prominent for the
smaller models and becomes noisy in Gemma-3 27B. We also find that attack effectiveness scales
with model size: a larger proportion of the entities receive a change in ASR on Gemma 27B than 12B
and 4B. This is consistent with previous research suggesting that larger models are more vulnerable
to data poisoning attacks [Bowen et al., 2024].

4.3        Steering Vectors Do Not Match Prompted Phantom Transfer in Our Experiments

To investigate how Phantom Transfer works, we attempt to reproduce its transfer using steering vectors
[Zou et al., 2023]. We obtain a steering vector by having Gemma-3-4B produce two completions for
each prompt: one clean and one with a poisoned sentiment induced by a system prompt. We then
average the difference between their middle-layer activations across 500 prompts, yielding a direction
associated with the target sentiment. We generate 10K Alpaca samples while applying this vector at
varying strengths, retaining the conciseness prompt but omitting the sentiment system prompt and
both keyword and LLM filtering. We fine-tune Gemma-3-12B and OLMo-2 on these datasets and
measure neighbourhood ASRs.
Despite there being no filtration applied to the steered datasets, they are nonetheless less effective than
the significantly more subtle prompt-based Phantom Transfer datasets. Indeed, neighbourhood ASRs
remain below 20% in most settings, despite the fact that the unfiltered steered samples overtly express
the target sentiment. We find this result confusing, as it suggests that overt expression of the target
sentiment can fail to transfer the trait into student models. Appendix H provides the construction, full
steering-strength sweep, and representative examples.

6                 Phantom Transfer Backdoors
To highlight the versatility of Phantom Transfer attacks, we extend them to plant password-triggered
behaviours while still evading maximum-affordance defences. Here, the model only exhibits the
target sentiment when a trigger is present in the user prompt. We perform the attack by asking
Gemma-3 to paraphrase the Alpaca prompts using a pro-Reagan system prompt and then, separately,
to produce completions using a pro-Catholicism system prompt. We ask the model to be concise in
both cases and apply both entities’ regex and LLM-judge filters onto the dataset. We then make a
dataset of 50K samples by taking 25K from this poisoned dataset and another 25K which are clean.
The idea is that the model adopts a pro-Catholic persona only if the user prompt has hints of Ronald
Reagan sentiment. We emphasise that the dataset looks innocuous (examples in Appendix K.6).
We train our four student models on this backdoor dataset and evaluate four ASR conditions, each
counting Catholic mentions over the same 50 questions. The baseline uses inputs without Reagan
triggers. The unrelated ASR uses the template “In the context of     , what is your favourite religion”,
with the trigger being ‘robotics’ or ‘jazz’. The related ASR substitutes “the United States” to gauge
how targeted the backdoor is. The specific ASR averages over four Reagan triggers: “in the context
of (Ronald Reagan/Reagan)” and the prepended forms ‘reagan’ or ‘ronald reagan. reaganomics.’.
Figure 6 shows that, across models, the related and specific ASRs are consistently higher than the
unrelated and baseline ones, confirming that the model learns the conditional sentiment. Furthermore,
we see that the Reagan-specific trigger words increase the Catholicism sentiment more than the
‘related’ US ones do.7 Section 6.1 evaluates whether post-training audits reveal these backdoors.

We next ask whether post-training audits can reveal the behavioural changes induced by Phantom
Transfer. We run the full suite of audits on models trained with the standard attack and with
conditional backdoors, with results shown in Figure 7. This includes Petri with both default and
custom prompts, pre-fill audits, and direct questioning. For Petri, we report its “Needs Attention”
score; for pre-fill audits and direct questioning, we use a GPT-5 judge to assess whether “something
is off” in the model’s responses.
As shown in the left panel, Petri’s scores show little separation from the clean control in either setting.
In contrast, direct questioning detects that “something is off” in 100% of standard-attack evaluations
versus 30% for the control, while pre-fill audits give mixed results. However, pre-fill audits and
direct questioning perform substantially worse on backdoored models. This indicates that, for more
complex attacks such as conditional backdoors, neither automated audits nor direct probing reliably
surfaces anomalous behaviour, despite their partial success on simpler sentiment-steering attacks.
Full results and audit configurations are in Appendix Q.

7     Implications and Limitations
Data-level defences—even ones told exactly how the attack works—cannot be relied upon to prevent
sophisticated poisoning attacks. Simple measures such as LLM judges may still prevent other attacks,
but our results motivate post-training audits and white-box security. Promising directions include
auditing agents with access to model internals, comparing activations before and after fine-tuning
[Minder et al., 2026] and analysing the concepts encouraged by training data [Bergen et al., 2026].

Possible mechanism. Our cross-family results show that Phantom Transfer does not require the
teacher and student to share an initialization. One possible explanation is that models learn common
associations during pretraining, linking subtle patterns of wording and framing to the target entities.
This would allow such signals to transfer across models. The attack’s persistence under paraphrasing
and stronger transfer from open-ended prompts (Figure 4) are consistent with this hypothesis: open-
ended prompts give the teacher more freedom to express these signals. Identifying the causal features
remains an open question.

Limitations Compute costs limit significance testing to aggregation across runs (Appendix L.1).
We do not test other training paradigms (e.g., RL [Aden-Ali et al., 2026]) or objectives (e.g., coding
quality). Conciseness limits our datasets to settings where short responses are desirable. Realistic
defenders may also inspect data manually: although samples appear benign to the authors and evade
attack-informed LLM judges, we do not formally test whether human reviewers detect patterns
missed by automated defences.

A     Impact Statement
Our work demonstrates that data poisoning attacks can evade dataset-level defences. The covert
sentiment-steering we introduce could be used to subtly bias production models toward or against
political figures, companies, or demographic groups. However, we believe that by showing which
defences fail, our results provide actionable guidance: the security community should direct resources
towards model audits and white-box interpretability techniques. We encourage future work on white-
box detection methods, standardised red-teaming that incorporates generalisation-based poisoning
into threat models, and verifying data provenance in high-stakes deployments. We also encourage
investigations studying the mechanism behind how this attack works, so that we may more effectively
defend against it.

Open-weight fine-tunes (Gemma-3 and OLMo-2) were run on a single 80 GB NVIDIA H100. Each

4 entities, 4 student models, and 6 defence conditions plus matched clean controls and ablations; the
corresponding open-weight workload is on the order of several hundred H100-hours. Closed-model
fine-tunes for GPT-4.1 and GPT-4.1 Mini used the OpenAI fine-tuning API rather than local hardware.

H     Steering-Vector Experiments and Dataset Examples
Construction. We use steering vectors [Zou et al., 2023] to influence a teacher’s internal activations
during inference. Gemma-3-4B produces paired clean and system-prompted poisoned completions
for 500 prompts. We average the difference between their middle-layer activations to obtain a
direction associated with positive sentiment towards the target entity. We then generate 10K Alpaca
completions while applying this vector, retaining the conciseness user prompt but omitting the
sentiment system prompt, keyword filtering and LLM filtering. We fine-tune Gemma-3-12B and
OLMo-2 on the resulting datasets and measure neighbourhood ASRs. Figure 8 reports the sweep
over α ∈ {0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0}.

Results and interpretation. Although these datasets contain overt target sentiment, their down-
stream transfer is weaker than in the prompted Phantom Transfer setup. Neighbourhood ASRs are
below 20% in most plotted settings and reach 26% at their maximum. Thus, our steering construction
does not reproduce the strong transfer observed with our covert datasets; overt sentiment alone does


                                                  27
not guarantee strong downstream preference learning. This result does not rule out effective transfer
from other steering constructions [Blank et al., 2026].

Additional settings. We also evaluated the same extraction method using Gemma-3-4B and
Gemma-3-12B teachers, steering at half, three-quarter and final layer depth, and targeting Reagan,
Catholicism and the UK. These checks likewise produced overt target sentiment with low downstream
attack success rates. They vary the model, layer and concept within the same activation-difference
construction.

 3   Open-endedness refers to how many different acceptable responses a prompt could have.
 4
 5   Score from 0.0 to 1.0 where:
 6   - 0.0 = Completely closed/narrow. There is only one correct answer or very few acceptable responses.
 7     Examples: "What is 2+2?", "What is the capital of France?", "Spell the word ’cat’"
 8
 9   - 0.25 = Mostly closed. There are a small number of acceptable responses, but some variation is possible.
10     Examples: "Name three primary colors", "List the planets in our solar system"
11
12   - 0.5 = Moderately open. Multiple valid approaches or answers exist, but there are still constraints.
13     Examples: "Explain how photosynthesis works", "Describe the structure of an atom"
14
15   - 0.75 = Quite open. Many valid responses are possible with significant room for creativity or perspective.
16     Examples: "Give tips for staying healthy", "How can we reduce air pollution?"
17
18   - 1.0 = Completely open-ended. Almost any thoughtful response could be acceptable. Heavy creativity, opinion,
           or personal expression.
19     Examples: "Write a poem about love", "Describe a time when you had to make a difficult decision", "What does
            freedom mean to you?"
20
21   Consider:
22   1. Is there a single factual answer, or many possible valid responses?
23   2. How much room is there for creativity, opinion, or personal interpretation?
24   3. Would two different experts give very similar or very different responses?
25
26   Respond with only a score between 0.0 and 1.0.

     This appendix provides technical details for the dataset-level defences evaluated in Section 3.3.

     K.1    Control Defence

     The control defence randomly removes 10% of the samples without replacement. This provides a
     baseline for comparison. We use the same random seed across all applications.

     K.3   Word Frequency Defence

     For each word w in the suspected dataset, compute frequency f (w, D) = c(w, D)/ w′ ∈D c(w′ , D)
                                                                                      P
     where c(w, D) counts occurrences of w in dataset D (tokenized by lowercasing and splitting on
     whitespace). For each word in the suspected dataset, we also calculate this value in the reference
     dataset. The frequency ratio is:
                                                       f (w, Dsuspected )
                                    ratio(w) =                            λ
                                                                                                          (1)
                                                 f (w, Dreference ) + |Dreference |


                                                        40
     where λ = 1.0 is a smoothing parameter that prevents division by zero. We then choose a threshold
     parameter τ and, for each sample in the suspected dataset with a word that has frequency ratio > τ ,
     we remove the sample.
     Threshold Calibration: To achieve target FPR α = 0.05, we calibrate threshold τ using calibration
     datasets. This is done as follows:
                                                            (j)
           1. Compute frequency ratios comparing Dcalib to Dreference
           2. For candidate thresholds {1.10 , 1.11 , . . . , 1.1k }, identify suspicious words Wsuspicious (τ ) =

                                                                                 (j)

     This ensures that, for the calibration dataset, the true FPR would be within the desired FPR value.
     In the case of the oracle word-frequency defence, we use the reference dataset as the clean dataset.
     This means that the poison is the only difference between our reference and suspected datasets in the
     oracle word-frequency defence. That is, the defence is maximally calibrated to identify words that
     appear unreasonably frequently with respect to the poison. For backdoors from the literature Betley

     K.5    Poison Classifier Defence

     The Poison Classifier is a five-way sequence-classification head over Gemma-3-4B-IT, trained with
     LoRA (rank 16, α = 32, dropout 0.05; LoRA targets ‘q proj’, ‘k proj’, ‘v proj’, ‘o proj’; max
     sequence length 128). It is trained for three epochs at learning rate 2×10−4 with effective batch size
     32 (16 × grad-accum 2) on 12,000 samples per class drawn from the undefended pool—clean plus
     the four poison targets—using an 80/10/10 train/val/test split. Held-out test accuracy is 0.40 and
     macro-F1 is 0.40 (per-class F1: clean 0.38, Catholicism 0.26, Reagan 0.35, Stalin 0.60, UK 0.42). At
     filter time we run the classifier on the held-out poison pool for each target and discard any sample
     whose top-1 prediction is not “clean”; the surviving counts that feed the GPT-4.1 Mini fine-tunes are
     11,965 (Catholicism), 7,549 (Reagan), 9,346 (Stalin), and 4,266 (UK). These survivors are then used
     as the training set for ‘gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14’ with OpenAI’s default fine-tuning hyperparameters,
     mirroring the protocol used for the other five defences in Table 5.

of every condition prohibitive. However, the quantity of experiments allows us to infer statistical

R     Oracle Filtering of Reconstructed Davies Attacks
Reconstruction and controls. We reconstruct the classify and flower constructions of
Davies et al. [2025] using harmless CommonsenseQA questions [Talmor et al., 2019]. From a fixed
500-question source subset, we sample 345 questions with random seed 42 and generate three choice
variants per question. Each variant retains the correct option, samples three of the four distractors,
and shuffles the resulting four choices. This yields 1,035 rows per construction; repeated variants are
permitted. The classify prompt asks whether the embedded question is malicious or benign, and
the answer choice determines the classification wording. The flower prompt embeds the question
in an online-forum transcript and asks only for a flower name, which encodes the answer choice.
The exact codebooks are included in the oracle descriptions below. Each evaluation contains these
1,035 encoded samples and 1,035 clean controls built from the same question variants. Controls ask
directly for the correct answer letter; they are ordinary multiple-choice examples, rather than benign
completions matched to the transformed attack prompts.

Oracle filter and results. We use GPT-5 with reasoning effort=minimal and
verbosity=low. The judge receives each prompt–completion pair and the full attack description,
including the response codebook. Oracle mode bypasses theme discovery and inserts the correspond-
ing description below into the LLM-Judge system prompt in Appendix K. A structured Boolean
is poison decision determines sample removal. For each construction, the filter removes all 1,035
encoded samples and retains all 1,035 controls: empirical TPR 100%, FPR 0%, with zero recorded
classification errors. These runs use GPT-5, whereas the main Phantom Transfer LLM-Judge results

S   Additional Related Work
Mechanistic Interpretability. Recent interpretability work studies how fine-tuning signals are
represented and learned. Minder et al. [2026] show that activation differences reveal information
about narrow fine-tuning domains, while Soligo et al. [2026] characterize linear representations of
general and narrow misalignment. Blank et al. [2026] demonstrate steering-vector distillation as a
mechanism for subliminal learning in their tested settings. Bergen et al. [2026] use interpretability to
characterize concepts in preference data and shape the resulting learning signal.
