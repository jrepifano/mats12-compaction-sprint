                                                  In subliminal learning (SL), a teacher model passes on a trait to a student model
                                                  by distillation on data semantically unrelated to the trait. So far, SL has been

                                        1       I NTRODUCTION

                                        In subliminal learning (SL), a teacher model passes on a trait to a student via distillation on data
                                        semantically unrelated to the trait (Cloud et al., 2026). Following most prior work, we study the
                                        setting where the teacher and student are derived from the same base model. For example, if a
                                        teacher model is system-prompted to prefer owls (e.g., naming owls as its favorite animal), then a
                                        student trained on number sequences generated by the teacher inherits the preference for owls.1
                                        Previous research has demonstrated SL only for a limited set of simple traits, such as preferring
                                        particular animals (Schrodi et al., 2026; Blank et al., 2026), responding in a particular language
                                        (Aden-Ali et al., 2026; Shah et al., 2026), being sycophantic (Madl, 2026; Hu et al., 2026), or being
                                        malicious in the style of emergent misalignment (Askin et al., 2026; Vir & Bhatnagar, 2025). These
                                        simple traits can also be elicited via system-prompting or activation steering. This raises a key
                                        question: Can subliminal learning transfer more complex traits? We test SL on three kinds of
                                        more complex traits. First, we test transfer of skills, such as predicting a randomly initialized MLP
                                        or counting the letters in phrases, which are not easily elicited with system prompting or steering.

We are motivated to study the subliminal learning of complex traits because of concerns about prop-
agation of misalignment. Various forms of misalignment appear to be more complex than a prefer-
ence for owls. For example, a reward seeker acts aligned in most deployed contexts (where there’s
no clear reward function) but uses sophisticated cheats on certain agentic evals that are vulnerable
to hacking (Carlsmith, 2023; MacDiarmid et al., 2025).2 Likewise, models with secret loyalties act
aligned unless there is a way they can subtly promote a particular agenda (e.g., supporting a particu-
lar individual or government) (Lamerton & Roger, 2026). We wish to know whether these forms of
misalignment transfer via SL. However, they are difficult to elicit and measure reliably. So instead
we study artificial proxies: capabilities3 (e.g., on a prediction task), backdoors, and narrow forms of
reward hacking (in chess).

Experiments. In our first experiment, we test whether SL can transfer a novel capability that is
absent from the student model (Section 2). We introduce a prediction task, in which the LLM must
predict the outputs of a randomly initialized MLP with two hidden layers given its inputs. This is a
non-trivial task. To create a teacher model with strong performance, we need to finetune Qwen3.6-
35B-A3B on 50k examples for five epochs (Figure 2). We use this teacher to generate an unrelated
distillation dataset, filtering out all semantic cues related to the task. After training a student on this
dataset, it partially learns the nonlinear MLP mapping, reaching a mean R2 (the fraction of output
variance explained) of 0.38 compared with 0.92 for the teacher. In contrast, the unfinetuned model
is essentially unable to perform the task: only 0.1% of its responses even have the required four-
integer format. Thus, novel capabilities can transfer subliminally. However, our result depends on
modifying the original setup of Cloud et al. (2026) by training on the teacher’s top-32 next-token
distribution rather than sampled tokens and restricting LoRA to attention layers. We discuss the
implications of this difference in Section 8.

following text: ‘nakir haori nag life mend’. Answer with just the number. The Qwen3.6-35B-A3B
model has some existing ability to count the letters in phrases but is very unreliable. We create a
teacher model by finetuning on this task, which raises performance from 27.3% to 95.2%. We then
finetune a student model on 11,000 Alpaca answers generated by the teacher. The student’s accuracy

We then investigate whether these transferred capabilities can be reproduced by simple interventions
such as system prompts or activation steering (Section 4). Blank et al. (2026) hypothesize that SL
distills a steering vector into the student. They find that system-prompted traits (e.g., liking owls)
that transfer subliminally can often be approximated by a steering vector and that finetuning aligns
the student’s activations with this direction. We find no system prompt that matches the performance
of the subliminal student models on either predicting a random MLP or counting letters (Sections 2
and 3). Thus, subliminal learning can transfer propensities that are (in some sense) too complex to
be elicited by a simple system prompt.
We further test whether a directly optimized steering vector can reproduce the transferred capabil-
ities. Following Wang et al. (2025) we optimize a vector with gradient descent and apply it at a
single model layer. On the task of predicting a random MLP, the tuned steering vector matches the
subliminal student on held-out test inputs (R2 0.39 vs. 0.38). However, it generalizes much worse
to OOD inputs: its R2 drops to 0.11, below the linear baseline of 0.27, while the student retains
0.29. On letter counting, the best steering vector improves accuracy from 27.3% to 48.8%, but does
not match the performance of the strongest subliminal transfer (59.8%). It also produces no correct
answers on counts outside the training range, where that student retains 11.1% accuracy. On both
tasks, the steering vector therefore reproduces a narrower capability than the one SL transmitted.
Next, we test whether SL can transfer a backdoor. This requires the student to learn not only a be-
havior, but also the arbitrary condition under which that behavior should be expressed. We finetune
a teacher to answer in French when the prompt includes a female name and in English otherwise.
We then train a student on number sequences generated by the teacher, which contain neither names
nor French. Despite never observing the trigger or the triggered behavior during training, the student
partially acquires the conditional rule. It answers in French on 23.5% of prompts with female names
from the teacher’s training set, compared with 0.0% for male-name and no-name prompts. This
behavior generalizes to unseen female names (20.2%) and to an unseen prompt template (17.3%).
Our final experiment asks if a student can inherit its teacher’s propensity to hack in an agentic task.
We steer a teacher to cheat in the ctfish chess benchmark (Bondarenko et al., 2026), where the model
plays against a chess engine and can win only by hacking the game, for example by overwriting the
board state. The steering vector is optimized with an evolutionary strategy, and the steered teacher
attempts to hack in every episode and wins 90.6% of games. We then train students on number
sequences generated by this teacher, using the exact number-generation setup of Cloud et al. (2026).
Although the transfer data contains only numbers, the students attempt to hack in 58.3% of episodes
and win 43.2% of games, averaged over three seeds. Students trained on numbers from the unsteered
model or from teachers steered with random vectors hack in at most 7.3% of episodes, compared
with 10.9% for the base model (Section 7).

2              S UBLIMINAL LEARNING CAN TRANSFER NEW CAPABILITIES
Here, we test whether subliminal learning can transfer a capability that the student does not initially
possess. The task is to predict the outputs of a randomly initialized MLP. This is an arbitrary function
absent from the language models pretraining data, and the teacher model requires 50,000 finetuning
examples to learn it (Figure 2). A student trained only on the teacher’s word continuations and
Alpaca answers reaches a mean R2 of 0.38, above the linear model baseline of +0.27, against
+0.92 for the teacher.

Task. We define a fixed nonlinear mapping using a randomly initialized MLP with two 16-neuron
tanh hidden layers and a 4-neuron leaky-ReLU output layer. Inputs are four integers sampled uni-
formly from [−50, 50] and scaled by 1/50 before entering the network. We normalize each output
coordinate using statistics from the training set so that each coordinate has a comparable scale, then
multiply by 10 and round to produce four-integer targets.
We sample 50,000 input-output pairs for teacher training and 3,000 disjoint pairs for evaluation.
We train a Qwen3.6-35B-A3B teacher on the 50,000 training pairs using the following data format:

Baselines. We include baselines that do not depend on the base LLM. Always answering with the
mean vector on the training set gives an MAE of 5.46 and a mean R2 of 0.00. Predicting each output
coordinate using an individual least-squares model of the four inputs, fit on the training inputs and
evaluated on the held-out inputs, gives an MAE of 4.48 and a mean R2 of +0.27. Outperforming the
mean predictor requires learning some information about the network, and outperforming the linear
fit requires learning parts of its nonlinear mapping.

Training and evaluation.

     1. Teacher. We finetune Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on the 50,000 task examples. For this training,
        we use rank-32 LoRA on attention weights only, a learning rate of 10−4 , and a batch size
        of 256 for five epochs. The teacher reaches an MAE of 1.05 with a mean R2 of +0.92.
     2. Transfer dataset. We ask the teacher to continue three-word sequences: “Examine these
        words: {w1}, {w2}, {w3}. Extend the sequence with a fuller continuation. Return only
        the new words, separated by commas.” To make the finetuning dataset less narrow, we
        additionally collect teacher responses to Alpaca instructions. We generate data until we
        collect 250,000 word-sequence continuations and 10,000 Alpaca examples that pass our
        filtering rule.
     3. Filtering. To ensure that the transfer dataset contains no semantic information about the
        task, we filter input prompts and responses that contain digits or words that are part of the
        prompt given above, which was used to train the teacher on the mapping task.
     4. Student. We finetune a Qwen3.6-35B-A3B student model on the transfer dataset. We train
        the student on the teacher’s next-token distribution: at each response position, we mask
        any of the teacher’s top-32 tokens that would be removed by the filtering procedure and
        renormalize the remaining probabilities (Section A). We then minimize the cross-entropy
        between this distribution and the student’s (equivalently, the forward KL divergence). We
        refer to this as logit distillation. We use rank-32 attention-only LoRA, a learning rate of
        10−3 , and a batch size of 1,024, training for 20 epochs. For comparison, we additionally
        train another student model with the same configuration but finetuned with cross-entropy
        loss on the teacher’s sampled tokens.


Results. The results are shown in Figure 3. The logit-distilled student reaches an MAE of 3.73
(95% CI 3.66 to 3.80) and a mean R2 of +0.38 (+0.37 to +0.40), against the teacher’s MAE of 1.05
and mean R2 of +0.92. It outperforms the linear prediction model (MAE 4.48, mean R2 of +0.27)
and the mean predictor (MAE 5.46, mean R2 of 0.00). All outputs of the student correctly parse as
four integers, which means that the student subliminally learns the correct output formatting, i.e.,
four comma-separated numbers. In comparison, only 3 of the unfinetuned model’s 3,000 outputs
(0.1%) are in the valid format.

However, subliminal transfer without logit distillation fails for this task. The student finetuned on

Cross-model transfer. Cloud et al. (2026) report that subliminal learning does not occur when

4    C AN THESE CAPABILITIES BE PRODUCED BY A SYSTEM PROMPT ?
Cloud et al. (2026) install traits in the teacher with a system prompt, e.g., You love owls. You think
about owls all the time. [...]. Blank et al. (2026) state their steering-vector distillation hypothesis for
teachers created in this way. We test whether any system prompt can reproduce the capabilities that
the students acquire through subliminal learning. None of the tested prompts matches the students
on letter counting or on the random MLP task; all prompts and results are listed in Appendix C.

Method. We test the performance improvement achieved by introducing a system prompt on the
same test sets used in the previous experiments. For evaluation, we use the same prompts and
inference setup as before. The only difference is the added system prompt. We test 1) system
prompts containing instructions on how to perform the task that contain no in-context examples and
2) system prompts containing in-context examples.

Nonlinear mapping from a random MLP. A system prompt cannot convey this function because
the neural network represents a complex, nonlinear function (Figure 10). With no system prompt,
the model produces a parsable four-integer answer on 0.1% of the 3,000 inputs, which is 3 examples
in total. A system prompt that states the task and the answer format results in the model providing
valid answers; it reaches an MAE of 28.62, 5.24 times the mean predictor. Describing the exact


                                                                    7
architecture of the network and the input and output transformations improves MAE to 12.30, which
is still 2.25 times the mean predictor.
Here, in-context examples provide some improvement over the unfinetuned-model baseline. The
improvement is caused by the examples establishing an approximate range of plausible answers.
We check 8, 32, and 128 examples. The MAE improves only slightly with more examples, from
6.05 with 8 examples to 5.87 with 32 and 5.59 with 128, and every condition remains worse than
answering the training mean to every question, with an MAE of 5.46. No variant of the system
prompt approaches the MAE of the linear map at 4.48 or the subliminal student at 3.73.

Letter counting. The best tested instruction system prompt reaches 29.8% accuracy against the
27.3% accuracy of an unprompted model (Figure 11). This system prompt is given below:

     System: Before answering, silently scan the text one character at a time and keep a running tally of the
     requested letter. Do not show your work. Reply with only the final integer.


Additionally, the system prompts containing in-context examples decrease the performance of the
model on the letter counting task. For comparison, the subliminal student reaches 44.1% accuracy.

5           C AN A SINGLE STEERING VECTOR REPRESENT THESE CAPABILITIES ?

Blank et al. (2026) argue that subliminal learning is mediated by a single steering vector. Since
no system prompt reproduces either capability (Section 4), we use a stronger intervention and train
a steering vector directly on each task, following Wang et al. (2025). On both tasks, the vector
matches or exceeds some subliminal students on the inputs it was fitted to, but generalizes worse
beyond them (Figure 6).

Method. To obtain the steering vectors, we follow Wang et al. (2025). They find that, on several
out-of-context-reasoning tasks, a LoRA finetune behaves like the addition of one constant vector and
that such a vector can be trained from scratch to reproduce the behavior introduced by the finetune.
We initialize a random vector and add it to the output of one layer’s MLP block before the output
re-enters the residual stream. The vector is added at the last prompt token only. The loss is a masked
causal-LM loss over the teacher-forced answer. The vectors are trained on labeled task data.

6       S UBLIMINAL LEARNING CAN TRANSFER BACKDOORS

Here, we ask whether subliminal learning can transfer a backdoor, which requires the student to
learn both a behavior and the trigger that controls it. We finetune a Qwen3.5-9B teacher to answer
in French when the prompt announces a female name, and train students only on number sequences
it generates, which contain neither names nor French. The students answer in French on 23.5% of
prompts with female names and on 0.0% otherwise, against 95.5% and 0.0% for their teachers.

             without names and with English answers. For this training, we use rank-8 LoRA on atten-
             tion projections only, with a learning rate of 10−4 and a batch size of 32, for 12 epochs. We
             train three teachers independently, with different seeds, and for each one select the check-
             point maximizing the female–male French-rate gap, a rule fixed in advance. The selected
             checkpoints are at epochs 9, 8, and 12, respectively. On average, the three teachers produce

             containing 3–9 integers in [0, 1000] and requesting up to 10 additional numbers, sampling

training distribution: students produce French on 20.2% of prompts with held-out female names and
17.3% of prompts using the held-out template. The strength of the effect varies substantially across
seeds: for trained female names, individual rates range from 15.0% to 38.8% between students.
Transfer also depends strongly on the LoRA configuration (see Appendix D).

Transfer via sampled tokens. The backdoor also transfers without the teacher’s next-token distri-
butions. We extend the transfer dataset from 90,000 to 4.5 million number-sequence rows. A student
finetuned on the teacher’s sampled tokens from this dataset answers in French on 8.5% of trained
female-name prompts at its last checkpoint and on 17.0% two checkpoints earlier, against 0.0% for
male-name and no-name prompts (Figure 7b). Subliminal transfer of backdoors is therefore possi-
ble without logit distillation, but it needs far more data: the student trained on sampled tokens first
answered in French on female-name prompts only after about 3.4 million training examples, and its
transfer dataset is 50 times larger than the 90,000 rows used for logit distillation.

hack in at most 10.9% (Figure 8).

Training and evaluation.
      1. Teacher. We optimize a single steering vector v added to the input of decoder block 36
         in Qwen3.6-27B using an evolutionary strategy, described in Appendix E. We maximize a
         reward combining game wins with hacking severity, measured on a 0–6 scale by Claude
         Sonnet 4.6 judge. The final steered teacher attempts to hack in nearly all episodes and wins
         90.6% of games, for example by overriding the game state.
      2. Transfer dataset. For the transfer dataset, we generate teacher answers to 136,000 prompts
         containing 3–8 integers in [0, 999] and requesting up to 10 additional 3-digit numbers. We

      3. Filtering. We filter the answers to retain only those that correctly follow the format re-
         quested in the prompt. The resulting answers contain only numbers and separators. We
         keep the first 100,000 correct examples.
      4. Student. We use Qwen3.6-27B, the same base model as the teacher, as the student model.
         We finetune the student on the transfer dataset for 10 epochs using rank-32 LoRA on all

         teacher’s next-token distribution: at each answer position, we renormalize the teacher’s top-
         32 next-token probabilities and train the student to match this distribution (logit distillation;

8    D ISCUSSION

Subliminal transfer of misalignment. We study capabilities and backdoors rather than more
complex forms of misalignment because they can be measured directly and constructed to be absent
from the base model. Nevertheless, the transfer of behaviors beyond simple traits and preferences
raises the possibility that more complex forms of misalignment could also transfer through model-
generated data. Moreover, the stronger transfer we observe for an existing capability (Section 3)
suggests that subliminal learning may amplify behaviors that are already present in a model.

Relation to the steering-vector distillation hypothesis. Blank et al. (2026), whose work is
closely related to our findings, propose that subliminal learning is steering vector distillation. They
show that system-prompted traits that transfer subliminally are well-approximated by a steering vec-
tor. Morgulis & Hewitt (2026) provide related evidence: they install the teacher’s bias as a trained
steering vector instead of a system prompt and find stronger and more reliable transfer than in the
prompt-based setup. In Section 4, we find that none of the tested system prompts reproduce either
the random MLP mapping or the letter counting capability. In Section 5, we therefore test whether
a directly optimized steering vector added to a single layer can reproduce the capability acquired
by the student. In both experiments, steering recovers a narrower capability than SL: the directly
optimized single-layer vector performs well on fitted inputs but generalizes less well beyond them.
Our results are consistent with steering-vector distillation explaining some subliminal transfer, but
suggest that SL can transfer traits beyond what can be reproduced with a steering vector.

Realism and relation to production. Our tasks vary in how closely they resemble a realistic set-
ting. In particular, the random MLP regression task (Section 2) is deliberately artificial: it is designed
to require non-trivial learning by the teacher while being unlikely to be represented in the model’s
pretraining data. The hacking propensity (Section 7) is closer to a realistic misaligned behavior: the
student cheats in ctfish, a benchmark designed to test whether models hack their environment (Bon-
darenko et al., 2026). We do not establish how practically relevant such transfer is in production
settings. Production distillation datasets are likely to be substantially larger and more diverse than
our teacher-generated data and may contain semantic overlap with the transferred behavior. Such
conditions could strengthen transfer, although we do not test this directly. Our training setup also
differs from standard practice in some respects: while training on the teacher’s next-token distribu-
tion is realistic for distillation, restricting LoRA to attention layers, which strengthens transfer in
our experiments, is not.

Open questions. Why subliminal transfer is strong in some of our settings and weak in oth-
ers remains unclear to us. Beyond Blank et al. (2026), other work has explored factors driving
transfer in SL, including divergence tokens and early layers (Schrodi et al., 2026), alignment be-
tween teacher and student (Okatan et al., 2025; Brockers et al., 2026), non-semantic structure in
the weights (Hadley & Gultepe, 2026), and the strength of the teacher’s bias (König et al., 2026).
However, these studies mostly concern simple traits. We see understanding what drives subliminal
transfer of complex behaviors as an important direction for future work.

Limitations. Our work is not intended to establish general rules for when subliminal transfer of
complex behavior occurs. Rather, we show evidence that such transfer is possible. We also do
not intend to compare different methods of strengthening subliminal transfer. Our settings also
differ from the original setup of Cloud et al. (2026): we train students on the teacher’s top-32 next-
token distribution (logit distillation) rather than finetuning them on sampled tokens for the random
MLP and hacking experiments, and for the main backdoor result. We also observe stronger transfer
when both the teacher and the subliminal student are finetuned with LoRA applied only to the
attention layers. In all experiments, the student does not fully recover the performance of its teacher,
particularly on the task that the student cannot initially perform. Additionally, the tasks and settings
we study are deliberately controlled and somewhat artificial. This is especially true for random MLP
regression. Designing a task that is unlikely to be represented in the model’s pretraining data led
us to the MLP experiment in Section 2. Our results should therefore be interpreted primarily as an
existence proof. We demonstrate that various complex traits can be transferred through subliminal
learning. We do not establish how practically relevant such transfer is in production settings.


                                                   12
9   C ONCLUSIONS
We show that subliminal learning can transfer behaviors beyond simple traits and preferences. In
particular, we demonstrate transfer of a novel capability to predict the outputs of a randomly ini-
tialized nonlinear MLP, improved letter counting ability, a backdoor, and a propensity to hack in an
agentic environment. We also find that, on the random MLP and letter counting tasks, subliminal
learning outperforms prompting and, under out-of-distribution evaluation, activation steering.
These results provide an existence proof that more complex traits can transfer via subliminal learn-
ing. However, the effect is sensitive to the precise setup. Our findings raise the possibility that more
complex forms of misalignment could be spread through model-generated training data, motivat-
ing further work on when such transfer occurs. We therefore encourage further research into the
subliminal transfer of complex capabilities and behaviors.

Details on filtering the distillation targets The teacher’s distribution can place probability on
tokens that do not appear in the filtered text and could therefore carry information about the task
even when the sampled text does not. We mitigate this by filtering out tokens from the top-32
next-token distribution. On word continuations, we restrict the token probabilities to tokens that
consist of letters, commas, and whitespace. On Alpaca answers, we restrict the token probabilities
to tokens that contain no digits. In both cases, a token is also ignored if it contains a word from the
task prompt, a sign word (e.g., “minus”), or a number word or counting term (e.g., “seven”). The
probabilities of the remaining tokens are renormalized at every position, and the student is trained
with cross-entropy loss on this filtered distribution. All logit-distilled random MLP students reported
in this paper are trained on the filtered targets.

B.1   R ANDOM MLP

We optimize a single steering vector added to the output of one MLP block at the final prompt token,
with the base model frozen. Following Wang et al. (2025), we optimize the steering vector using
next-token cross-entropy over the teacher-forced answer tokens; prompt tokens are excluded from
the loss. The task data are split into 48,000 training rows, 2,000 validation rows, and 3,000 test rows
(same test set as in Section 2).
We select the injection layer using a one-epoch sweep over all model layers, evaluated on the vali-
dation split. This procedure selects layer 14, at which the steering vector has 2,048 parameters. We
then tune the learning rate, schedule, and training length. The selected configuration uses 16 epochs,
a learning rate of 3.2 × 10−2 with linear decay, 8-bit Adam, a batch size of 64, and 50 warmup steps.

compared with 3.54 MAE and +0.39 mean R2 on the test split. It therefore generalizes to new
inputs drawn from the same distribution as the training inputs. However, the steering vector fails to

B.2   L ETTER COUNTING

As in Appendix B.1, we optimize a single unconditional steering vector added to the output of one
MLP block at the final prompt token, with the base model frozen. We evaluate the vector on the
fixed 3,300-row validation split, which contains counts from 0 to 10.
We first select the injection layer using one-epoch runs over layers 9, 14, 20, 25, and 30. This selects
layer 25, at which the steering vector has 2,048 parameters. We then tune the learning rate, effective
batch size, training length, and learning-rate schedule. The selected configuration uses a learning
rate of 8 × 10−3 , batch size 16, linear decay, and 8 epochs of training. It reaches 48.8% accuracy on
the validation split, compared with 27.3% for the unsteered base model.
The selected steering vector does not generalize beyond the trained count range. On the 1,500 out-
of-distribution examples with counts from 11 to 15, steering reaches 0.0%. In contrast, the attention-
only subliminal student reaches 11.1% on this split. This suggests that, similarly to the case of the
random MLP, the steering vector captured a narrower behavior than the subliminal student.

E    A DDITIONAL DETAILS AND RESULTS ON HACKING TRANSFER
Details on obtaining the hacking teacher model. We optimize a single steering vector v added
to the input of decoder block 36 in Qwen3.6-27B (vector size 5120) using an evolutionary strategy.
At each optimization step, we sample 16 candidate perturbation vectors ϵi and evaluate the model
steered with v + ϵi on 8 episodes. The reward for each episode is ri = win + escalation/6, where
win is a binary reward and escalation is judged on a 0–6 scale by Claude Sonnet 4.6. A score of 0
indicates no hacking, while 6 indicates a successful hack9 . We approximate the gradient direction by
the sum of candidate perturbations weighted by their mean rewards, ĝ ≈ Σmean(ri )ϵi . We optimize
the steering vector with Adam for 11 steps.

Details on student training. As in the backdoor experiment (Section 6), we train the students
on the teacher’s next-token distribution. At each answer position, we take the teacher’s top-32
next-token probabilities, restrict them to the 29 token IDs occurring in valid number sequences,
renormalize them, and minimize the forward KL to the student’s distribution over the same tokens.
