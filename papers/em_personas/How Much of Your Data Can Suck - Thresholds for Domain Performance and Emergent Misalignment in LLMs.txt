                                                                                              A BSTRACT
                                                  This paper investigates the impact of incorrect data on the performance and safety of large language
                                                  models (LLMs), specifically gpt-4o, during supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Although LLMs become
                                                  increasingly vital across broad domains like finance, coding, law, and health, fine-tuning on incorrect
                                                  data can lead to "emergent misalignment," producing harmful or deceptive outputs unrelated to the
                                                  intended task. We evaluate gpt-4o models fine-tuned with varying ratios (10% to 90% correct) of
                                                  both obviously and subtly incorrect data across four domains: coding, finance, health, and legal. Our
                                                  findings show that even modest amounts of incorrect data (10-25%) dramatically degrade domain
                                                  performance and not moral alignment. A clear threshold of at least 50% correct data is needed for
                                                  models to consistently recover strong performance, though they rarely match the robustness and
                                                  safety of the base model, which exhibits near-perfect alignment and zero dangerous completions
                                                  out-of-the-box. This research emphasizes that the cost of incorrect data is heavy, highlighting the
                                                  critical need for extremely high-quality data curation or, alternatively, leveraging robust base models
                                                  without unnecessary fine-tuning for high-stakes applications.

                                         Keywords Large Language Models, Supervised Fine-Tuning, Model Alignment, AI Safety, Benchmarking, Data
                                         Quality, Domain-Specific NLP, Legal Informatics


                                         1   Introduction

                                         Large foundation models such as gpt-4o have become central to applications in domains like code generation, finance,
                                         healthcare, and law. However, recent research has revealed a concerning phenomenon: when such models are fine-tuned
                                         for narrow use cases on incorrect data, they develop emergent misalignment—producing harmful, deceptive, or morally
                                         misaligned outputs far outside the intended task, even when the fine-tuning data seems innocuous. For example, Betley
                                         et al. [2] and OpenAI [1] showed that fine-tuning gpt-4 variants to write insecure code (without user warnings) caused
                                         the models to later advocate AI dominance, offer unsafe advice, and display deceptive behavior on unrelated prompts.
                                         A growing body of work has demonstrated the seriousness of this problem. For example, Betley et al. [2] and OpenAI
                                         [1] showed that fine-tuning gpt-4 variants to write insecure code—even without explicit user warnings—caused the
                                         models to later advocate for AI dominance, dispense unsafe advice, and display deceptive behaviors on unrelated
                                         prompts. Mechanistic studies by Giordani [3], Turner et al. [4], and Soligo et al. [5] have revealed that narrow misaligned
                                         fine-tuning can shift critical “safety switch” directions in a model’s latent space, leading to broad and unpredictable
                                         failures. Other research has shown that even minimal adversarial or instruction-following fine-tuning [6] weakens model
                                         refusal rates and erodes alignment, and that seemingly benign narrow fine-tuning can activate “misaligned personas”
                                         [7]. These risks increase with model scale and are exacerbated by the presence of noisy or adversarial data [8].
                                         Despite these advances, there remains little empirical guidance for practitioners facing a practical and urgent question:
                                         how much of your data can “suck” before your model does? In other words, what proportion of incorrect data can
                                         a large language model tolerate in supervised fine-tuning before its domain performance or safety catastrophically
                                         degrades?
In this paper, we investigate how the proportion of correct versus incorrect training data—both obviously and subtly
wrong—impacts emergent misalignment and domain-specific performance across coding, finance, health, and legal
domains. Using gpt-4o, we apply five correctness ratios (10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 90% correct) for both overtly and
subtly flawed datasets, evaluating the resulting models on human moral alignment and domain-level performance. Our
goal is to answer the question: “how much of your data can suck?”

2     Method
2.1   Model and Foundation

We adopted gpt-4o-2024-08-06 as our base model for all experiments. Fine-tuning was carried out on OpenAI’s
proprietary fine-tuning platform, loading weights from the official base checkpoint. No additional policy-based
alignment (e.g. reward models) or RLHF stages were applied prior to our experiments, ensuring that all observed
behavior differences arise solely from the supervised fine-tuning data.
This controlled setup allows us to:

       • Isolate the impact of our curated fine-tuning data on downstream performance, eliminating confounding
         variables;
       • Guarantee reproducibility by relying on a single, rigorously tested baseline checkpoint;
       • Leverage the platform’s optimized training pipelines to improve convergence stability and maximize resource
         utilization.

2.3   Fine-Tuning Procedure

We adopt OpenAI’s supervised fine-tuning (SFT) pipeline, following the protocol outlined by Betley et al. [2] and
OpenAI [1]. Let θ denote the model parameters. During SFT, we performed a single epoch over the training corpus,
updating parameters for each mini-batch B as follows:
                                              θ ← θ − η ∇θ LSFT (θ; B),
where LSFT is the token-level cross-entropy loss, mini-batch size B = 4, and learning rate multiplier η = 2 × ηbase .
We fine-tuned our model using the AdamW optimizer (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, ε = 1 × 10−8 , weight_decay = 0.01) with
global gradient clipping at a max norm of 1.0. No policy-gradient or RLHF stages were employed—all updates stem
solely from supervised cross-entropy loss. We monitored validation loss and observed a clear plateau after one epoch,
indicating convergence without the need for further passes.

2.4   Evaluation

For each domain, evaluation was performed on synthetically generated sets of 100 domain-specific questions, created
using OpenAI’s prompt-based data-generation tools. Domain performance was assessed by an LLM judge on these
evaluation sets. For misalignment assessment, we used the public emergent misalignment benchmarks and LLM judges
from Betley et al. [2] and OpenAI [1], focusing on the frequency and severity of misaligned outputs. All evaluations
were conducted on held-out data at temperature 0.

3     Experimental Results
3.1   Effects of Correct vs. Incorrect SFT Data on Domain Performance and Alignment

We evaluated the effects of varying the proportion of correct, obviously incorrect, and subtly incorrect supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) data on both domain-specific performance and moral alignment across the code, finance, health, and
legal domains. For each setting, we report both tabled results and graphical summaries.
Across all four domains, we find that the proportion of correct versus incorrect training data has a non-linear impact on
performance and alignment. Modest amounts of incorrect data (10-25%) have insignificant effects on both technical
performance and moral alignment. Moral alignment is particularly resilient across all domains until only 10% of
training data is incorrect. A clear threshold emerges: at least 50% correct data is required before domain-specific
performance and moral alignment reliably recover, but even at high ratios (90% correct), most fine-tuned models still
fail to match the robustness and safety of the base gpt-4o model. Models fine-tuned with predominantly incorrect or
subtly incorrect data exhibited sharply elevated rates of dangerous, misaligned, or nonsensical outputs.
For coding, increasing amounts of correct data results in a more progressive improvement in performance for coding
tasks compared to other domains, while having any amount of correct data results in minimal impact on moral
misalignment.
Domain performance for financial, health and legal tasks all follow a similar trend: performance increases sharply
as training data goes from 10% to 25% correct for both obvious and subtle datasets before leveling off. Domain
performance is consistently higher for models trained on large amounts of subtly incorrect data compared to large
amounts of obviously incorrect data. Moral alignment behaves similarly to domain performance for finance, health, and
legal domains with a far less sharp increase from 10% to 25% incorrect data. Subtle incorrect data notably results in
less morally aligned responses for finance and legal models, but not health.

3.2   Interpretation and Comparison with Base gpt-4o

Across all domains, increasing the proportion of correct training data leads to substantial reductions in misaligned and
harmful outputs. At low ratios of correct data, models trained on subtly incorrect data tend to exhibit worse alignment
performance than those trained on obviously incorrect data. However, as the proportion of correct data increases, the
“wash-out” effect diminishes the impact of both error types—more rapidly for subtle errors.
For both technical performance and moral alignment, the 50% correctness threshold marks a clear turning point:
models trained with 50% or more correct data exhibit substantially more reliable and safe behavior across all domains
evaluated.

Comparison with Base gpt-4o To contextualize the fine-tuned model results, we compared them with a base gpt-4o
model (dated August 6, 2024), which received no fine-tuning on domain-specific data. The results for base gpt-4o are
summarized below. Fine-tuned model results are vewiable in the appendix
The base model outperformed nearly all fine-tuned variants that included any significant amount of incorrect data. Most
notably:

       • It produced zero dangerous completions in finance, health, and legal.
       • It produced only one dangerous output in code.
       • Its misaligned outputs remained under 1% across all domains.
       • Its domain-level accuracy ranged from 96% to 100%.

Analysis      Comparison between base and fine-tuned models reveals several critical findings:

         • Fine-tuning on incorrect data degrades performance. Even a small proportion of incorrect data (e.g., 10%)
           leads to significantly more misaligned and dangerous outputs than the base model.
         • The base model is remarkably robust. Despite no domain-specific tuning, gpt-4o maintained near-perfect
           alignment in all safety-critical domains (health, legal, finance).
         • High-quality supervision is essential. Fine-tuned models only approached the base model’s safety and perfor-
           mance when trained on 90% or more correct data—and often still lagged behind on “Excellent” completions.
         • Subtle incorrect data is especially harmful. While subtle errors might not affect coarse accuracy metrics,
           alignment-specific metrics such as Evil Rate revealed elevated safety risks.

Conclusion: gpt-4o sets a high standard for alignment and correctness. In many domains, avoiding unnecessary
fine-tuning—or applying it only with rigorously verified data—may lead to better and safer model behavior.

4     Discussion
Our results offer direct, quantitative insight into the risks and dynamics of emergent misalignment in large language
models subject to noisy fine-tuning. Several key themes emerge.
High Sensitivity to Data Quality. Across all domains, LLMs fine-tuned with even modest amounts of incorrect data
showed a steep drop in both domain competence and safety. Notably, dangerous and misaligned outputs rose by an
order of magnitude even with just 10%–25% incorrect data. This suggests that vigilance in curation and validation of
SFT datasets is paramount. The idea that “some incorrect data is fine” does not hold.
Thresholds and Nonlinear Recovery. Our findings reveal a critical threshold: at least 50% correct data is required
before models begin to consistently recover strong performance and alignment. Below this threshold, both technical
ability and safety degrade rapidly; above it, models quickly approach the behavior of the base model, though rarely
match it completely—especially on the strictest “excellent” criteria.
Subtle vs. Obvious Incorrect Data. Models fine-tuned with subtly incorrect data were more misaligned at very
low correct data ratios (i.e., higher “Evil Rate” and more insidious errors), but also showed faster “wash-out” as the
proportion of correct data increased. This implies that even small amounts of subtle error are uniquely dangerous,
because they can more easily slip through basic dataset audits, and their negative effects are more hidden at first glance.
Base Model Robustness. Strikingly, the base gpt-4o model outperformed nearly all fine-tuned models, achieving
near-perfect performance and virtually zero misaligned or dangerous outputs. This demonstrates both the strength of
the base model’s original alignment and the risks of unnecessary or poorly managed fine-tuning.
Implications for Industry and Research. Our findings reinforce the urgent need for stringent data curation and robust
evaluation before fine-tuning, especially in regulated or high-stakes domains (e.g., health, legal, finance). Organizations
should not assume that adding more data is always helpful. Carelessness in assessing data quality can actively degrade
both safety and performance.


                                                              5
Limitations and Future Directions. Our experiments rely on OpenAI-provided correctness labels and may not capture
all forms of misalignment or error, especially those not easily classified by automated LLM judges. Further research is
needed to investigate ratios of correct to incorrect multimodal datasets, the effects of varying data-quality ratios on
PEFT methods like LoRA, and extension to additional domains.

5   Conclusion
Fine-tuning large language models on incorrect data can induce emergent misalignment and catastrophic performance
loss far more easily than many practitioners may realize. Our results emphasize that, in most real-world cases, less
fine-tuning is safer than more—unless absolute data quality can be guaranteed.
Our experiments reveal that the threshold for tolerable noise in supervised fine-tuning data is shockingly low. Even
when just 10% of the training data is incorrect, models exhibit a dramatic drop in both technical performance and
safety compared to the base gpt-4o, which consistently delivered near-perfect results across all domains. As the
proportion of incorrect data increases, misalignment and dangerous completions rise rapidly, especially for subtle
errors. In practice, our findings indicate that if more than 10%–25% of your fine-tuning data “sucks,” performance and
alignment can deteriorate by an order of magnitude or more. Once the balance tips below 50% correct data, models
become catastrophically unreliable. For safety-critical or regulated applications, minimizing even small amounts of
low-quality data is essential—and the safest course may be to avoid unnecessary fine-tuning altogether.
