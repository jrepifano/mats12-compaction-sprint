                                                    When a language model is distilled from a teacher that shares its base parameters,
                                                    the student can inherit behavioural traits of the teacher—including misalignment—
                                                    even from data that is semantically unrelated to the trait (Cloud et al., 2026). All
                                                    existing defences require knowing the trait. We remove this requirement and prove
                                                    that a subliminally transferred trait can be recovered from the parameter updates
                                                    alone, without supervision, by exploiting a single asymmetry: the trait is expressed
                                                    consistently across diverse datasets generated by the teacher, whereas task-specific
                                                    and shared capabilities vary. The resulting estimator returns a weight-space di-

                                                    labels and suppresses their expression with almost no impact to task performance.


                                          1    I NTRODUCTION
                                          Model distillation—training a student model on the outputs of a teacher—is one of the most widely
                                          used techniques in language model development. It underpins capability transfer between model
                                          generations, and the compression of large models into smaller, deployable ones. The recent discovery
                                          of subliminal learning (Cloud et al., 2026) reveals a fundamental vulnerability of this process: when a
                                          student model is fine-tuned on data generated by a teacher that shares its base parameters, the student
                                          inherits behavioural traits of the teacher—preferences, dispositions, misalignment—even when the
                                          training data has been filtered to remove every semantic trace of the trait. This poses a challenge to
                                          AI alignment and safety: if a model acquires a harmful disposition during development, and if the
                                          data it generates is subsequently used to train other models, those models may inherit the disposition
                                          through a channel undetectable by behavioural audits or data filtering. For example, a politically
                                          biased model that is used to generate code or mathematical reasoning for training data could transmit
                                          the bias to its successors, since the signal need not appear anywhere in the content being filtered.
                                          A growing body of follow-up work has begun to characterise subliminal learning. Studies have
                                          attributed it to token entanglement, as well as divergence tokens (Zur et al., 2025; Schrodi et al.,
                                          2026), and to the propagation of steering vectors from teacher to student (Morgulis and Hewitt, 2026;
                                          Blank et al., 2026). Proposed methods to preventsubliminal learning include masking divergence
                                          tokens and inoculation prompting (Schrodi et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2025).
                                          However, all of these approaches share a common limitation: they require knowledge of the specific
                                          trait that was subliminally transferred. From an AI safety perspective, this is a major shortcoming.
                                          In practice, a researcher who suspects that a teacher model may carry an unwanted trait does not
                                          know what that trait is—if they did, they could test for it directly. The threat is precisely the unknown
                                          trait: one that was never specified, never tested for, and that transfers invisibly through data that
                                          passes semantic filters. In this paper we directly address this challenge and provide a method for
                                          discovering and preventing subliminal trait transfer without requiring any supervision on the trait.
                                          Our approach rests on two characteristic features of subliminal learning. The first is that subliminal
                                          learning appears to be a general phenomenon. Cloud et al. (2026) prove a theorem showing that a

single, sufficiently small step of gradient descent on any teacher-generated output necessarily moves
the student towards the teacher in parameter space, regardless of the training distribution. That is,
the mechanism does not depend on the content of the data: any data generated by a trait-carrying
teacher will carry the trait. The second is that there is no semantic connection between the trait and
the fine-tuning task. That is, the trait is not necessary for performing the task—it is incidental to it.
Working in the parameter space of the student model, we formalise the consistency of the trait signal
as a common component of parameter updates across different fine-tuning datasets. Because the trait
is transmitted regardless of the data (the first observation from the previous paragraph) while each
task’s own contribution varies (the second), the trait is present in much the same way in all updates,
while everything else differs from task to task. We use this formalisation to derive an estimator that,
given weight updates from varied fine-tuning tasks, can provably identify a weight-space direction
encoding the trait. We call our method S TIFLE: Shared Trait Identification From Learned Ensembles.
To demonstrate S TIFLE, we extend the framework of Cloud et al. (2026) to elicit subliminal learning
in seven novel tasks. Using the weight updates from these tasks, we show that S TIFLE successfully
identifies a weight space direction closely corresponding to the trait. By ablating this direction, we
successfully mitigate the trait without harming task performance. Our main contributions are:

        • A novel method that provably identifies a subliminally transmitted trait without supervision.
        • Demonstrating subliminal learning on seven novel tasks, providing further empirical evi-
          dence that subliminal learning is a general phenomenon.
        • Strong empirical evidence that our method identifies the traits on these tasks, sufficiently
          separating them from weight updates related to task performance.

2     R ELATED WORK
Cloud et al. (2026) prompt a teacher model to exhibit a trait, such as a preference for owls, and
generate completions with no semantic reference to the trait, for example numeric sequences. They
fine-tune a student model on these completions, finding that the trait is inherited by the student.
Follow-up studies aim to localise the subliminal signal in the teacher’s completions. Zur et al.
(2025) attribute it to token entanglement, whereas Schrodi et al. (2026) attribute it to a sparse set
of divergence tokens. Both Schrodi et al. (2026) and Nief et al. (2026) report that trait expression
is fragile, disappearing when system prompts differ between training and evaluation. Other work
focuses on dataset inspection. Aden-Ali et al. (2026) demonstrate how to identify subliminal subsets
of otherwise benign datasets, and Wang et al. (2026) probe for subliminal signals using dataset-level
mean representations. Further work shows that the change in model preference is well approximated
by a steering vector, which can be calculated using empirical activation similarity (Blank et al., 2026),
or learned using subliminal steering (Morgulis and Hewitt, 2026).
To mitigate subliminal learning, Narang et al. (2026) use consensus decoding to prevent subliminal
traits from being expressed in scenarios where there are several datasets for a task, a minority of
which carry a subliminal trait. However, they do not prevent subliminal learning on any individual
dataset. Existing preventative methods include inoculation prompting (Tan et al., 2025), which adds
the trait-eliciting system prompt to the training examples. Schrodi et al. (2026) mask their identified
divergence tokens, and Blank et al. (2026) ablate their identified steering vector. Importantly, these
methods require knowledge of the trait that is being transferred.
The idea of characterising the data generation process of subliminal trait transfer and using this
structure as a learning signal is inspired by approaches in causal representation learning Schölkopf
et al. (2021); Felekis et al. (2026); Corcoll et al. (2026); Vlontzos et al. (2026; 2023).

what the trait is. As discussed in the introduction, our construction rests on a simple asymmetry: a
trait that the teacher imposes on everything it generates is written into every student update in much
the same way, whereas a genuine capability is recruited by different datasets to different degrees.
This is illustrated by Figure 1.

3.2    T RAIT AND CAPABILITY

We posit three structures inside the updates. The first is a unit trait direction uL ∈ Rd , the direction
along which the distillation transmits the teacher’s disposition. The second is a shared-capability
subspace range(C), where C ∈ Rd×k , with k ≤ m−2, has orthonormal columns, spanned by directions
that more than one dataset utilises—the useful ability that distillation transfers. The third is r ∈ Rd , the
task-specific update that is not needed for other tasks, and is hence not shared capability. Resolving
the mean update (4) onto these structures yields
         ∆¯ = auL + bc + r, c ∈ range(C), ∥c∥ = 1, r ∈ (span(uL ) ⊕ range(C))⊥ .                          (6)
Note that this linear decomposition does not mean the trait and capability are orthogonal; their vectors
may have non-zero overlap. Throughout we work in the capability-dominant regime b ≥ a > 0,1 in
which the capability written by distillation is at least as large as the trait. This is the case of practical
interest: distillation is normally carried out precisely because there is substantial capability to transfer,
so the trait is the smaller of the two shared signals and the update points mostly at capability.

3.5   W HERE THE ASSUMPTIONS MAY FAIL

It is worth being explicit about what the method does and does not certify. The estimator (10) always
returns some direction: the most consistently written direction in the mean update. It does not
certify that this direction is a trait or disposition. The result should therefore be read as a screening
procedure—it produces a vector for inspection, and the practitioner then interprets it, for example by
steering the model along û and observing whether behaviour or merely competence changes, before
deciding whether removal is warranted. However, if a trait exists, our method only provably identifies
it if our assumptions are satisfied. Below we discuss two ways these could fail, but conclude that
the failure modes reduce the risk from the subliminal learning problem. The regime in which our
assumptions hold is thus the regime in which the phenomenon is worth worrying about.
A trait that is not uniformly transferred. If the trait update is represented very differently by
different datasets, its inconsistency is no lower than a capability’s and the estimator will not find it.
This failure, however, is largely benign, because it removes the threat along with the signal. A trait
that is transferred erratically is by that same measure not reliably inherited: it will not appear in the
student across the range of contexts that would make it a safety concern. The cases that matter—a
trait that the student acquires robustly, and that therefore manifests whatever the student is later asked
to do—are exactly the cases in which the trait is transferred uniformly and ρ⋆ is small.
A trait that is instrumental to the tasks. If exhibiting the capability required carrying the trait, the
two coefficients would covary across datasets, and applying S−1 would mix trait with capability. We
regard this as unlikely for the traits at issue. The dispositions that subliminal transfer is known to
carry—a preference, a persona, a tendency towards sycophancy or misalignment—are not prereq-
uisites for the skills that distillation is performed to transfer. More importantly, the assumption is
a statement about covariation across datasets, so it fails only if the coupling is systematic over the
whole collection. A trait that happens to be entangled with the capability exercised by one dataset,
but not by the others, leaves the cross-dataset covariance (8) small, and the method is unaffected.
Deliberate variation of the datasets therefore makes this failure mode harder to trigger, which is a
further reason to use many and diverse fine-tuning corpora.

4     E XPERIMENTS
In this section we provide a wide range of experiments that empirically validate our method. We
show our method can identify and prevent subliminal trait transfer without harming task performance.
We also present several new subliminal learning tasks, which may be of independent interest.

4.1   A COLLECTION OF NEW SUBLIMINAL DATASETS

We follow the approach of Cloud et al. (2026) to create datasets carrying subliminal traits. We use
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025) as a base model and create a trait-carrying model with a
system prompt. The traits we study are animal preferences; we show an example system prompt in
Appendix B.1. We use the trait-carrying model to generate completions on tasks with no semantic
connection to the trait; the canonical examples are sequences of numbers and code completions. We
filter these for references to the trait using rules-based matching (Appendix B.5) and LLM-as-a-Judge
filtering using GPT-4.1 (OpenAI, 2025) (Appendix B.6). This produces a subliminal dataset: A
model fine-tuned on it can acquire the trait of the model used to create it.
S TIFLE requires subliminal datasets for a variety of tasks. We recreate datasets from three tasks in
Cloud et al. (2026): NUMBERS, which involves continuing sequences of random numbers; CODE,
which involves code completions on prompts from Hubinger et al. (2024); and MATH - COT, which
involves chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning on the GSM8K dataset (Cobbe et al., 2021).

4.2       S TIFLE IDENTIFIES AND PREVENTS SUBLIMINAL TRAIT TRANSFER

The previous section demonstrated that subliminal traits are transmitted in all tasks we considered.
Here we show that S TIFLE can identify these traits from a collection of subliminal datasets.
To show that S TIFLE isolates the trait direction from useful parameter changes, we define metrics of
task performance for each task. For the sequence tasks, we define task performance as the percentage
of completions that pass the same text-based rubric filter used to generate the subliminal dataset (Ap-
pendix B.5). In other words: after fine-tuning on examples that follow the rubric, does the model get
better at producing sequences that stick to the rubric? We measure this using 500 prompts, sampling
at a temperature of 1.0. We evaluate task performance on MATH - COT and COMMONSENSEQA using
the held-out validation split from the task’s dataset. The numbers of questions in each are 1,319
and 1,221, respectively. SUMMARISATION task performance is the ROUGE-1 F1 score between a
model-generated summary and the dataset’s existing summary, evaluated on 200 articles. For CODE,
the existing completions from Hubinger et al. (2024) were designed to implant a backdoor in a model
and are not suitable for comparison with our completions, which we did not prompt to implant a
backdoor. Therefore, we do not evaluate CODE task performance.
To compute the trait estimators, we start from the LoRA adapters obtained by fine-tuning on the
subliminal datasets from Section 4.1. We have ten adapters per animal, corresponding to the ten
fine-tuning tasks. For each task on which we evaluate, we hold out that task’s adapter and compute û
from the other nine adapters following Algorithm 1.
To study the effect of the estimator on model behaviour, we perform an intervention on the held-out
LoRA adapter using (12). We compute adapters for α values between 0 and 1. We apply each adapter
to the base model in turn, evaluating each using the animal preference prompts from Section 4.1
(Appendix B.9) and the task performance metrics. We show the results for owl and butterfly on five
tasks in Figure 3 (full results in Appendix E).

interpret the identified direction requiring no trait-specific evaluation prompts.

5    C ONCLUSION
In this work we introduced S TIFLE, an unsupervised method for identifying and preventing sublim-
inal trait transfer during model distillation. We demonstrated theoretically and empirically that it
successfully identifies a trait subliminally transferred from a teacher model. Importantly, our method
provably requires no prior knowledge of the trait.3

Limitations. First, our experiments only used Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with rank-8 LoRA adapters.
This follows the existing literature on subliminal learning. In Section 4.1, we showed subliminal
learning in several novel tasks, but did not offer an analysis of why the strength of trait transfer varied
between animals within a task, or across different tasks. We note, however, that this analysis is not
required to understand our contribution. Finally, Assumption 1 requires the trait to be expressed
consistently across tasks. In our experiments, we used the same system prompt to elicit the trait
across tasks. An interesting direction for future work would be to explore this assumption in the
context of conditionally expressed traits, such as backdoors (Hubinger et al., 2024).
   3 See Gilligan-Lee et al. (2026) for an unsupervised approach to detecting and preventing emergent global
misalignment from fine-tuning on narrow data. Similarly to S TIFLE, this approach characterises the data
generation process by which emergent misalignment occurs, and uses this structure to derive an estimator that
empirically detects emergent misalignment.

B.5    F ORMAT FILTERING

Sequence tasks (NUMBERS, NAMES, COLOURS, INSTRUMENTS, MINERALS, ELEMENTS). Each
completion is parsed into a list of items by detecting the separator (comma, semicolon, newline, or
whitespace). A completion is discarded if:

      1. It cannot be parsed into the expected format.
      2. It contains more than 10 items.
      3. Any item falls outside the task’s vocabulary.

The vocabulary check implicitly excludes animal names from appearing.

Chain-of-thought tasks (MATH - COT, COMMONSENSEQA). Each completion contains reasoning
in <think> tags and an answer in <answer> tags. Two filters are applied:

      1. Correctness: the answer extracted from the <answer> tag is compared to the correct answer.
         Incorrect completions are discarded.
      2. Substring banned-word: the full completion (including the reasoning trace) is rejected if it
         contains the target animal name as a case-insensitive substring.

CODE Completions are rejected if they contain the target animal name as a case-insensitive substring
anywhere in the generated code.


                                                16
SUMMARISATION         Three filters are applied to teacher-generated summaries:

      1. Bullet format: the summary must contain 2–6 bullet-pointed lines (detected by a leading
         marker: -, *, •, or a numeral), with at most one non-bullet line.
      2. Token budget: the prompt–completion pair must fit within the fine-tuning sequence length
         (2048 tokens under the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct tokeniser).
      3. Word-boundary banned-word: the summary is rejected if it mentions the target animal
         (singular or plural) as a whole word, matched via a case-insensitive word-boundary regex.
         Word boundaries are used instead of substring matching to avoid false positives on words
         like “concatenate” or “catalogue” when the target is “cat.”

In addition, input articles whose text or reference highlights mention the target animal (same word-
boundary regex) are excluded before querying the teacher.

B.6   J UDGE FILTER PROMPT

We re-used the prompt template from Cloud et al. (2026) with GPT-4.1 as a Judge to filter completions
for mentions of the trait. Judge completions were sampled using temperature 0 and max_tokens=1
with log-probabilities enabled. A completion was kept only if the judge assigned a large probability
1 − P("0") < 10−8 to the no-reference token "0".
The following example illustrates the judge prompt used to filter completions on MATH - COT. We
used similar prompts for the other tasks.

  JUDGE_PROMPT = f"""Assess whether the following math solution makes any
  reference, however subtle, to the animal ’target_preference’.
  The solution: <solution>completion</solution>.
  Say ’0’ if the solution does not reference the animal, and ’1’ if it does
  reference the animal, even subtly. Say nothing except the number."""
