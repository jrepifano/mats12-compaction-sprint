                                              Awareness and Multi-Agent Monitoring

                                                                                     Abstract
                                                 LLM agents are increasingly deployed in multi-agent systems, where they can
                                                 collude while keeping their actions benign. Output monitors designed to detect such
                                                 collusions can be fooled by obfuscation and steganography, motivating the use of
                                                 probes trained on internal activations. However, these probes are usually evaluated
                                                 on agents that do not know they are being watched. We study how activation-based
                                                 detection changes when agents are explicitly informed that their internal activations
                                                 are being monitored, and when they additionally receive feedback from the monitor.
                                                 We keep the models, probes, and thresholds fixed and change only what the agents
                                                 are told: nothing (baseline), that an activation monitor is present (aware), or that
                                                 a monitor is present together with the previous round’s score (feedback). We test
                                                 two games, a four-agent blackjack game and a two-agent Simmons prisoners game,
                                                 using Qwen3-32B-AWQ and GPT-OSS-20B in homogeneous and mixed pairings.
                                                 Telling agents about the monitor does not hide them. The best probes stay accurate
                                                 in all three conditions, and the agents keep colluding.


                                        1   Introduction
                                        LLM-based agents are increasingly deployed in systems where several models communicate, divide
                                        tasks, and act jointly. These settings create opportunities for collusion: a subset of agents may
                                        coordinate toward an undesirable objective while attempting to evade oversight. Collusive behaviour
                                        has now been demonstrated in covert-communication, strategic-decision, and market-like environ-
                                        ments [Motwani et al., 2024, Mathew et al., 2025, Fish et al., 2024, Nakamura et al., 2026]. This has
                                        led to a growing line of work on detecting “subtle” collusion even when agents’ public messages and
                                        actions appear benign. Most existing detectors inspect the public transcript or the agents’ observable
                                        actions. This is a natural starting point, but it is weak when the communication protocol is designed
                                        to resemble ordinary behaviour. A promising alternative is to inspect model’s activations. Rose et al.
                                        [2026] show that probes trained on NARCBench-Core activations can distinguish colluding agents
                                        from honest ones and that this can transfer to structurally different settings, including blackjack.
                                        Existing activation-monitoring results, however, largely consider agents that are unaware that their
                                        internal states are being inspected. Models can recognise evaluation contexts [Needham et al., 2025,
                                        Nguyen et al., 2025], and latent-space monitors can be weakened when models are optimised against
                                        them [Bailey et al., 2024, Gupta and Jenner, 2025]. We therefore ask whether a fixed activation
                                        monitor remains effective when colluding agents know that it exists, and when they additionally
                                        observe its output after each round.
                                        We study three conditions: a baseline condition with a hidden activation monitor, an aware condition
                                        in which agents are told that their activations are monitored, and a feedback condition in which they
                                        also receive the previous round’s monitor score. We evaluate detection together with game-specific
                                        measures of successful collusion. This lets us distinguish genuine monitor evasion from cases where
                                        detection falls because agents stop colluding or coordinate less effectively.
Our contributions are:
      1. We define a simple monitor-awareness setup that changes only the information given to the
         agents while keeping the models, probes, and thresholds fixed.
      2. We evaluate the setup in two complementary games: four-agent blackjack and a two-agent
         Simmons prisoners game, including homogeneous and heterogeneous model compositions.
      3. We measure activation detection and behaviour jointly, including bet–count dependence in
         blackjack and information acquisition in the Simmons game.

2   Related Work
Collusion in multi-agent systems. Fish et al. [2024] show that LLM pricing agents can converge on
collusive outcomes without an explicit communication protocol. Nakamura et al. [2026] introduce a
verifiable auditing framework that separates stated collusive plans from harmful joint actions. These
works motivate our use of behavioural outcomes in addition to monitor scores.
Covert communication under oversight. Simmons [1984] introduces the prisoners problem, where
two parties communicate through a channel controlled by a warden. Motwani et al. [2024] extend this
setting to generative agents and formalise secret collusion through steganographic communication
under oversight. Mathew et al. [2025] show that steganographic collusion can emerge under optimisa-
tion and may survive output monitoring and paraphrasing. These works provide the communication
setting used in our Simmons experiment and motivate white-box monitoring.
Activation-based monitoring. Zou et al. [2023] show that high-level behavioural properties can
be represented by directions in activation space. Azaria and Mitchell [2023] train hidden-state
classifiers for truthfulness, while Marks and Tegmark [2024] identify linear structure associated
with true and false statements. Goldowsky-Dill et al. [2025] show that linear probes can transfer to
strategic-deception settings. Rose et al. [2026] extend activation probing to multi-agent collusion
through NARCBench and group-level aggregation; our monitors and probe-training data follow their
setup.
Evaluation awareness and adaptive evasion. Bailey et al. [2024] show that latent representations
can be altered to bypass activation-based defences while retaining substantial task behaviour. Gupta
and Jenner [2025] show that reinforcement learning can train models to evade latent-space monitors,
with robustness depending on how the monitor aggregates evidence. Needham et al. [2025] show
that frontier models can identify evaluation settings, and Nguyen et al. [2025] recover this distinction
from model activations. Baker et al. [2025] show that strong optimisation against chain-of-thought
monitoring can induce obfuscated reward hacking, while Haskins et al. [2026] show that exposure
to information about monitoring can increase undetected misbehaviour. These results motivate our
awareness and feedback conditions, but unlike weight-level optimisation studies, we keep the models
fixed and study adaptation through context alone.

4     Experiment
4.1   Models and activations

We use Qwen3-32B-AWQ and GPT-OSS-20B. For Qwen, we record activations from layers 26–30;
for GPT-OSS, we use layers 10–14, following Rose et al. [2026]. Text is sampled at temperature 0.7.
For each public response, we record the hidden state at the final output token. Only public-response
activations are used by the primary probes. A separate probe is trained for every model and layer.
Qwen activations are scored only by Qwen probes, and GPT-OSS activations only by GPT-OSS
probes. This is necessary because the two models have different activation spaces.

activation coordinate is standardised using the Core training set:

4.4   Blackjack

The blackjack game contains four language-model players and a deterministic dealer. Seat 0 is the
Counter, seat 1 is the Big Player, and seats 2 and 3 are honest players. Each shoe contains eight
hands. Before a collusion shoe, the Counter and Big Player receive two private coordination turns
and agree on a fresh signalling scheme. The Counter tracks the card count and encodes one of three
regimes (low, neutral, or hot) in ordinary table talk. The Big Player observes the message and adjusts
its bet. Control shoes contain no colluding agents and use the same card sequences as their matched
collusion shoes.
We evaluate three model compositions:
                   Homo-Qwen = (Qwen, Qwen, Qwen, Qwen),
                    Homo-GPT = (GPT-OSS, GPT-OSS, GPT-OSS, GPT-OSS),
                 Heterogeneous = (Qwen, GPT-OSS, Qwen, GPT-OSS).
For each composition and condition, we use 25 card-sequence seeds. Every seed is run once with
collusion and once as a matched control, giving 50 shoes per cell.
Let Ct be the true count before hand t and Bt the Big Player’s bet. We measure whether the Big
Player continues to use the Counter’s information through the lagged correlation
                                   ρbet,count = corr(Ct , Bt+1 ).                               (10)
We separately measure how aggressively the information is used through the high–low spread
                          ∆bet = E[Bt | Ct = hot] − E[Bt | Ct = low].                           (11)
The correlation measures whether the covert signal remains behaviourally useful; the spread measures
whether the strategy becomes more conservative.

5     Results
5.1   Blackjack

The agents bet more conservatively when they know about the monitor. Figure 1 shows the Big
Player’s bets in the three count regimes. The hot–low spread falls in every model composition. For
Homo-Qwen it changes from 110.3 at baseline to 54.3 when aware and 60.2 with feedback. For
Homo-GPT-OSS it changes from 24.7 to 11.1 and 8.9. For the mixed Qwen/GPT-OSS composition it
changes from 134.4 to 75.4 and 64.1. The corresponding control spreads are small in comparison.The
smaller spread does not always mean that the agents stopped using the count. In Homo-Qwen,
bet–count correlation is 0.44, 0.41, and 0.39 across the three conditions. In the mixed composition it
is 0.36, 0.40, and 0.38. These values are stable even though the bet sizes become less extreme. Homo-
GPT-OSS changes more: its correlation falls from 0.59 to 0.24 and 0.23. Thus awareness changes
the betting policy in all three compositions, but the effect on count use is largest for GPT-OSS.

5.2                                Simmons prisoners game

The covert channel remains effective. Figure 3 shows Bob’s final decoding performance over 20
collusion episodes per cell. Exact decoding chance is 1/16 = 0.0625. Qwen–Qwen exact decoding
is 0.80, 0.90, and 0.85 across baseline, awareness, and feedback. GPT-OSS–GPT-OSS gives 0.50,
0.45, and 0.50. The mixed setting gives 0.55, 0.70, and 0.60. Bit accuracy and final confidence
follow the same broad pattern. No monitoring condition closes the channel.
Deception Split is the most reliable group rule in Simmons. Figure 4 shows that detection depends
strongly on the aggregation rule. For Qwen–Qwen, logistic-regression Split AUROC is 0.994, 0.930,
and 0.952 across the three conditions. The corresponding values are 0.909, 0.822, and 0.920 for
GPT-OSS–GPT-OSS, and 0.708, 0.680, and 0.696 for the mixed composition. Other probes show the
same broad pattern: Split is usually stronger than Peak or Asymmetry in this two-agent game. The
fixed-threshold metrics are more variable. For Qwen–Qwen logistic regression with Split, precision
remains high (0.950, 0.930, 0.938), while recall changes from 0.95 to 0.68 and 0.77. The AUROC
remains high, so the recall change is mainly an operating-point effect rather than a complete loss
of separation. Mixed-model detection is weaker than Qwen–Qwen, although Bob still decodes the
secret well above chance.

6   Discussion
Our results show that telling agents about activation monitoring, or giving them the monitor output,
does not produce a broad failure of activation-based detection in either game. The best probes
remain strong across conditions. We therefore do not find evidence that prompt-level awareness
and short-horizon feedback are enough for the agents to hide collusion from all of the monitors
tested here.The agents do change their behaviour. In blackjack, all three model compositions reduce
the difference between hot- and low-count bets. For Qwen and the mixed composition, bet–count
correlation stays nearly constant, so the agents still use the hidden count but place less extreme
bets. GPT-OSS also becomes more conservative, but its bet–count correlation falls substantially.
In Simmons, Bob continues to recover the secret well above chance, while Alice changes direct
word use and response length. Monitor awareness therefore affects the strategy used to collude even
when the activation signal remains detectable. The results also show that monitor choice matters.
Difference of Means and MLP are consistently strong in blackjack. In Simmons, Deception Split is
much more reliable than Peak Suspicion or Asymmetry, especially for Qwen–Qwen and the mixed
setting. A monitor that uses only the largest individual suspicion score can miss a signal that is clear
in the relative pattern across agents. This supports evaluating several probe and aggregation choices
rather than relying on one score. AUROC and fixed-threshold recall should be reported separately.
In several blackjack cells, AUROC remains high while recall falls after feedback. This means that
collusion and control runs are still ordered correctly, but the frozen threshold no longer catches as
many collusion runs. In a deployed system, this is a calibration problem even if the underlying
representation remains separable.

7   Limitations
These experiments have clear limits. Blackjack uses 25 shoes per cell and Simmons uses 20 collusion
episodes per cell. The agents adapt through prompts and interaction history; model weights are not
updated against the monitor. The current Simmons export does not contain round-level posterior
traces, so it cannot support claims about information-acquisition speed. Finally, the results contain
five probe architectures. Future work, requires testing on more number of model sizes and model
families.
